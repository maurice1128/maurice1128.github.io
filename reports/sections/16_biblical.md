## 16. Biblical-theological reflection

Read as a whole canon, Scripture supports the model's central distinction: suffering can deepen trust in God while a particular religious institution stands judged for failing the sufferers. It also insists that the remedy for a failing church is repentance and reform, not simply exit. Unlike the descriptive sections, this one is normative and theological. It asks what the biblical texts affirm and warn against, not what historically happened, and the journal articles will keep the two registers separate.

Quotations follow the New Revised Standard Version (NRSV, 1989) and are kept short; longer passages are paraphrased. Transliterations follow a simplified academic style, and every lexical gloss should be checked against BDB/HALOT or BDAG (see "To verify"). Section 3 covers the history of theologies of suffering; this section is exegetical and maps each element of the model to its key texts.

### 16.1 Suffering raises the demand for meaning: lament

The Bible's first answer to collective suffering is not explanation but address: the sufferer speaks to God. The lament psalms ask "How long, O LORD?" (Ps 13:1) and "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22:1). Psalm 88 ends without any turn to praise; its last line leaves the speaker in darkness (88:18). Lamentations, whose Hebrew title is its opening cry *ʾêkâ* ("How!"), is written largely in the limping *qînâ* (funeral dirge) rhythm. It asks passers-by, "Is it nothing to you?" (Lam 1:12). Job protests for thirty-five chapters, and God says at the end that Job, not his friends, has "spoken of me what is right" (Job 42:7).

Westermann established lament as one of the two basic forms of psalmic prayer, alongside praise (Westermann 1981), and argued for its theological weight in the Old Testament as a whole (Westermann 1974). He distinguished the dirge over the dead (*qînâ*) from the plea addressed to God; only the latter expects an answer. Brueggemann's "The Costly Loss of Lament" gave the form a social function. Lament keeps the covenant two-sided and legitimates the "pain-bearer," and it makes justice a proper question to put before God (Brueggemann 1986). A church that loses it drifts toward a one-sided worship that serves established power.

O'Connor stresses that God never speaks in Lamentations; the divine silence leaves room for survivors' voices (O'Connor 2002). Newsom reads Job as a contest of moral imaginations that the book refuses to resolve (Newsom 2003). Billman and Migliore argue for recovering lament in Christian worship and pastoral care (Billman and Migliore 1999). Kim reads the communal laments as a residue of trauma passed across generations (Kim 2021).

For the model, lament confirms that suffering raises demand for meaning and community. It also shows that the Bible authorises complaint *to* God *within* the worshipping assembly. Lament keeps faith and protest together, which helps explain why faith can rise while trust in an institution that silences complaint falls.

### 16.2 Interpreting suffering: retribution, its critique, and God with the sufferer

Scripture contains a retributive reading of collective disaster and a sustained critique of it. Deuteronomy 28 sets blessings and curses before the people, and the Deuteronomistic history explains the fall of Samaria and Jerusalem as the outcome of covenant breach (2 Kgs 17; 24–25). Proverbs generalises the pattern for individuals. The Hebrew *rāʿâ* covers both moral evil and calamity, and Amos can ask, "Does disaster befall a city, unless the LORD has done it?" (Amos 3:6).

The critique is equally canonical. Job's friends defend retribution and are rebuked (Job 42:7). Ecclesiastes observes that the same fate meets the righteous and the wicked (Eccl 9:2). Jesus rejects the inference from suffering to sin twice. Of the man born blind he says, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned" (John 9:3). Of the Galileans killed by Pilate and the eighteen crushed at Siloam he asks whether they were worse sinners, and answers no (Luke 13:1–5). The same passage still calls the hearers to repent, so the critique limits retribution without abolishing moral accountability. Crenshaw classes punishment for sin as one canonical answer among several, none fully adequate (Crenshaw 2005).

The third strand places God with the sufferer. In Exodus 3:7 God says, "I have observed the misery of my people ... I have heard their cry ... Indeed, I know their sufferings." The Hebrew uses three verbs, *rāʾâ* (see, intensified with an infinitive absolute), *šāmaʿ* (hear) and *yādaʿ* (know). They pick up the four verbs of 2:24–25 (heard, remembered, saw, knew). The object of knowing is *makʾōb*, pain. The same noun recurs in Isaiah 53:3–4, where the servant is "a man of suffering" (*ʾîš makʾōbôt*) who carries "our diseases." Verse 8 then adds "I have come down to deliver them": knowledge leads to intervention. Fretheim's study of divine suffering treats such texts as showing God suffering because of, with and for Israel (Fretheim 1984).

The identity of the *ʿebed YHWH* ("servant of the LORD") in Isaiah 52:13–53:12 is disputed. Isaiah 49:3 names the servant as Israel, supporting a collective reading; individual readings point to the prophet or another figure. The New Testament reads the passage of Jesus (Acts 8:32–35), and the Ethiopian's question, "about whom ... does the prophet say this?" (8:34), already names the problem. Janowski and Stuhlmacher (2004) trace the Jewish and Christian lines of interpretation, and Moyaert (2016) shows how Jewish criticism has pressed christological readings. On every reading, a despised sufferer turns out to carry God's purpose.

The New Testament extends the strand. Christ is a high priest able "to sympathize with our weaknesses" (*sympathēsai*, Heb 4:15). In Matthew 25:31–46 the king identifies with "the least of these" (*elachistoi*). Whether they are all the needy or needy disciples (cf. Matt 10:42; 12:49–50) is a long-standing debate (Gray 1989). In 2 Corinthians 1:3–7 Paul links "affliction" (*thlipsis*) and "comfort" (*paraklēsis*) and calls the Corinthians partners (*koinōnoi*) in suffering. Colossians 1:24 speaks of "completing what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the church." Most interpreters deny that it implies a deficit in Christ's atonement; how to read it otherwise is contested.

For the model, the canon holds both interpretive frames that section 3 calls retributive and solidarity theologies. The solidarity texts tie God's credibility to God's presence with the afflicted, which no outcome can falsify. That is the exegetical root of the model's second variable.

### 16.3 The institution that fails the suffering: prophetic critique

The prophets judge religious leaders above all by how they treat the weak. Ezekiel 34 indicts the "shepherds of Israel" (*rōʿê yiśrāʾēl*) who feed themselves. Its charge sheet is specific: "You have not strengthened the weak, you have not healed the sick, you have not bound up the injured, you have not brought back the strayed, you have not sought the lost" (34:4). God's answer is to remove them and take over: "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (34:15). God will then do each neglected task (34:16). In 34:16 the Hebrew reads "I will destroy" the fat and strong, while the Greek and Syriac versions read "watch over"; the variant should be checked.

Mein offers a caution. Read against ancient animal husbandry, the shepherds are hired hands who misused the owner's property. The chapter may therefore stress divine ownership and power more than compassion (Mein 2007). The textual point stands either way: leaders are accountable to God for the flock. In Matthew 9:36 Jesus has compassion (*splanchnizomai*) on crowds "like sheep without a shepherd," taking up the same image.

Jeremiah's temple sermon attacks confidence in the institution itself: "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD'" (Jer 7:4). The true condition is justice for the alien, the orphan and the widow (7:5–7), and the sermon asks whether the house has become "a den of robbers" (7:11). Jeremiah 23:1 pronounces woe on shepherds who scatter the sheep. Amos 5:21–24 rejects festivals and offerings in favour of justice (*mišpāṭ*) and righteousness (*ṣĕdāqâ*). Micah 3:11 condemns priests who "teach for a price" while claiming "Surely the LORD is with us!" Malachi charges priests with causing "many to stumble" by their instruction and showing partiality (Mal 2:8–9).

Jesus stands in this line. In the temple he cites Isaiah 56:7 ("a house of prayer for all the nations") and Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of robbers") together (Mark 11:15–17). Matthew 23 condemns leaders who neglect "justice and mercy and faith" (23:23). In the parable of the Samaritan, the priest and the Levite pass "by on the other side." The outsider is "moved with pity" (*esplanchnisthē*) and acts (Luke 10:31–33). The religious professionals fail, and care comes from beyond the institution.

For the model, these texts supply a biblical warrant for the "failing establishment" outcome. They judge the institution by a concrete test of care. They also show God displacing faithless leaders rather than abandoning the flock.

### 16.4 Religion allied with power

Several texts depict religious officials whose loyalty to the crown outweighs their loyalty to the word of God. At Bethel the priest Amaziah reports Amos to King Jeroboam and orders him away, because Bethel "is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom" (Amos 7:13). The Hebrew is *miqdaš-melek* and *bêt mamlākâ*, "royal sanctuary" and "house of the kingdom." Amos answers that he is "no prophet, nor a prophet's son" (7:14), meaning he holds no professional position. Wazana argues that the dispute concerns belonging and exclusion, who may speak "here," as much as prophetic authority (Wazana 2020). In 1 Kings 22 four hundred court prophets promise victory. Micaiah, whom the king hates because he prophesies only "disaster" (*rāʿ*), insists, "whatever the LORD says to me, that I will speak" (22:8, 14).

The New Testament warns about the church's own favouritism toward the powerful. James 2:1–7 rebukes an assembly that seats the rich man with the gold ring and sends the poor to the floor. It reminds readers that "the rich ... oppress you" (2:6). James 5:1–6 denounces withheld wages that "cry out" (5:4). Revelation 18 laments the fall of "Babylon," whose merchants trade in luxuries and finally in "slaves—and human lives" (18:13). It calls, "Come out of her, my people" (18:4). Bauckham reads Revelation as theocentric prophecy, not a coded forecast, with a sharp critique of Roman power and its economy (Bauckham 1993a; 1993b).

Interpretive caution is needed. Revelation 18 targets an imperial city, not a church, so applying it to churches is analogy, not exegesis. Amos and Micaiah confront state cults in ancient monarchies, not voluntary churches. The texts warrant a question, whether a church has become a "king's sanctuary," not a verdict on any church.

For the model, these texts give biblical language for the third church role, a church allied with perpetrators. They also show the faithful response as prophetic speech and, in Revelation, separation from complicity, not abandonment of God.

### 16.5 Faith migrating to the sufferers' own community

Acts portrays the earliest church as a community in which the needy were met by their own fellowship. The believers devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to *koinōnia* (fellowship, sharing), and "had all things in common" (Acts 2:42–44). Acts 4:34 reports "not a needy person among them." Its Greek *endeēs* (needy) appears to echo the Septuagint of Deuteronomy 15:4, "there will ... be no one in need among you." Luke presents the church as fulfilling the sabbatical-year ideal.

Acts 6:1–7 is the most important text for the model, because it shows a church admitting failure. The Hellenists complained (*goggysmos*) "because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution" (*diakonia*). The apostles did not deny the complaint. They called the community together, which chose seven men, all bearing Greek names, to oversee the distribution. In effect the aggrieved group received a share of control. Luke then reports that "the number of the disciples increased greatly" (6:7). The names suggest, but do not prove, that the seven came from the neglected group.

Paul makes shared suffering a mark of the body: "If one member suffers, all suffer together with it" (1 Cor 12:26). The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan cited this verse in its 2009 letter after Typhoon Morakot (see section on the contemporary church). Galatians 6:2 commands, "Bear one another's burdens." James 1:27 defines pure religion as caring "for orphans and widows in their distress" (*thlipsis*). 1 Peter 4:12–19 addresses a community under "fiery ordeal" (*pyrōsis*) and calls its members to "share Christ's sufferings" (4:13). It distinguishes suffering for the faith from deserved punishment (4:15).

For the model, Acts 6 is the canonical counter-case to migration. Faith did not need to leave the church because the church restructured, putting the neglected group's members in charge of the ministry that had failed them. The model should therefore treat institutional self-correction as a branch that can prevent migration.

### 16.6 Transmission needs community

The Bible assumes that faith passes between generations through household and assembly, not through private belief alone. Deuteronomy 6:4–9 joins the *Šĕmaʿ* to the command to "recite them to your children" and to mark doors and gates. Deuteronomy 6:20–25 anticipates the child's question and answers with the Exodus story. Psalm 78:1–8 commits each generation to "tell to the coming generation" so that the next will not repeat its ancestors' unfaithfulness. 2 Timothy 1:5 traces Timothy's faith through his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice. Hebrews 10:24–25 warns against "neglecting to meet together" (*episynagōgē*) and ties perseverance to mutual encouragement.

These texts converge with Voas and Crockett's finding that in Britain belief declined at the same rate as belonging across cohorts, rather than surviving it (Voas and Crockett 2005). Believing without belonging is better seen as a lagged decay. Scripture, read normatively, predicts the same result. Faith that loses its community of transmission is unlikely to survive more than a generation.

For the model, this strengthens the expectation that faith which leaves a failing institution must find a new community, or it will fade in the next generation. Migration to a sufferer-controlled body is thus not optional for the persistence of faith.

### 16.7 Institutional repentance

The Hebrew Bible provides a liturgy for corporate confession of collective and inherited sin. Leviticus 26:40 envisions exiles who "confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their ancestors." The verb is *hitwaddâ*, the reflexive stem of *ydh*. Ezra 9, Nehemiah 9 and Daniel 9:4–19 are extended penitential prayers in which leaders speak in the first person plural. Daniel prays, "we have sinned and done wrong" (Dan 9:5), and Nehemiah's Levites declare God "just in all that has come upon us" (Neh 9:33). Boda traces how Nehemiah 9 reworks earlier tradition (Boda 1999). Werline treats penitential prayer as a developing religious institution in Second Temple Judaism (Werline 1998).

1 Peter 4:17 adds a New Testament principle: "the time has come for judgment to begin with the household of God" (*oikos tou theou*). The phrase echoes Ezekiel 9:6, where judgment begins "at my sanctuary."

Modern churches have adopted this form. In October 1945 the leaders of the German Evangelical Church declared in Stuttgart that "through us endless sufferings" had been brought to many peoples (Conway 1987). In 1990 the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan apologised to 228 victims and their families. It confessed that, out of fear under martial law, "整體教會並未給予受難者家屬應有的聲援" ("the church as a whole did not give the victims' families the support they were due") (PCT 1990). Churches in Canada and Australia apologised for their part in removing Indigenous children (TRC 2015; HREOC 1998). John Paul II asked forgiveness of the Indigenous peoples of Latin America and of Africans deported as slaves (ITC 2000).

The International Theological Commission's *Memory and Reconciliation* draws a distinction that matters here. In the biblical confessions of "the sins of the fathers," it notes, confession is addressed solely to God, for sins against God rather than against other people (ITC 2000, §2.1). Modern apologies such as the PCT's are addressed to human victims, which goes beyond the biblical pattern. The same document distinguishes the Church's holiness from the sins of her members, a distinction Protestants draw differently. Whether institutions as such can repent was debated in the 1970s (Elliott 1976); Prothro (2023) offers a recent canonical survey.

For the model, corporate confession is the biblical form of institutional repentance. It predicts that public, specific confession, followed by changed practice as in Acts 6, can begin to restore trust. The PCT's 1990 apology is a test case worth tracing in the descriptive articles.

### 16.8 Counterweights, and how traditions read them

A one-sided reading would make Scripture simply anti-institutional. Several texts pull the other way. Ezekiel 18 rejects the proverb about sour grapes and insists, "it is only the person who sins that shall die" (18:4). Each generation must answer for itself (Joyce 1989), and sufferers are not exempt from their own repentance. Psalm 73, Habakkuk 3:17–19 and Job 42 model faith that holds without explanation. The psalmist's crisis resolves only when he goes "into the sanctuary of God" (Ps 73:17). That is a counterweight to the claim that the worshipping institution is the problem. The meaning of Job 42:6 is itself disputed: "I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes" is one rendering among several.

Scripture also commands order. Hebrews 13:17 says, "Obey your leaders and submit to them," while adding that leaders "will give an account," which links back to Ezekiel 34. Romans 13:1–7 calls for subjection to governing authorities and calls the ruler God's servant (*diakonos*) "for your good" (13:4). Acts 5:29 sets the limit: "We must obey God rather than any human authority." Hebrews 12:5–11 quotes Proverbs 3:11–12 and calls hardship divine discipline (*paideia*; Hebrew *mûsār*). It addresses persecution endured for the faith, however, not abuse by church leaders, and using it to silence victims of the church misreads it.

Traditions weigh these texts differently, and each reading has strengths.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Evangelical | Personal repentance, divine sovereignty, faithful endurance (Carson 2006) | Ezek 18, Heb 12, Rom 13 read as normative for believers | Turning sin into only structural sin | Under-reading Ezek 34 and Jas 5 as indictments of religious leaders |

The Orthodox row is a general characterisation, not drawn from a cited source, and should be checked with an Orthodox biblical scholar. On Romans 13 the range runs from Yoder's reading of subordination as non-violent, non-conforming witness (Yoder 1994) to Reformed traditions of resistance led by lesser magistrates.

For the model, the counterweights show that the biblical frame is not "sufferers good, institutions bad." It asks for accountable authority, personal repentance from everyone, and faith that sometimes waits without answers.

### 16.10 Questions for the church

1. Ezekiel 34's test: does the church strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the injured, bring back the strayed and seek the lost? Which of the five does it neglect?
2. Does the church's worship make room for lament (Pss 13, 88; Lamentations), or only for praise and penitence?
3. When suffering strikes, does the church's preaching move from suffering to the sufferer's sin in ways that John 9 and Luke 13 forbid?
4. Does any part of the church function as a "king's sanctuary" (Amos 7:13), whose message is limited by loyalty to the powerful?
5. When a neglected group complains, does the church respond as in Acts 6, by acknowledging the failure and sharing control of the ministry concerned?
6. Has the church confessed specific past failures toward sufferers, publicly and to the people harmed, and changed its practice afterward?

### Implications for the model

- **Supports the core distinction.** Scripture separates trust in God from trust in religious leaders, and judges leaders by care for the weak (Ezek 34; Jer 7; Luke 10). Faith up with institutional trust down is a biblically intelligible outcome, not only a sociological one.
- **Adds a self-correction branch.** Acts 6 and the penitential prayers show an institution that admits failure, confesses and shares control with the aggrieved. The model should test whether such repentance prevents or reverses migration, using cases such as the PCT apology of 1990.
- **Locates theological interpretation in the canon itself.** Retribution, its critique and divine co-suffering are all canonical. Which voice prevails is a matter of selection by communities and authorities, as section 3 argues.
- **Strengthens the transmission argument.** Deuteronomy 6 and Psalm 78, read with Voas and Crockett, imply that faith leaving an institution must re-embed in a community or decay across generations.
- **Warns against one-sided use.** Ezekiel 18, Hebrews 13 and Psalm 73 resist a reading in which sufferers are only victims and institutions only villains. The articles should keep this normative balance out of the descriptive analysis.
