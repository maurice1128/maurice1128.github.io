## 6. Comparative cases: Western and twentieth-century catastrophes

Across seventeen Western and twentieth-century catastrophes, the identity of the perpetrator and the church's relation to it predict the religious outcome better than the scale or kind of suffering. Where the perpetrator stood outside the sufferers' faith, as with Armenians, Koreans under Japan and Irish Catholics under a Protestant state, faith and the sufferers' own church rose together. Where the church was allied with the perpetrators, devotion migrated or Christianity was rejected. From the eighteenth century on, a further path appears: secular states and medicine took over the interpretation of disaster, displacing the church's role without destroying faith.

This section treats the cases section 7 leaves aside and does not repeat its African, Asian and Indigenous material. Jewish and Muslim responses to 1348, Korean minjung theology and Holocaust-era Hebrew texts are treated in sections 4, 10 and 13; here they are coded, not re-surveyed. Codings use the working model's variables: sufferers' power position, dominant theology, church role, devotion outcome and institutional-trust outcome.

### Holding the disaster constant: 1348 seen from its minorities

**Jewish communities, 1348–51.** The same epidemic struck Christians and Jews, but Jews also faced massacre by Christian neighbours, often with elite and episcopal involvement (section 4.5; Cohn 2007; Finley and Koyama 2018). Their response consolidated faith inside their own institutions. Memorial books such as the Nuremberg *Memorbuch* listed destroyed communities for synagogue recitation and framed the dead as martyrs for the sanctification of the Name, *qiddush ha-Shem* (Salfeld 1898; sections 10 and 13). Yuval (2006) reconstructs the mutual images through which each community read the other's suffering. Einbinder (2018) finds continuity of worldview among Iberian and Provençal Jews, not a crisis of belief. The case is the model's first branch in pure form, with Christianity in the role of perpetrator's religion. It tests position, not the Christian outcome (section 14).

**Muslim societies.** Muslim jurists read plague as martyrdom for believers and mercy, not as collective punishment (Dols 1977; sections 10 and 13). Ibn al-Wardī, who died of plague in 1349, wrote in that vein. Dols (1977) stressed the absence of flagellants and of attacks on minorities. Stearns (2011) qualifies the contrast: Muslim and Christian scholars in the western Mediterranean debated contagion in parallel ways. Islam had no sacramental monopoly to fail, so the institutional contest ran between jurists and physicians, not laity and priests. This supports the claim that Latin anticlericalism after 1348 depended on Latin ecclesiology. It also shows that a mercy theodicy did not by itself prevent institutional conflict over plague.

### Establishment churches under mass mortality

**The Justinianic plague (541–c. 750).** The scale of this pandemic is now the most contested question in late antique history. Harper (2017) and the contributors to Little (2007) defend a catastrophic reading. Mordechai and Eisenberg (2019), with Mordechai et al. (2019), argue that its effects were limited, and Newfield (2022) says the Black Death analogy inflated estimates. Meier (2003; 2016) argues that the plague's strongest effects were cultural and religious, driving a reorientation that helped turn the eastern Roman into the Byzantine empire; he also judges its short-term demographic consequences catastrophic (Meier 2016). De Wet (2022) reads John of Ephesus's plague narrative as a pro-imperial discourse of power. The church here was a fused imperial establishment, and no anticlerical or lay-penitential movement is reported. For the model, the case suggests that a church fused with a functioning state can absorb mass death without visible loss of trust.

**The Thirty Years' War (1618–48).** Wilson (2009) argues that religion was one driver among several, not the war's single cause. Lehmann (1980; Lehmann and Trepp 1999) frames the seventeenth century as an age of crisis piety, in which war, famine and climatic cooling fed penitential preaching and eschatology. Stauff (2025) shows Lutheran music serving confession, politics and devotion at once. Sufferers were subjects of confessional states whose churches were allied with armies on both sides. Devotion rose within those churches; there is no sign of mass exit. The link sometimes drawn to later Pietist conventicles, lay-led bodies inside the church, would be redistribution rather than rejection, but the causal chain is not established here.

**Lisbon, 1755.** The earthquake, tsunami and fire destroyed a capital of a Catholic monarchy on All Saints' Day (Kendrick 1956; Molesky 2015). Neiman (2002) treats Lisbon as the moment natural and moral evil began to separate in European thought (section 3). Braun and Radner (2005) collect reactions across Europe. Inside Portugal the contest was institutional. Pombal's state directed reconstruction. The Jesuit Gabriel Malagrida, whose 1756 pamphlet blamed the disaster on sin, was exiled from court, implicated by Pombal in the 1758 Távora plot and executed by the Inquisition for heresy on 21 September 1761, not for the sermon itself (Molesky 2015). The state, not the church, won control over the disaster's meaning. Devotion was not destroyed, but the church lost its monopoly on interpretation.

**Nineteenth-century cholera.** Rosenberg (1962) traces American responses from 1832, when cholera was widely read as divine judgment on sin, to 1866, when it was a sanitary problem for boards of health. Morris (1975) finds a similar shift in Oxford's cholera pamphlets of 1832–54. Evans (1987) shows Hamburg's 1892 epidemic as a political failure of a merchant oligarchy that refused to filter water; blame fell on the state, not the church. Cholera is the clearest case of functional displacement. Medicine and the state absorbed the demand for explanation and protection, while churches remained centres of consolation.

### Subordinate peoples with their own church

**The Great Irish Famine (1845–52).** Larkin (1972) argued that a "devotional revolution" between 1850 and 1875 made Irish Catholics a uniformly practising people. He linked it to the Famine's shock, the loss of Gaelic culture and Catholicism's role as a substitute marker of national identity. Miller (1975), using the 1834 Commissioners of Public Instruction returns, showed that pre-Famine attendance was high in the anglophone east and low in the Irish-speaking west. The Famine killed or removed the poor of the west disproportionately, so part of the "revolution" may be a composition effect. Larkin himself, in a posthumously published article (Larkin 2014), traced the beginnings to parish missions of 1825–46, and Kerr (1996) studies the church's conduct during the crisis. Barr (2020) extends the revolution to the Irish diaspora. Irish Catholics were subordinate to a Protestant state and landed elite, while their church was their own. Faith and the institution rose together, as the model predicts.

**The Armenian genocide (1915–23).** The perpetrator was the Ottoman state, not a Christian institution, and the Armenian Apostolic Church lost clergy alongside its people (Hovannisian 1992; Suny 2015). In Soviet Armenia the church was then persecuted and controlled (Corley 1996a; 1996b). In the diaspora it became the main carrier of genocide memory and national identity. Barović (2026), using surveys in Armenia and the diaspora, finds that diaspora religiosity is tied to the church's communal activities, and that some respondents doubt an Armenian identity would exist without it. The church canonized the victims, about 1.5 million, as martyrs at Holy Etchmiadzin on 23 April 2015 (AGBU 2015). The case fits pattern A, though the evidence is about identity and belonging, not personal belief.

**Korea: colonial rule, war and democratization.** Section 10 covers the Korean literature in depth. In comparative terms, Korea differs from China and Japan because the colonizer was not Christian. Christianity became linked to nationalism, and Christians made up 16 of the 33 signatories of the 1919 independence declaration (Park 2003). The shrine-worship conflict split the churches: compliant establishments against resisters who formed their own bodies after 1945 (Kim Sung-Gun 1989; section 10). The Korean War produced growth but reshaped faith toward anticommunism and blessing (Lee 2010; Kim and Kim 2014). Minjung theology then gave the suffering people a theological voice under military rule. Within two generations the sufferers' church became an establishment, and section 17 records the later fall in trust.

**Maoist China and post-1978 growth.** Christianity entered the Maoist era tainted as a foreign, imperialist religion (Bays 2012). The state forced Protestants into the Three-Self Patriotic Movement and closed all churches during the Cultural Revolution. After 1978, Christianity grew fast, especially in unregistered house churches. Yang (2012) argues that repression does not reduce religion but pushes it into "black" and "gray" markets outside state control. Lian Xi (2010) shows that much growth was popular, sectarian and apocalyptic, shaped by suffering and indigenous leadership. The case fits pattern B in a new form: the state-registered church was read by many as compliant, and devotion moved to sufferer-controlled bodies. Like Taiwan's Indigenous peoples (section 7), mass growth came after constraint eased.

### War, defeat and the national churches

**The US Civil War.** Noll (2006) argues that the war was a theological crisis: a shared Bible and common-sense hermeneutic could not settle slavery, and providential readings fractured along sectional lines. Goen (1985) shows the great denominations had already split over slavery. Faust (2008) traces how mass death strained the "Good Death" ideal and raised doubt, and Rable (2010) and Stout (2006) show both sides claiming divine favour. After defeat, white Southern churches did not lose authority. Wilson (1980) shows them sacralizing defeat in a civil religion of the Lost Cause. The defeated were a former ruling class whose churches were their own, and faith and institution rose together. Freedpeople's choices are covered in section 5.

**World War I.** Jenkins (2014) reads the war as a holy war whose apocalyptic rhetoric reshaped world religion. Becker (1994; trans. 1998) documents a French Catholic revival of mourning, pilgrimage and the cult of the dead. Wilkinson (1978) shows the Church of England struggling to speak to soldiers, while Snape (2005; 2009) finds a resilient "diffusive" Christianity in the ranks. Winter (1995) argues that traditional, largely religious languages of mourning persisted and that spiritualism also rose. Brown (2009) places the decisive British decline in the 1960s, not after 1918. The sufferers belonged to the churches that blessed the war. Devotion was redirected, not lost, and institutional decline was slow and later.

### Churches allied with the perpetrators

**Native American prophetic movements.** Wallace (1956) defined revitalization movements from cases like these. Dowd (1992) traces a pan-Indian nativist movement, 1745–1815, in which prophets such as Neolin and Tenskwatawa called for rejecting colonial goods and ways. Martin (1991) reads the Muskogee Redstick war of 1813–14 as a "sacred revolt." Handsome Lake's Seneca religion, from 1799, combined Iroquois ritual with Quaker-influenced ethics (Wallace 1970). The Ghost Dance of 1889–90 promised restoration of the dead and the land; its suppression ended at Wounded Knee (Mooney 1896; DeMallie 1982; Warren 2017). Kracht (2018) shows Kiowas combining Ghost Dance, peyote and Christianity. These movements are the North American parallel to the Andean Taki Onqoy (section 7). Christianity was the perpetrators' religion and living alternatives existed, so outcomes ranged from rejection to selective re-coding.

**The Holocaust.** Jewish responses are polarized. Rubenstein (1966) rejected the God of history; Fackenheim (1970) and Berkovits (1973) called for renewed fidelity (section 3). Brenner (1980) surveyed survivors and found both lost and strengthened faith. Carmil and Breznitz (1991) found greater belief in God among survivors and their children than among controls. On the perpetrators' side, Ericksen and Heschel (1999) and Bergen (1996) document German church complicity. For the model, the sufferers' own institutions remained their community, and loss of faith appears as a strong minority branch. The perpetrators' churches paid the institutional cost in a long postwar reckoning.

**Apartheid South Africa.** The Dutch Reformed Church (NGK) gave apartheid theological support (de Gruchy 1979; Elphick and Davenport 1997). Elphick (2012) traces segregationist thinking to missionary debates over the "equality of believers." Racially separate "daughter" churches of the same confession were dominated by the NGK. In 1982 the Dutch Reformed Mission Church drafted the Belhar Confession, rejecting apartheid theology as contrary to the gospel, and adopted it in 1986 (Cloete and Smit 1984). The Kairos Document (Kairos Theologians 1985) attacked both "state theology" and the neutral "church theology" of English-speaking churches. Black South Africans did not abandon Christianity. Trust moved to sufferer-controlled bodies: the mission churches' own confession, the prophetic ecumenical movement and the independent churches (section 7). Exit was rare; redistribution was the norm.

### Modern shocks with secular competitors

**The 1918 influenza and COVID-19.** The 1918 pandemic killed tens of millions in months (Phillips and Killingray 2003). In the United States, churches closed under public-health orders, and Bristow (2012) finds that Americans quickly folded the epidemic into a culture of optimism and forgetting. In South Africa, Phillips (2008) documents a wide range of religious explanations, including divine punishment. Where mission churches would not practise prayer-healing, the epidemic fed independent churches such as Aladura (section 7). COVID-19 raised prayer searches worldwide (Bentzen 2021), but in mid-2020 about two-thirds of Americans (68 percent) said their own faith had not changed much (Pew Research Center 2021c; section 17). In high-security societies, pandemics now raise short-term demand without durable institutional effects.

**Clergy abuse crises.** Section 17 reviews the evidence (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; CIASE 2021a). Abuse is the purest case of the church as perpetrator against its own members. Belief largely persists while affiliation, attendance and giving fall. Ireland links this case to the Famine: the church that was the sufferers' own in 1850 became the harming institution by the 1990s (Ganiel 2016). Church role must therefore be time-indexed.

### Patterns across cases

Four patterns emerge, and they refine rather than overturn the working model.

| Pattern | Cases | Mechanism | Implication for the model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perpetrator outside the faith | Irish Famine, Armenians, Korea, Jews in 1348 and the Holocaust | The church becomes the nation's or community's memory | Faith and institution rise together; adds "perpetrator's religion" as a variable |
| Establishment absorbs the shock | Justinian, Thirty Years' War, US South, World War I | A church fused with a functioning state frames suffering as chastisement or sacrifice | Trust holds unless the church visibly fails; supports the Black Death "redistribution, not exit" reading |
| Church allied with perpetrators | Native Americans, apartheid, Holocaust (for Christian churches), abuse | Devotion moves to sufferer-controlled bodies or alternatives | Confirms patterns B and C; exit is rare where no alternative exists |
| Secular competitor | Lisbon, cholera, 1918, COVID | State and medicine take over explanation and protection | A fourth outcome: functional displacement without loss of faith |

The first pattern is the most important addition. Section 7 found that rejection occurs when Christianity is the perpetrators' religion. The converse holds too: when the perpetrator is of another faith or of none, suffering binds people to their own church. Korea and China show this sharply. The same Japanese and communist pressures produced growth in both, but Christianity's earlier association with nationalism or imperialism shaped which churches gained.

The fourth pattern challenges the model's first step. From Lisbon onward, demand for meaning and protection did not flow automatically to churches. States and physicians competed for it. Rosenberg's cholera and section 17's COVID evidence show that, in high-security societies, the church may keep consolation while losing explanation.

Scholars disagree on several codings. The Justinianic plague's scale is unresolved (Harper 2017 against Mordechai and Eisenberg 2019). Larkin's devotional revolution may be partly a demographic artefact (Miller 1975). Whether crisis piety in the Thirty Years' War fed later Pietism is asserted more than shown. And for most cases, outcome evidence measures belonging, not belief.

### Recommended cases for the core design

Section 14 recommends a core pair (Latin plague, 1348–1400; enslaved and freed African Americans, 1760–1900) with Brazil and the Jews of 1348–51 as variable-separating shadow cases. Two cases from this section best complement that design; neither should replace a core case.

1. **The Great Irish Famine, 1845–75, as a most-similar partner for the plague case.** It holds constant the Latin Church, its sacramental system and a mass-mortality shock, while changing the sufferers' position and the church's role. In 1348 the church was the establishment of a society in which the sufferers were the majority. In Ireland the sufferers were subordinate and the church was theirs. The model predicts faith and institution up in Ireland, against redistribution after 1348, and the devotional revolution fits. Miller's composition effect and Larkin's own pre-Famine dating are the tests the comparison must survive. The later abuse crisis adds a within-case reversal of church role.
2. **The South African Reformed family, 1948–1994, as a complement to the slavery case, alongside Brazil.** One confession, one polity and one era contained a church allied with the state and subordinate churches of the same tradition. That separates church role from power position, as Brazil does, with a modern documentary record. The Belhar Confession and the Kairos Document are explicit statements linking establishment complicity to a choice of alternative theology. They supply the "smoking-gun" evidence section 14 asks process tracing to find. Brazil should stay first for the slavery side because it matches the era; South Africa is the stronger second.

The Armenian or Korean case could serve as a short limit case for the "perpetrator outside the faith" pattern.

### Implications for the model

- Add the perpetrator's religion as a variable. Suffering inflicted by outsiders of another faith binds sufferers to their own church; suffering inflicted by co-religionists pushes devotion to alternatives.
- Add functional displacement as a fourth outcome. From Lisbon onward, states and medicine compete for the demand for meaning and protection, weakening the church's interpretive role without destroying faith.
- An establishment church survives mass death when it is fused with a functioning state and does not visibly fail (Justinian, Thirty Years' War). This supports coding the Black Death as redistribution, not exit.
- Church role must be time-indexed. Korea and Ireland show sufferers' churches becoming establishments and, in Ireland, perpetrators.
- Mass growth after the lifting of constraint recurs (post-1978 China), reinforcing the timing hypothesis drawn from Fountain (2010) and Taiwan (section 7).
- Most outcome evidence measures identity and belonging. The Holocaust survivor studies are rare attempts to measure belief, and the series should say so.
