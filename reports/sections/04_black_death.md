## 4. Case 1: The Black Death and its religious aftermath (1347–c.1500)

The Black Death raised demand for intercession and meaning far more clearly than it lowered trust in the church. Most of the intensified devotion ran through ecclesiastical channels, and trust frayed mainly where the institution visibly failed to deliver pastoral care or moved to suppress lay initiative. This section covers mainly English-language scholarship on Latin Christendom. German, French and Italian work is cited briefly here and treated fully in section 8.

### 4.1 The shock and the state of the field

The first wave (1347–52) killed perhaps a third to a half of Europe's population. Aberth (2021) argues that the familiar "one-third" estimate is too low. Diocesan evidence supports heavy local losses: Aberth (1995), re-evaluating the Ely bishop's register, finds that East Anglia, and Cambridgeshire in particular, was among the hardest hit in England in 1348–49. Plague then returned repeatedly, in 1361–63 and at intervals for the rest of the period. Section 8 discusses the continental chronologies of these waves.

Recurrence matters for the model: several of the best studies find that religious change followed the second or third wave, not the first.

The standard English source collections are Horrox (1994) and Aberth (2005; 2nd ed. 2017). Stearns (2009) argued that the long debate over whether the disease was bubonic plague had crowded out the study of religious responses. He also criticised older work that treated Christian, Muslim and Jewish responses as fixed and essentially different. Dols's (1974a) comparison of Muslim and Christian communal responses remains the classic starting point for that comparative frame.

### 4.2 Contemporary theological interpretations

Contemporaries combined three explanations that did not exclude each other: divine punishment for collective sin, celestial or miasmatic causes, and human agency through poisoning. Divine punishment was the dominant framing. The Paris medical faculty's 1348 consilium blamed a planetary conjunction of 1345 (Horrox 1994). L. Jones (2022) shows that in later plague tracts "what, where, and especially who was blamed" shifted with political, religious and publishing contexts.

Eschatological readings were common but contained. Lerner (1981) shows that many Western observers read the plague as a sign within existing prophetic frameworks, and he plays down millenarianism among the flagellants. Smoller (2000) traces how chroniclers combined plague, earthquakes and other portents in "investigating" the apocalypse. Apocalyptic interpretation thus fitted into existing learned and clerical genres rather than breaking them.

The punishment frame prescribed a remedy, and the church controlled that remedy. Bishops ordered processions, litanies, masses and moral reform. Wingard (2026) argues that the English hierarchy, from 1348 to 1400, treated moral reform and liturgical activity as the foundations of plague management. It made the containment of lust, and especially sodomy, central to that programme. Archambeau (2021) reaches a parallel result from below. In sixty-eight testimonies from the 1363 canonisation inquest of Delphine de Puimichel at Apt, Provençal witnesses who had lived through 1348 and 1361 understood plague and war as symptoms of spiritual sickness caused by sin. They sought cures in confession and in saints.

Alternative theodicies existed but were marginal. Julian of Norwich, a child in 1348, later wrote an optimistic theology of divine love ("all shall be well"). Her *Showings* never mention the plague directly *[verify]*. Her theology is the closest late-medieval analogue to the model's "God suffering with the afflicted" pole. But it circulated narrowly and did not shape the mass response.

**Relation to the model.** This case complicates the model's second variable. A punishment theology did not push people away from the institution, because the institution owned the penitential remedy. Collective-sin theology produced *more* demand for clerical mediation. It became corrosive only when the clergy could not, or would not, supply that mediation.

### 4.3 Devotional intensification

Scholarship agrees that devotion became more intense and more visible after 1348. It disagrees about when the change happened, what form it took, and whether it bypassed the clergy. The best empirical studies show that the change was delayed, uneven and mostly routed through church institutions.

**Flagellants (1349).** The flagellant processions of 1349 are the clearest case of lay penance bypassing clerical mediation. Kieckhefer (1974) challenges the view that the movement began orthodox and was later radicalised from outside. He argues that anticlerical and heterodox tendencies developed within it. At Tournai the abbot Gilles li Muisis reported that much of the crowd valued the penitents' rite above the clergy's liturgy and mocked the clergy who opposed it: "magna pars vulgi factum talium plus quam divinum officium approbabant … quod propter hoc homines ecclesiasticos … sepissime deridebant" (Lemaître 1906, 239). Clement VI condemned the movement in a bull dated 20 October 1349; the copy to the archbishop of Magdeburg printed by Raynaldus begins *Molesta nobis* (Raynaldus 1880, 25:470–72). The bull charged that the penitents "claves Ecclesiae vilipendunt" and usurped jurisdiction, yet it expressly allowed private penance at home. What it banned was organised, self-governing public penance (Raynaldus 1880, 25:471–72). The movement collapsed within about a year. German scholarship on the flagellant songs and on Graus's (1987) "crisis" thesis is treated in section 8.

A later comparison sharpens the point. Bornstein (1993) stresses the orthodoxy of the Bianchi processions of 1399, also driven by fear of plague and apocalyptic expectation, and uses them to challenge the separation of lay and clerical religion. That clergy and communes absorbed the movement rather than condemning it is not confirmed in the descriptions and reviews consulted *[verify]*. The same penitential energy could run against the institution or through it, depending on the institution's response.

**Plague saints.** The cults of Sebastian and later Roch were new or newly intensified devotions. Marshall (1994) argues that the image of the wounded but living Sebastian worked as plague imagery. The arrow was an ancient symbol of pestilence, and the saint's acceptance of the arrows suggested vicarious sacrifice. Barker (2007) dates Sebastian's plague cult to after 1348. Mormando and Worcester (2007) collect essays on plague piety from Byzantium to the Baroque. These cults spread through altarpieces, confraternities and civic and clerical commissions, that is, through institutional channels. Li Muisis saw pilgrimages from Tournai to St Sebastian's relics at Hasnon and Soissons surge in 1349 and then stop: "Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio" (Lemaître 1906, 258).

**Confraternities and charity.** Henderson (1994) compares Florentine charity before and after 1348. He places confraternal charity within a network of church, state, guild and hospital, all organised around the theological virtue of Charity. Lay confraternities expressed lay initiative inside the church, not against it. Section 8 covers the Italian and French work on confraternities, including the *disciplinati*.

**Wills, chantries and masses.** Cohn's (1992) study of testaments from six Tuscan and Umbrian cities is the central quantitative evidence. He finds that the decisive change came after the second plague of 1363, not 1348. Testators of all classes stopped dividing bequests into many small pious gifts. Instead they concentrated them in larger gifts designed to perpetuate memory: chapels, perpetual masses and named artworks. Cohn reads this as a "cult of remembrance," not as a turn away from the church, since the bequests went to church institutions.

Wray (2009), using more than a thousand Bolognese testaments from 1348 itself, stresses continuity and resilient networks during the crisis, rather than the collapse reported by chroniclers. Chiffoleau's (1980) study of the Avignon region describes an "accounting" piety of accumulated masses for the dead *[verify argument; see section 8]*. In England, Platt (1996) traces investment in after-death soul-care and links demand for memorial masses to clergy leaving parishes for chantry posts. Reviewers fault his causal evidence. Kreider (1979) follows English chantries to their dissolution.

**Ars moriendi and the macabre.** The *ars moriendi* literature, cadaver ("transi") tombs and the *danse macabre* are often read as plague products. O'Connor (1942) remains the standard English study of the *ars moriendi* tradition. But the chronology argues against a direct 1348 effect. Cohen (1973) studies transi tombs from the late fourteenth to the seventeenth century and treats them as expressions of hope for resurrection, not only as warnings to the living *[verify nuance]*. Binski (1996) loosens the causal tie between plague and the macabre. Oosterwijk and Knöll (2011) take the 1420s Holy Innocents mural in Paris as the genre's starting point. The macabre peaks generations after 1348, which points to cumulative recurrence acting on an older penitential culture.

**The Meiss debate.** Meiss (1951) argued that the calamities of the 1340s, plague above all, produced a sterner, more hieratic religious art in Florence and Siena. He read it as a penitential response to divine punishment. Critics showed that the stylistic shift began before 1348 and was plural. Van Os (1981) reopened the problem of interpretation for Siena, and Boskovits and Cole made related arguments *[verify works]*. Steinhoff (2006) argues that post-1348 Sienese painting was "profoundly and intentionally pluralistic." She attributes it to new patronage and a new art market rather than to a penitential mentality.

Norman (1995) collects essays stressing continuity across 1348 *[verify]*. Green, Walker-Meikle and Müller (2014) warn that many "plague" images were mislabelled. The lesson for the whole project is that changes in religious *expression* after a catastrophe can be misread as changes in religious *feeling* when patronage, markets and earlier trends explain them.

**Relation to the model.** Rising demand for intercession is well supported. But for most Christians the evidence fits "faith up + institution up" better than "faith migrates"; the flagellants are the main, brief exception. The 1363 finding suggests that recurrence, not the first shock, drives durable change. Li Muisis's remark that pilgrimage and devotion ceased with the storm also supports a rival reading: short-term devotional peaks may track the acute threat and fade with deliverance, so durable change has to be shown beyond the waves themselves.

### 4.4 The clergy and institutional trust

The claim that trust in the church declined is the weaker half of the conventional picture. It rests mainly on older syntheses. Recent archival and quantitative work does not support a general collapse, but it does identify specific points of friction.

**The older thesis.** Ziegler (1969) and Gottfried (1983) associate the plague with heavy clerical losses, poorly prepared replacements and damage to the church's reputation *[verify specific claims]*. Cantor (2001) presents plague as weakening church authority, but specialists widely criticise the book. Jedwab, Johnson and Koyama (2022) list "a decline in the authority of religious institutions" among the plague's long-run effects. Following Johnson and Koyama (2019, 110–12), they argue that the plague's universality and the "inability of religious leaders to offer protection" called the church's legitimacy into question. Their evidence is historiographical, not a causal estimate.

**Clerical mortality and replacement.** Starting from the estimate that about half of England's priests died, Robinson (2019) uses surviving English ordination lists. Ordinations in 1349 far exceeded the annual average, then fell below half the pre-plague level for the rest of the 1350s. Crucially, plague-era priests came from much the same social and economic background as earlier priests. This undercuts the chain from "inferior replacement clergy" to anticlericalism. Robinson (2022) extends the analysis to clerical career prospects.

Diocesan studies point the same way. Dohar (1995) examines Hereford's administration, recruitment and pastoral provision across the century and stresses adaptation *[verify conclusion]*. Courtenay (1980) tests whether plague sharply reduced Oxford enrolment *[verify conclusion]*. Harper-Bill (1996) surveys the English church after the plague in cautious terms *[verify]*.

**Ralph of Shrewsbury.** In a mandate dated at Wiveliscombe on 10 January 1349 (iv Id. Jan. 1348, Old Style), the bishop of Bath and Wells wrote that plague had left many parishes without a priest: "cum non inveniantur presbyteri, qui velint devotionis zelo, aut pro stipendio aliquo curas … subire" (Wilkins 1737, 2:745). Many, he added, were dying without confession, perhaps because of fear of infection. In necessity the dying might confess to a layman, "et si desit vir laicus, mulieri" ("and if a layman is lacking, to a woman"). Those who recovered had to confess the same sins again to their own priest (Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46; see also Horrox 1994). Holmes's edition only calendars the mandate (Holmes 1896, no. 2130) and refers to Wilkins for the text. The letter is cited both as evidence of clerical desertion and as institutional adaptation. It is best read as an emergency use of canon-law norms of necessity, not a renunciation of clerical mediation: the bishop delegated the remedy and reclaimed it from the survivors. It also shows exactly where trust could break: at the deathbed, when no priest came.

**Chantries versus parishes.** Platt (1996) suggests a second friction point. Demand for memorial masses drew stipendiary priests away from the cure of souls. More devotion could thus produce more friction with the clergy who were left. The hierarchy said as much itself. Archbishop Islip's *Effrenata* (28 May 1350) complained that surviving priests left the cure of souls for better-paid annual masses and set a bad example to lay workers: "sacerdotes, qui jam supersunt … nec erubescentes quod eorum insatiabilis avaritia ab aliis operariis etiam laicis nequiter et perniciose trahitur in exemplum ; de animarum cura … non curant" (Wilkins 1737, 3:1). The complaint about greedy priests is therefore not only mendicant or Lollard polemic.

**Who stayed with the sick.** Chroniclers disagree, partly along lines of clerical rivalry. The Carmelite continuator of Guillaume de Nangis, usually identified as Jean de Venette, wrote that "in multis villis parvis et magnis sacerdotes timidi recedebant, religiosis aliquibus magis audacibus administrationem dimittentes." He praised the sisters of the Paris Hôtel-Dieu, "mori non timentes" (Géraud 1843, 2:211–12). Yet he also reports that the dying confessed, received the viaticum and left bequests to churches and religious (Géraud 1843, 2:213). Li Muisis records that many curates and chaplains who heard confessions and visited the sick died at their work (Lemaître 1906, 258).

**Papal response.** The papacy was active, not absent. Wood (1989) studies Clement VI's pontificate and ideas. Rollo-Koster (2015) shows the Avignon curia redistributing funds, deferring or reducing taxes, and granting indulgences and absolutions to plague victims who died without last rites. The *Vita* of Clement quoted by Raynaldus adds physicians paid to visit the poor, a new cemetery, and indulgences for priests who gave the sacraments to the sick (Raynaldus 1880, 25:454). Clement VI also issued two bulls in 1348 protecting the Jews. Raynaldus dates the first, a reissue of the *Sicut Judeis* protection, 4 July 1348, and the second, commonly called *Quamvis perfidiam*, 26 September 1348. The second argued that the plague struck Jews, and peoples who had never lived among Jews, alike, so the poisoning charge was implausible (Raynaldus 1880, 25:453–55). Raynaldus himself conceded that it failed: "ubique enim, excepto comitatu Avenionensi, tanquam venefici ad necem quaesiti" (Raynaldus 1880, 25:455). The 1350 Jubilee drew pilgrims to Rome through the indulgence system immediately after the first wave. That is evidence of mass devotion channelled through the papacy, not of an early collapse of trust.

**Lollardy and the Hussites.** No recent peer-reviewed English study argues a strong causal link from plague to Lollardy. The link survives mainly in older encyclopedic and confessional writing. For Bohemia, Mengel (2011) shows that the textbook map, in which Bohemia was spared in 1347–52, rests on a misreading. The 1380 epidemic was far more damaging there. Czech historiography has largely concluded that plague was not a decisive cause of the Hussite revolution (Nodl 2021).

**The long view.** Duffy (1992) argues that English Catholicism on the eve of the Reformation was vigorous and popular, not decadent or in decline. He does not centre the plague. But his work is the strongest evidence against a long post-plague erosion of institutional trust in England. Arnold (2005) supplies a useful distinction between belief, practice and trust in institutions. That distinction warns against reading anticlerical complaint as loss of faith.

**Relation to the model.** Trust tracked pastoral performance. It frayed where priests were absent from deathbeds and held where bishops dispensed, popes granted indulgences and parish provision recovered. The church's role should include its *capacity to deliver* care, not only its alignment with sufferers or perpetrators.

### 4.5 The persecution of Jews

The massacres of 1348–51 destroyed the principal Jewish communities of the Rhineland and much of the Empire. Cohn (2007) calls them "the most monumental of medieval Jewish persecutions." Historians and economists now agree that the violence was shaped by political structure, local elites and economic interest, not only by plague panic.

**Historical scholarship.** Cohn (2007) finds that the perpetrators differed socially from those in later pogroms. Town councils, princes and other elites were often involved. Barzilay (2022) traces well-poisoning accusations from the 1321 Lepers' Plot to their fifteenth-century decline. He argues that their preconditions existed in the thirteenth century and that municipal councils promoted them, partly for material gain. Nirenberg (1996) treats the 1348 attacks as contingent events shaped by local systems of coexistence, not as expressions of timeless hatred.

Haverkamp (1981) showed that many German pogroms occurred *before* plague reached a town *[verify; see section 8]*. Dols (1974a) links flagellant propaganda to a second wave of massacres from mid-1349. For German towns, however, Haverkamp (1981, 43) finds the flagellant processions closer in time to the plague wave than to the pogroms, which argues against treating them as co-perpetrators. Cohn (2018), surveying epidemics from Athens to AIDS, argues that epidemics more often produced compassion than hatred. On that view the Black Death massacres are an exception that needs explaining.

**Quantitative scholarship.** Jedwab, Johnson and Koyama (2019) use city-level data on mortality and persecution in Western Europe. They find that negative shocks raise the likelihood of persecution, but that more severe shocks lower it where Jews and Christians were economically complementary. Cities with higher plague mortality were less likely to persecute. The published article also reports that the protective effect was weaker in towns first infected during Christmastide and Easter, and stronger in Advent and Lent (Jedwab, Johnson, and Koyama 2019, 347, 382–83). The authors discuss this under "the liturgical and agricultural calendars," since some of the same months were also borrowing and repayment months. Close to Avignon, higher mortality brought no protection, and towns that were seats of bishops or archbishops showed only a weak, statistically insignificant extra protection (380).

Finley and Koyama (2018) find that communities governed by archbishoprics, bishoprics and imperial free cities suffered more intense and violent persecutions than those governed by the emperor or by secular princes. They treat these bishops as secular territorial rulers competing for the revenue from taxing Jews, and they reject a religious explanation of the effect. Anderson, Johnson and Koyama (2017) show, over 1100–1800, that cold growing seasons raised the probability of persecution, especially where states were weak. Voigtländer and Voth (2012) find that plague-era pogroms predict anti-Jewish violence in the 1920s, early Nazi voting and other measures of antisemitism in the same German towns. The link with the Nazi vote is clear in 1928, weaker in 1930, and vanishes as the party gains mass appeal. The persistence is absent in Hanseatic trading towns and in fast-growing towns with large inflows of outsiders. Becker and Pascali (2019) find that after the Reformation, persecution became more common in Protestant areas where Christians began to compete with Jews in moneylending.

**Jewish religious responses.** Einbinder (2018) studies Jewish responses to plague and violence in Provence and Iberia. She finds a fundamental continuity of worldview and means of expression. Foa (2000) stresses Jewish cultural agency after 1348 rather than a catalogue of disasters.

**Relation to the model.** This sub-case fits the model once "the church" is split into levels. The papacy formally opposed the killings, but its protection reached little beyond the papal enclave around Avignon (Raynaldus 1880, 25:455). Local ecclesiastical lordship correlated with worse violence, which Finley and Koyama attribute to bishops acting as rulers competing for Jewish tax revenue, not to religious zeal. For the Jewish victims, their own religious institutions remained the sufferers' community, and the evidence points to continuity rather than loss of faith.

### 4.6 Long-term effects: Reformation and secularisation

Grand claims linking the Black Death to the Reformation or to secularisation come mainly from synthesisers. Specialists largely reject them in their strong form. A weaker claim, that recurrent plague reshaped the economy of salvation the reformers later attacked, is defensible.

**Synthesis and rebuttal.** Huizinga's (1996 [1919]) image of an overwrought, death-obsessed late-medieval piety supplied the classic "crisis" frame, though he did not centre the plague. Bowsky (1971) framed the "turning point" as an open question. Herlihy (1997) treats the plague as a destructive but ultimately effective spur to renewal, mainly economic. Cohn (2002a) argues that falling mortality across successive waves bred cultural confidence, not religious despair. Bailey (2021) locates lasting institutional change in 1350–75, but in law, labour and economy rather than religion.

**Causal evidence.** Dittmar and Meisenzahl (2020) provide the cleanest causal link between plague and religious institutional change. They use plague outbreaks in 1500–1522 as an instrument for German cities' adoption of Protestant church ordinances, controlling for plague frequency in 1350–1499. Each additional outbreak raised the probability of adoption by 14 percentage points. Their proposed mechanism is that plagues weakened incumbent elites and increased demand for public-goods institutions. The measured outcome is the adoption of a church ordinance, a law that set up poor relief, schooling and pastoral care; it is not exit from the church or a change in belief. The effect was concentrated in cities subject to lords and muted in free cities (Dittmar and Meisenzahl 2020, 987).

This result concerns early-sixteenth-century plague, not the Black Death. The authors find no comparable institutional effect of fourteenth-century plague in German cities (988 n. 63), and they state the condition directly: "Before religious competition led to the articulation of institutional alternatives, these shocks did not lead to institutional change" (983). The shock eroded the old institution only once an organised alternative existed. Cantoni, Dittmar and Yuchtman (2018) show that the Reformation then shifted wealth, graduates and construction to secular uses. The chain from plague to secularisation is therefore indirect.

**Contrasting evidence.** Disasters can also entrench rulers who hold religious office. Belloc, Drago and Galbiati (2016) find that earthquakes delayed the move to communal self-government only in Italian cities where the bishop held political power. The published article also finds that after earthquakes these cities built and decorated more churches than cities under secular rulers, which supports, but does not by itself establish, the authors' reading that quakes strengthened religious authority. The delay was temporary: in the 2014 working draft (Belloc, Drago, and Galbiati 2014) it lasts no more than a decade. That draft lacks the church-building result and proxies religiosity only by present-day church counts. Chaney (2013) finds that Nile flood shocks strengthened Egypt's religious authorities without raising a proxy for popular religiosity.

**Relation to the model.** A shock seems to strengthen a religious institution when its leaders can credibly explain or mitigate it, and to weaken it when they cannot and an alternative exists. Kreider (1979) links the two ends of the period: the post-plague economy of intercession became a main Reformation target. Leppin (2025) is discussed in section 8.

### 4.7 The "new" Black Death scholarship

Since about 2010, ancient-DNA studies and the global turn have transformed the biology and geography of the Black Death. They have left the religious-history picture largely intact. Haensch et al. (2010) and Bos et al. (2011) confirmed *Yersinia pestis* as the agent. This ended the "was it plague?" debate, including Cohn's (2002a; 2002b) dissent. Little (2011) reviewed what that scientific turn meant for historians.

Green (2014; 2020) reframed the Black Death as a global, multi-lineage pandemic with Central Asian origins. Benedictow (2021) and Aberth (2021) offer full syntheses, and Aberth's includes religious responses to 1500. Belich (2022) offers an economic reading of the plague's long-term consequences.

Recent religious work is scattered. A. T. Brown (2026) studies plague in English popular memory *[verify content]*. Nissi and Testoni (2025) read Italian miracle testimonies from the canonisation processes of Clare of Montefalco and Nicholas of Tolentino through terror-management theory. That evidence predates 1348, so it is not plague evidence, but it could serve as a pre-plague baseline for death anxiety. No English monograph since 2010 has systematically tested "devotion up, institutional trust down" for the Black Death. No study measures belief and institutional trust together before and after the plague.

### Implications for the model

- **Demand rose, and the church supplied it.** For the Christian majority the modal outcome was "faith up + institution up": wills, chantries, saints and confraternities all ran through the church.
- **Punishment theology was not corrosive here.** Reading suffering as punishment for collective sin increased reliance on clerical penance, because the church controlled the remedy. The second variable should be paired with who controls the prescribed response.
- **Trust tracked pastoral performance.** Trust frayed at specific failures: absent priests at deathbeds (Ralph of Shrewsbury), clergy drawn to chantries (Platt 1996), suppressed lay penance (the flagellants). "Church role" should include capacity to deliver care.
- **Lay control of remedies came by three routes.** It was *delegated and reclaimed*: Ralph of Shrewsbury let the dying confess to lay men and women but required survivors to confess again to their own priest (Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46). It was *seized and suppressed*: the flagellants took over penance, preaching and, at Tournai, funerals and the bequests that went with them, until crown, town governors and papal bull stopped them (Lemaître 1906, 240, 251–52; Raynaldus 1880, 25:470–72). And it was *taken over by town authorities*: at Tournai, with the see vacant after the bishop's death in 1349, the city governors issued ordinances on burial and bell-ringing, "videntes quod decanus, et capitulum, et clerus totus, de remedio apponendo non curabant, quia sua intererat et in facto lucrabantur" (Lemaître 1906, 255). The model should record which route applied, since only the second and third set lay actors against the clergy over the remedy.
- **Recurrence and alternatives matter.** Durable change followed recurrent plague (Cohn 1992); institutional change (Protestant church ordinances) followed plague only where an organised alternative existed (Dittmar and Meisenzahl 2020).
- **"The church" must be disaggregated.** The papacy opposed the pogroms while local ecclesiastical lordship correlated with worse violence (Finley and Koyama 2018). Jewish victims kept faith within their own communities (Einbinder 2018).
- **Measurement gap.** No study measures belief and institutional trust together across the plague, and the Meiss debate warns against inferring feeling from expression.
