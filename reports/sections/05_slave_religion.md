## 5. Case 2: Enslaved and freed African Americans (c. 1619–1877)

Collective suffering under slavery produced intensified but reinterpreted Christian faith, combined with deep distrust of the white churches that sanctioned slavery. That faith migrated into structures the sufferers controlled: first the "invisible institution," then independent Black churches after 1865. The case supports the working model's second outcome, but with three corrections. Mass conversion came late, the white churches often excluded Black Christians before Black Christians rejected them, and doubt and non-Christian religion persisted as real minority currents.

### The classic historiography: survival, accommodation and the invisible institution

The field began with sociological and institutional studies. Du Bois's Atlanta conference report treated the Black church as a social institution with African antecedents, analysed apart from personal piety (Du Bois 1903). Woodson wrote the first historical survey, which presented independent denominations as achievements of self-direction (Woodson 1921). Mays studied how Black writers related God to their circumstances; his distinction between "compensatory" and "constructive" ideas of God is an early map of the theodicy problem (Mays 1938) *[verify categories against text]*.

The first great debate concerned African survivals. Herskovits argued that African culture survived the Middle Passage and attacked the "myth" of a people without a past (Herskovits 1941). Frazier replied that slavery stripped Africans of their social heritage, so the church became the main institution of a people denied all others (Frazier 1963). Later commentators treat the disagreement as one of degree, since Frazier accepted stronger survivals in the Caribbean and Latin America. Butler revived the Frazierian side with the claim that slavery caused an "African spiritual holocaust" that destroyed African religious systems, though not all practices (J. Butler 1990) *[verify wording and chapter]*.

The second debate concerned accommodation and resistance. Genovese argued that slave religion operated within a paternalist compact: it helped sustain the system yet gave the enslaved a weapon of self-defence and potential liberation (Genovese 1974). Raboteau, Levine, Blassingame, Sobel and Stuckey moved the emphasis to a religion the enslaved made their own. Raboteau's "invisible institution" described secret worship outside masters' supervision alongside attendance at biracial churches (Raboteau [1978] 2004). Levine reconstructed a "sacred world" from spirituals and folk tales in which the enslaved saw themselves as a chosen people (Levine [1977] 2007). Blassingame placed religion within a slave community that preserved autonomy against the "Sambo" thesis (Blassingame [1972] 1979).

Sobel and Stuckey pressed the African side further. Sobel argued that enslaved people fused an African sacred cosmos with Baptist teaching into an "Afro-Baptist" faith, though critics find her evidence for a coherent worldview thin (Sobel 1979). Stuckey read the ring shout as an ancestral African ritual beneath a Christian surface (Stuckey 1987). Wilmore separated radical, folk-rooted "Black religion" from an institutional "Black church" that could be deradicalized (Wilmore [1972] 1998). For the model, the classic consensus already separates faith (often intensified) from the masters' institutions (often rejected). It also assumed broad Christianization, which later work questions.

### Masters against baptism and the making of "Christian slavery"

For roughly a century after 1670, the main obstacle to Black conversion was the slaveholder, not the slave. Gerbner shows that missionaries arriving in the plantation colonies found most owners opposed to converting the enslaved, partly because baptism was feared to imply freedom (Gerbner 2018). Quaker, Anglican and Moravian missionaries answered with an ideology of "Christian slavery": conversion would make slaves loyal and industrious. In doing so they shifted the basis of free status from Christianity to whiteness.

Goetz documents the same process in Virginia county records. Planters and officials developed the idea of "hereditary heathenism," the belief that Africans and Indians were incapable of true conversion (Goetz 2012). A Virginia statute of 1667 declared that baptism did not free a slave *[verify]*. Glasson shows that the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, which owned the Codrington plantation in Barbados, came to treat slavery and Christianity as compatible (Glasson 2012). The established church was thus not a neutral bystander. It was, in places, a slaveholder.

Mass conversion arrived only with the evangelical revivals. Frey and Wood date the decisive shift to the Great Awakening and the post-Revolutionary Baptist and Methodist revivals before 1830 (Frey and Wood 1998). Ecstatic worship and Black exhorters let African forms survive within evangelical Protestantism. Antebellum "missions to the slaves" offered a controlled gospel of obedience, yet Black leaders used them to build organized congregations (Cornelius 1999). Clarke's studies of the Presbyterian missionary Charles Colcock Jones show enslaved hearers resisting or reinterpreting his catechism (Clarke 1979; Clarke 2005).

This literature strengthens the model's third variable. The church's role was not merely failure but alliance with the perpetrators, built into law and missionary theory. It also complicates the model's sequence: distrust could not be a response to a church that most enslaved people had not yet been allowed to join.

### The invisible institution and Exodus hermeneutics

The enslaved built a Christianity they controlled, centred on the Exodus. Secret meetings in "hush harbors," spirituals and the ring shout formed the "invisible institution" (Raboteau [1978] 2004). New work continues to probe its practices of concealment (Huneycutt 2026) *[argument not yet read]*. Exodus turned collective suffering into chosen-ness and divine promise. Glaude traces how Exodus shaped early nineteenth-century Black political language and the convention movement (Glaude 2000). Smith reads the Bible as a "conjure book" whose figures were used performatively to transform history (Smith 1994). Cone read the spirituals as a theology of liberation (Cone 1972).

The hermeneutic sharpened a contrast between the Christianity of Christ and the religion of masters. Douglass's 1845 appendix states it directly. He loved "the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ" and hated "the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land" (Douglass 1845). Enslaved Christians also turned damnation against slaveholders, using hell to condemn the masters' religion (Gin Lum 2014). Callahan's study of African Americans and the Bible offers a broader account of this reading tradition (Callahan 2006).

Prophetic faith could also drive revolt, though not uniformly. Nat Turner's apocalyptic Christianity was not shared by most local Black Christians, who faced choices to join, resist or stay out (Breen 2015). Gabriel's 1800 plot appears more secular and artisan-republican than religious (Egerton 1993) *[verify characterization]*. Charleston's African Methodist congregation, linked to the Vesey conspiracy, was suppressed after 1822 (Egerton 1999) *[verify details]*. Each revolt brought restrictions on Black preaching and assembly. The model gains a corollary here: when sufferers' own religious institutions become sites of resistance, the dominant power targets them, and faith goes underground.

### Islam, conjure and African-derived traditions

Not all enslaved people moved toward the Christian God. A significant minority of captives were Muslims, and some kept prayer, fasting and Arabic literacy for decades (Austin 1997; Diouf [1998] 2013; Gomez 2005). Umar ibn Said's Arabic writings are often read as evidence of Muslim belief beneath outward Christian affiliation *[verify]*. Gomez argues that African ethnic religious identities persisted well into the nineteenth century, slowing Christian conversion (Gomez 1998). No verified estimate of the Muslim share of North American captives exists in these notes.

African-derived ritual persisted alongside Christianity rather than against it. Creel shows the Gullah reshaping Baptist practice through African initiation and community structures (Creel 1988). Young links Lowcountry conjure, burial and spirit practices to Kongo and reads them as resistance to slavery's ideology (Young 2007). Rucker finds conjure, drum and transmigration beliefs underwriting revolts from 1712 to 1831 (Rucker 2006). Chireau rejects the African-versus-Christian binary and treats conjure and Christianity as complementary parts of one lived religion (Chireau 2003).

This strand complicates the model's outcome categories. Collective suffering drove people toward several religious resources at once. Conjure offered protection from immediate harm; Christianity offered ultimate meaning; Islam offered a rival monotheism with its own institutions. Coding responses as "faith up" or "faith down" misses this layering.

### Doubt, unbelief and theodicy

The loss-of-faith branch is real but thinly documented for the period before 1877. Cameron locates the origins of Black nonbelief in slavery, treating its brutality as an alternative origin for American religious scepticism (Cameron 2019). He draws on slave narratives in which formerly enslaved people reassessed Christianity, though most of his evidence is post-1877. Pinn's documentary history gathers humanist voices from Douglass and Du Bois to the twentieth century (Pinn 2001).

The theological argument is sharper than the historical one. William R. Jones asked whether massive, disproportionate Black suffering implied "divine racism" and proposed a humanocentric theism (Jones [1973] 1998). Pinn argued that suffering must be treated as unredeemably evil, which leads to Black humanism, and recovered a sceptical strand in slave narratives and spirituals (Pinn 1995). Douglass himself is ambiguous: his 1845 statement defends Christianity, while later in life he moved toward freethought *[verify]*.

For the model, the loss-of-faith branch appears where the Christian God is identified with the perpetrators and alternatives exist. Yet the documented examples are mostly literate, often free, individuals. The evidence cannot yet say how common doubt was among the enslaved majority, which leaves this branch underspecified.

### Gender: survival faith and women's religious cultures

Gender scholarship adds a third outcome between liberation and erosion: faith as survival. Williams reads the enslaved Hagar's wilderness as the experience of being alone in pain with only God, who sustains rather than delivers (Williams 1993). She critiques Exodus-centred Black theology for overlooking this pattern. Pierce shows five early Black spiritual autobiographers, including Zilpha Elaw, recasting slavery's earthly hell as a "New Jerusalem," with conversion transforming self and community (Pierce 2005).

Wells-Oghoghomeh argues that gender and race jointly formed the enslaved religious subject in the Lower South (Wells-Oghoghomeh 2021). Enslaved women's religious cultures took shape around sexual violence, motherhood and kin loss, drawing on African-derived and Christian resources outside formal churches. Black women also met exclusion inside Black institutions, as the AME refused to license women preachers *[verify]*. The model's "church role" variable thus needs a gender dimension: one community's own church may still be someone else's establishment.

### Biracial churches and exclusion

Rejection of the white church was often a response to exclusion, not a starting stance. Irons shows that colonial and antebellum Virginia churches were biracial and that Black evangelicals, joining in large numbers, unintentionally shaped white proslavery theology (Irons 2008). Sparks finds the typical Mississippi evangelical church biracial, with African American influence on ritual (Sparks 1994). Snay shows elite southern clergy building a religious defence of slavery that fed secession (Snay 1993). A recent study of antebellum Missouri extends the regional evidence (K. Butler 2023) *[argument not yet read]*.

The sequence of embrace followed by segregation recurs. Sensbach traces Afro-Moravians in North Carolina from relatively elastic relations to a separate Black congregation by 1822 (Sensbach 1998). Boles, using records of more than 400 northern congregations, finds them interracial but unequal, with Black members leaving over access and equality rather than theology (Boles 2020). The AME, formalized in 1816 under Richard Allen, embodies the resolution: keep the faith, leave the white institution, build one's own (Newman 2008; Dickerson 2020). Dickerson also shows that the AME developed its own tensions between governance and prophetic mission.

This pattern supports the model's migration outcome. It also adds a dynamic step the model lacks: trust declines through experienced inequality inside a shared institution, not only through the institution's distant alliance with power.

### The timing problem: Fountain and late conversion

Fountain challenges the assumption that most enslaved people were Christians before 1865. Using WPA narratives, he finds that fewer than 40 percent of those who gave a datable conversion experience became Christians before freedom (Fountain 2010). Freedom, on this reading, led to Christianity more than Christianity led to freedom. Schweninger called the study "provocative" and summarized its claim that the great majority of late-antebellum slaves did not accept Christ (Schweninger 2011). Other reviews exist but have not been assessed here (Scott 2011; Forret 2012).

The method has a known bias. WPA informants were mostly children under slavery and so would report later conversions *[inference; check reviews]*. Fountain nevertheless converges with Gomez and Frey and Wood on gradual, late conversion. If he is right, the "invisible institution" may describe a committed minority, not the majority.

This is a significant challenge to the model. If the largest wave of formal Christian commitment followed collective deliverance, then suffering alone did not drive it. A refined claim is that suffering raised religious demand, but formal commitment rose when sufferers could control the institution supplying it.

### Civil War and Reconstruction: the exodus into independent Black churches

Emancipation produced a mass departure from white-controlled southern churches into independent Black congregations and denominations. Dvorak calls this "an African-American exodus" and argues that freedpeople themselves drove it, although whites also pushed separation (Dvorak 1991) *[verify argument]*. Montgomery traces the rise of independent Black denominations and the preacher's role in community life (Montgomery 1993). Stowell treats white southern, white northern and Black responses together, including "the Black quest for religious autonomy" (Stowell 1998).

The membership figures are suggestive but need primary checking. AME membership is reported at about 20,000 in 1856, mostly in the North, about 70,000 in 1866, and about 207,000 in 1876 (*Appletons' Annual Cyclopaedia* 1867, 1877, as compiled in secondary sources) *[verify]*. No verified figures were found for Black losses from the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, or from southern Baptist bodies.

The exodus was not uniform. Hildebrand shows the AME, the Colored Methodist Episcopal Church and the northern Methodist Episcopal Church competing for freedpeople with different models of freedom (Hildebrand 1995). The CME, organized in 1870 with the blessing of the white southern Methodists, shows that some freedpeople chose continuity *[verify]*. Walker finds the AME's southern mission carrying northern bourgeois values that clashed with folk religion (Walker 1982) *[verify]*. Nicole Myers Turner shows that postemancipation Black churches in Virginia were not always politically engaged, and that a male ministry narrowed women's roles (N. Turner 2020).

White religious institutions then abandoned freedpeople. Blum shows northern Protestants reconciling with the white South around a "white republic" that helped end Reconstruction (Blum 2005). Harper shows freedpeople interpreting emancipation and its reversal through Exodus, Jubilee, exile and jeremiad (Harper 2016). Renewed collective suffering was absorbed into theology, which kept faith intact while confirming distrust of white churches. Hahn places churches among the networks of grassroots Black politics (Hahn 2003).

### Atlantic comparisons: separating power position from church role

In the US case, power position and church role are confounded. The enslaved were powerless, and the dominant churches were allied with their masters. Atlantic comparisons help separate the two, and Brazil separates them best.

The comparative field began with Tannenbaum, who argued that Iberian law and the Catholic Church recognized the slave's moral personality through sacraments and eased manumission (Tannenbaum 1946). Elkins extended this to argue that the US lacked such intermediary institutions (Elkins 1959). Klein's Virginia–Cuba comparison broadly supported Tannenbaum (Klein 1967). Degler rejected the claim of milder Iberian slavery and stressed demography and economy (Degler 1971). De la Fuente reframed the debate around how enslaved people used legal and religious institutions to press claims (de la Fuente 2004). That shift, from the church as protector to the church as a resource sufferers can use, is the framing most useful for the model.

**Brazil.** Catholicism was established, and religious orders were major slaveholders; the Benedictines ran the most extensive sugar operations of any corporate body (Schwartz 1982). Yet enslaved and freed Africans held their own chartered Catholic corporations, the black lay brotherhoods (Russell-Wood 1974). These irmandades handled burial, festivals, elected kings and queens, and sometimes manumission aid. Soares shows Mina Africans in Rio rebuilding ethnic communities through brotherhoods (Soares [2000] 2011). Kiddy traces Rosary brotherhoods in Minas Gerais from the colonial period to the present (Kiddy 2005), and Reginaldo shows "Angola" Africans dominating Bahian Rosary brotherhoods (Reginaldo 2011).

The Brazilian evidence shows faith attaching to lay-controlled structures, not to the hierarchy. In the 1836 Cemiterada, Salvador's population rioted to defend church burial against a secularizing reform (Reis [1991] 2003). That is the opposite of institutional distrust. Yet rejection also occurred: the 1835 Malê uprising was organized by African Muslims outside Catholicism (Reis [1986] 1993). Calundu and healing practices persisted under Inquisition prosecution (Mello e Souza [1986] 2004; Sweet 2003). Sweet adds that much Catholic practice was nominal, a caution against reading brotherhood membership as deep conversion.

**Cuba and Spanish America.** Licensed African associations show the same pattern of faith within institutions. Cuban cabildos de nación, organized under church and colonial oversight, were the organizational base of the 1812 Aponte conspiracy (Ortiz 1921; Childs 2006). In Mexico, enslaved Africans used ecclesiastical courts and the Inquisition to gain leverage over masters (Bennett 2003; Bristol 2007). Institutional trust and political militancy were not opposites.

**British Caribbean.** The established Anglican Church was planter-aligned and attracted little trust (Gerbner 2018). Dissenting missions that gave the enslaved offices as deacons and class leaders produced intense faith. In Demerara in 1823 and Jamaica in 1831–32, chapel networks became the networks of rebellion (da Costa 1994; M. Turner 1982). Enslaved Christians acted on their own reading of spiritual equality against missionaries' counsel of neutrality. Moravian missions show a Protestant parallel to Brazil: the church owned slaves yet built the earliest Black Protestant congregations, including one shaped by the freed woman Rebecca Protten (Sensbach 2005; Hüsgen 2016).

**Haiti.** Saint-Domingue is the limit case. French law required baptism, but missions were thin and planter-dependent, and planters distrusted clerical zeal (Peabody 2002). Enslaved people had no Catholic corporate bodies comparable to the irmandades. Religious agency organized outside the church in Vodou, which served as an idiom of insurgent solidarity in 1791 (Geggus 2002; Dubois 2004). The Catholic label alone therefore predicts little; institutional access predicts more.

**Kongolese Christianity as a confound.** Many West Central African captives came from the Kingdom of Kongo, officially Catholic since the early sixteenth century (Thornton 1998a; Fromont 2014; Heywood 2002). Some arrived already Christian, carrying an African-controlled Christianity. The Antonian movement of Beatriz Kimpa Vita had already criticized European missionaries from within Christianity (Thornton 1998b). Faith that intensified under slavery may therefore partly be imported African Christianity that held clerical institutions at arm's length (Thornton 1988).

Brazil is the best comparison because the enslaved there were as powerless as in the US South, yet controlled their own church bodies. That isolates the church-role variable from power position. Brazil also mirrors Black Death Europe on all three institutional features: an established church, clerical landlords and lay confraternities devoted to death and intercession. Two confounds must be acknowledged. Brazil's large African-born population sustained ethnic brotherhoods and Islam, as Degler's demographic critique implies, and Kongolese prior Christianization affected uptake.

### A methodological caution: the trope of natural Black religiosity

The assumption that suffering Black people naturally "turn to God" has its own history. Evans traces American ideas about Black religion from the antebellum period to the mid-twentieth century (Evans 2008). The belief in innate Black religiosity was used to justify inequality as Americans came to prize reason over piety. Cameron's study of Black nonbelief likewise argues against the view that African Americans are naturally religious (Cameron 2019).

This supports the model's exclusion of race as a variable. The comparative evidence points the same way: responses varied with institutional access, demography and timing, not with ancestry. A model that predicts Black piety from race would reproduce the trope Evans dissects.

### Implications for the model

- **Supported:** Collective suffering under a Christian slaveholding order produced intensified faith and distrust of perpetrator-allied churches, and faith migrated to sufferer-controlled bodies (hush harbors, AME, post-1865 Black churches). This matches the model's second outcome.
- **Refine the church-role variable:** What predicts faith intensification is sufferers' access to offices and congregational structures, not denomination or establishment as such. Brazil, the British dissenting missions and Haiti show this most clearly, with Brazil best separating church role from power position.
- **Add a time dimension:** Distrust often followed exclusion within shared churches (Boles, Sensbach), and formal commitment may have peaked after deliverance (Fountain). The model should predict that formal institutional faith rises when sufferers gain control, not only when they suffer.
- **Add "survival faith" and religious layering:** Womanist scholarship and conjure studies show outcomes beyond liberation or erosion. Faith may sustain without delivering, and Christianity, conjure and Islam may operate together.
- **The loss-of-faith branch needs better evidence:** Cameron, Pinn and Jones establish it as real, but pre-1877 documentation is thin and skewed to literate, often free, individuals.
- **Race is not the variable:** Evans shows natural Black religiosity to be a historical trope; comparative variation tracks situation and institutional access.
