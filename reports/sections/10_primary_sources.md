## 10. Guide to primary sources

The primary record for both cases shows the same split: devotion grows through channels that sufferers control, while distrust settles on institutions that fail them or that their oppressors run. Neither record measures "trust" directly. Every inference rests on proxies such as bequests, exits, attendance, separate foundations and complaints. This section maps those proxies, the editions in which to read them, and the biases that shape each one.

### How the sources bear on devotion and institutional trust

The working model needs two separate measurements: the intensity of faith, and confidence in the institutions that mediate it. The sources fall into five evidential types, and each type measures one variable better than the other.

| Evidence type | Black Death examples | Slavery examples | Best measures | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Serial behavioural records | Wills, guild returns, institutions to benefices | Church membership counts, Freedmen's Bureau files, minute books | Where people put money and membership, and when | Silent on motive |
| Institutional self-reports | Episcopal letters and mandates, papal bulls | Planter-mission reports, SPG and Moravian records | Institutional failure admitted by insiders | Written to defend the institution |
| First-person and chronicle testimony | Chronicles, Petrarch, Boccaccio | Published narratives, WPA and Fisk interviews | Meaning, theodicy, anger at clergy | Clerical authors; editors and interviewers |
| Devotional and liturgical texts | Flagellant songs, *ars moriendi*, danse macabre | Spirituals, conversion narratives | Content of devotion and who controls it | Transcribed by outsiders or later compilers |
| Hostile or coerced records | Jewish "confessions", anti-flagellant polemic | Nat Turner's *Confessions*, Vesey trial record | Elite fear of autonomous devotion | Produced under coercion |

The decisive analytical move is to code each source for the *locus of control* of the devotion it records. Bequests to a lay guild rather than the parish, or a hush-harbour meeting rather than a planter-sponsored sermon, register both rising devotion and a shift of trust. Read that way, the two corpora support the model's middle outcome: faith up, establishment trust down, with faith moving into bodies the sufferers run.

Two open problems in the model also have source-level answers. Samuel Cohn's argument that 1363, not 1348, marks the turning point (Cohn 1992) requires sources from the recurrences: the 1388–89 guild returns, Brinton's sermons, *ars moriendi* and the danse macabre. Daniel Fountain's claim that mass conversion came largely after emancipation (Fountain 2010) rests on a quantitative survey of ex-slave testimony, including the WPA narratives, in which fewer than 40 percent of datable conversions came before freedom. It therefore inherits that testimony's biases. It should be tested against church membership series and Freedmen's Bureau records.

### Source-critical issues

#### Black Death

The chroniclers are almost all clergy, and many are mendicants or monks. Venette was a Carmelite, Herford a Dominican, Clyn and Piazza Franciscans, li Muisis an abbot and Knighton an Augustinian canon. Their attacks on fleeing or greedy secular priests partly reflect mendicant rivalry. They are therefore weak direct evidence of lay distrust.

Three further problems follow. Boccaccio and Kantakouzenos model their descriptions on earlier plague accounts, so "breakdown of rites" passages are partly literary topoi. Knighton, Stefani and Königshofen wrote decades later and may project Lollard-era or Schism-era anticlericalism back onto 1348–50. Nearly all descriptions of flagellants come from clerical opponents, and the Jewish "confessions" were extracted under torture.

The correction is triangulation with records the clergy did not write for polemic. Episcopal letters such as Ralph of Shrewsbury's show the institution recording its own weakness. Wills, guild returns and benefice registers give behavioural series. Cohn's testament corpus shows how such series can be read (Cohn 1992).

#### Slavery

The WPA interviews are mediated at several points. Most interviewers were white Southerners, and informants were elderly, often on relief, and remembered childhood (Blassingame 1975; Escott 1979). State offices edited texts and imposed dialect conventions (Musher 2001; Stewart 2016). Praise of kind masters or white preachers may be deference, not belief.

The Fisk collections offer a partial control because Black interviewers collected them in the late 1920s. But *God Struck Me Dead* was selected for conversion content, which inflates the evidence of intense devotion. Minton (2025) now offers a systematic reading of song and worship in the WPA corpus.

The antebellum narratives carry abolitionist mediation. White sponsors wrote prefaces (Garrison, Child), amanuenses shaped texts (Gray, Gilbert, Wilson for Northup), and the genre rewarded attacks on "slaveholding religion". Their hostility to white churches is partly campaign rhetoric. Narrators were also mostly literate, Protestant and male fugitives, so Muslim and African-derived practice is under-represented. Coerced records, notably Turner's *Confessions* and the Vesey trial, require the scepticism shown by Johnson (2001).

| Problem | Case | Bias it introduces | Corrective source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clerical, often mendicant authorship | Black Death | Overstates distrust of secular clergy | Wills, guild returns, episcopal registers |
| Delayed composition | Black Death | Projects later anticlericalism onto 1348–50 | Contemporary letters and mandates |
| Hostile and coerced records | Both | Frames lay devotion as threat | Devotional texts (songs, spirituals) |
| White interviewers and editors | Slavery (WPA) | Understates distrust of white religion | Fisk interviews; published narratives |
| Abolitionist genre | Slavery (narratives) | Overstates distrust of white religion | Mission reports; church minute books |
| Selection for conversion content | Slavery (Fisk) | Overstates intensity of devotion | WPA corpus as a whole |

The two slavery biases run in opposite directions. WPA deference understates distrust of white institutions, while abolitionist genre overstates it. Agreement between the two corpora is therefore stronger evidence than either alone.

### Bridge text: Jones and Allen on the 1793 Philadelphia yellow fever

Absalom Jones and Richard Allen's *Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, during the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia* (1794) is the one text that joins the two cases. It concerns an epidemic, like the Black Death. Its authors belong to a subordinate group within a white-dominated church, like the enslaved. It was written in the same months as their congregations were separating from white control.

The context is well documented (Powell 1949; Estes and Smith 1997; Nash 1988). At the epidemic's height, civic leaders asked the Free African Society to nurse the sick and bury the dead, partly on the false belief that Africans were immune. Black Philadelphians served and died. Mathew Carey's *Short Account of the Malignant Fever* (1793) then accused some Black nurses of extortion and theft. Jones and Allen answered with a detailed defence of their community's conduct and costs *[verify specific figures before quoting]*. They appended addresses to slaveholders and to "the people of colour" *[verify titles]*.

Three features matter for the model. First, collective suffering raised demand for care and burial, and a Black religious association, not a white church, met it. Second, the pamphlet turns service into a claim to moral and civic standing against a white author's slander, so trust in white civic voices falls while confidence in the community's own leaders rises. Third, it is an epidemic text from a free Northern community, not a plantation. It thus separates power position from enslavement, partly easing the confound the model notes for the US case. Spires (2019) reads it as a founding text of Black "neighborly citizenship", and Newman (2008) places it in Allen's institution-building.

The dating link should be checked, not assumed. Allen's *Life* (1833) dates the St George's walkout to 1787, but historians have argued for 1792–93 *[verify; see Nash 1988 and Newman 2008]*. If the later date holds, the walkout, the epidemic and the 1794 foundings of St Thomas's African Episcopal Church and Bethel cluster within about two years. The text is free on IA, and a modern excerpt appears in Parascandola (2024).

1. Horrox, ed. and trans., *The Black Death* (1994). The broadest English entry to the Black Death record; use Aberth (2017) as a second reader.
2. Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate of 10 January 1349: Latin in Wilkins (1737, 2:745–46), calendared in Holmes (1896, no. 2130), English in Horrox (1994). The institution admits it cannot deliver the sacraments.
3. Hugo of Reutlingen's flagellant songs (Runge 1900), with Closener's Strasbourg chronicle (Hegel 1870–71). Lay devotion outside clerical control, and its suppression.
4. Knighton's *Chronicle* (Martin 1995). The canonical complaint about clerical decline, to be read against its late date.
5. London Husting wills (Sharpe 1889–90). A free behavioural series on where lay testators placed trust.
6. *Born in Slavery* (Library of Congress) or Rawick (1972–79). The largest testimonial base, read with Blassingame (1975) and Stewart (2016).
7. *God Struck Me Dead* (Johnson 1969). Conversion as devotion independent of white churches.
8. Douglass, *Narrative* (1845), with its Appendix. The sharpest statement of faith retained and institution rejected.
9. C. C. Jones, *The Religious Instruction of the Negroes* (1842). The planter mission's own account of its aims and limits.
10. Jones and Allen, *Narrative of the Proceedings* (1794), with Allen's *Life* (1833). The bridge text: epidemic, subordinate church and institution-building in one record.

### Implications for the model

- **Supports the two-track pattern.** In both corpora devotion rises in sufferer-controlled channels (flagellant songs, guilds, hush harbours, conversion visions, Black churches), while complaints target institutions that failed or sided with power.
- **Trust is only measured by proxy.** The model's "institutional trust" variable must be operationalised through locus of bequests, membership, attendance and exits. Testimony alone cannot carry it.
- **Source bias can mimic the model.** Mendicant and abolitionist authors had motives to stress clerical failure. The model needs evidence that survives triangulation with behavioural series and institutional self-reports.
- **Recurrence needs later sources.** Testing Cohn's 1363 thesis requires the 1388–89 guild returns, Brinton, *ars moriendi* and post-1360 testaments, not the 1348–50 chronicles alone.
- **The confound is partly addressable.** The 1794 Philadelphia pamphlet and free Northern Black church records separate subordinate position from enslavement. Brazilian sources, not covered here, remain the stronger test.
- **The loss-of-faith branch is thinly documented.** Neither corpus contains many voices that leave Christianity, partly because survivors, believers and literate narrators dominate the record.
