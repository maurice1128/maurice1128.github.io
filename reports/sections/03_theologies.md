## 3. Christian theologies of suffering

How a theology frames collective suffering predicts two outcomes that can move apart: faith in God, and trust in the church that mediates or legitimates the suffering. Two families of frames recur from the Hebrew Bible to the present. Retributive-penitential frames read catastrophe as punishment for collective sin and route repentance through the institution. Solidarity frames place God with the sufferer, through Exodus, lament, the cross and the "crucified people." The first empowers the mediating church and then exposes it when intercession fails. The second can sustain faith through defeat while withdrawing legitimacy from a church allied with the oppressor. This section maps that contrast across the tradition. It is a typology of interpretive resources, not a set of measured outcomes; the predictions it generates need the historical evidence assembled in other sections.

### 3.1 Biblical traditions: retribution and its counter-voices

The canon holds both frames in tension. Deuteronomistic theology (Deut 28; the sin, punishment, cry and deliverance cycle of Judges; 2 Kgs 17 and 24–25) explains defeat and exile as punishment. Crenshaw classes "punishment for sin" as one canonical answer to evil among several, alongside mystery, limited divine power and the tension between justice and mercy (Crenshaw 2005). Under this frame disaster becomes a summons to repent under priestly or prophetic mediation. It is the scriptural template for the retributive type.

Against it stand Job, Lamentations, the lament psalms and Qoheleth. Newsom reads Job as a "contest of moral imaginations" in which the friends' retributive logic, Job's protest and the whirlwind speeches compete without final resolution (Newsom 2003). O'Connor stresses that in Lamentations God never answers; the silence makes room for survivors' voices (O'Connor 2002). Westermann established lament as one of the two basic forms of psalmic prayer (Westermann 1981). Brueggemann's orientation, disorientation and new orientation scheme gives lament a place in the life of faith rather than at its edge (Brueggemann 1984).

Brueggemann's essay "The Costly Loss of Lament" supplies the mechanism most relevant to institutional trust. Lament keeps the covenant two-sided, legitimates the "pain-bearer" and makes justice a proper theological question (Brueggemann 1986). When lament disappears, worship becomes a one-sided celebration that serves established power. Fretheim adds the exegetical basis for divine pathos: in the Old Testament God suffers because of, with and for Israel (Fretheim 1984). The Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53 and Pauline co-suffering (Rom 8:17; 2 Cor 4; Phil 3:10) extend this line, though the notes for this section lack a verified modern study of either.

For the working model, the point is that the Bible does not dictate an outcome. The relevant question is which canonical voice a community made dominant under catastrophe, and who controlled that choice. A community that keeps lament can protest to God and against power in the same breath.

### 3.2 Early church and plague

Third-century bishops framed plague as a test and a release, not chiefly as punishment. Cyprian's *De mortalitate* (c. 252) tells Christians not to fear the pestilence because it leads to immortality; the righteous and the wicked die alike but are received differently (Cyprian [c. 252] 1886). Dionysius of Alexandria, quoted by Eusebius, praised Christians who, "heedless of the danger," nursed the sick, and called their deaths "the equal of martyrdom" (Eusebius 1965, *Hist. eccl.* 7.22). He contrasted this with pagans who cast the sick into the streets. A study in *Histos* reads the passage as modelled on Thucydides' plague narrative, so it is apologetic rhetoric rather than neutral data.

Stark turned this material into a sociological mechanism. Mutual aid, he argued, gave Christians a far higher survival rate than pagans. Epidemics also destroyed pagans' social ties and made new ties to Christians both necessary and easier to form (Stark 1996, ch. 4). The thesis is influential and contested. A 1998 symposium in the *Journal of Early Christian Studies* explored the implications of its growth arithmetic (Hopkins 1998), questioned its rhetoric of scientific method (Klutz 1998) and criticised its treatment of gender (Castelli 1998); Stark replied in the same issue (Stark 1998).

The epidemiological base has also shifted. Harper reconstructed the "Plague of Cyprian" (c. 249–270) as a pandemic entering the empire from Ethiopia via Egypt (Harper 2015). Huebner revised its origin and spread and doubted that plague was the root of the third-century crisis (Huebner 2021). Scholars therefore agree on the texts but disagree on mortality differentials, conversion rates and causal weight. Stark is best cited as a hypothesis about mechanisms: care, networks and visible contrast.

Two later developments matter for the model. Augustine, answering pagan claims that the sack of Rome in 410 punished the abandonment of the gods, argued that good and evil people suffer the same temporal calamities and differ in how they use them (Augustine 1998, *Civ.* 1.8 *[verify locus]*). This decouples catastrophe from a simple tally of collective sin. By the late sixth century, however, plague called forth clergy-led penance. Gregory of Tours records Rogation processions under Bishop Gallus at Clermont in 543 and episcopal vigils at Marseille in 588 (Gregory of Tours 1974, *Hist.* 4.5, 9.22). He also reports Gregory the Great's sevenfold litany in plague-struck Rome in 590 (*Hist.* 10.1 *[verify]*).

The early-church material fits the model's first outcome: faith up and institution up. But the church in question was a persecuted minority that did not control penance for the whole society. It could contrast its own care with pagan flight. Its standing depended on visible service, not on the efficacy of its intercession.

### 3.3 Medieval penitential theology, purgatory and Julian of Norwich

Black Death sources overwhelmingly read the plague as divine vengeance for sin (Horrox 1994; Aberth 2005). The responses were penitential processions, masses, flagellant bands and, in many places, anti-Jewish violence. Le Goff showed that purgatory became a doctrinal "place" only in the late twelfth century (Le Goff 1984). By 1348 it supported an economy of suffrages, chantries and indulgences through which mass death turned into demand for clerical intercession.

This is the retributive type at full institutional strength. The clergy brokered repentance, so the church's authority rose with the demand for it. The same logic made the institution accountable for results. When repeated waves in 1361 and 1369 defeated intercession, and clergy died or fled, the penitential frame could become an indictment of clerical failure.

The flagellants show the institution defending its monopoly. The church first tolerated them, and some clergy joined. Once the movement read "letters from heaven" and claimed direct penitential access, Clement VI condemned it in a bull of 20 October 1349 as a menace to the priesthood (Clement VI 1349). Self-castigation was permitted only under ecclesiastical regulation, and Gregory XI renewed the ban in 1372. Lay penitential fervour, in other words, threatened the clergy precisely because it took the retributive frame seriously.

A solidarity current ran alongside. Ross reads late-medieval English images of the wounded Christ as revealing a trinitarian God of mercy rather than a God of vengeance (Ross 1997). Bynum traces blood cults such as Wilsnack and the theological debates they provoked, including their links to anti-Jewish libels (Bynum 2007). Julian of Norwich, who lived through the plagues of 1348–49, 1361–62 and 1369, received her visions in 1373 during a near-fatal illness (Julian of Norwich 2006; Colledge and Walsh 1978). Her assurance that "alle shalle be wele" (Long Text, ch. 27 *[verify]*) pairs sin with mercy, not wrath (McGinn 2012).

Aquinas's account of evil as privation supplied the scholastic frame within which both currents operated (Thomas Aquinas 2003). The inference for the model is that devotion to the suffering Christ could intensify without requiring the same clerical mediation. That would fit a pre-Reformation pattern of intense lay piety combined with criticism of the clergy. This is an inference from the sources, not a claim they make.

### 3.4 Reformation and early modern providentialism

The Reformation did not abolish the retributive frame; it changed the agent of mediation. Luther's 1527 letter "Whether One May Flee from a Deadly Plague," written during the Wittenberg outbreak, charts a middle path between panic and recklessness (Luther 1968, 119–38). Pastors and officials must stay at their posts; others may flee if their neighbours are provided for. Plague becomes a call to vocation and neighbour-love rather than to purchased intercession.

Zwingli's "Pestlied" (1519–20) is a personal lament. He returned to plague-struck Zurich, nursed the sick, fell ill and lost his brother; the stanzas track onset, crisis and recovery (Zwingli 1519). The song draws on lament psalms (Pss 22, 69, 143). Calvin's doctrine of providence (*Institutes* 1.16–18) teaches that nothing happens by chance and that the doctrine should comfort the afflicted (Calvin 1960).

Walsham shows that providentialism was shared across English society, cutting across differences of status, creed, education and wealth, and was not confined to Puritan circles (Walsham 1999). Sermons, ballads and pamphlets read fires, plagues and monstrous births as judgments. Judgment readings therefore survived the Reformation and spread through print. They now pointed toward moral and civic reform administered by preachers and magistrates.

Leibniz's *Theodicy* (1710) converted the question into a philosophical project of justifying God (Leibniz 1985). The Lisbon earthquake of 1755 and Voltaire's poem on it challenged the optimism of that project (Voltaire 2000). Neiman treats Lisbon as the moment natural and moral evil began to separate in modern thought (Neiman 2002). The question shifted from "what sin caused this?" to "can God be justified?"

For the model, the early modern period shows a third position within the retributive family. Institutions that serve rather than mediate (Luther's pastors at their posts) are judged by presence and care. Their legitimacy depends less on whether penance "works."

### 3.5 Modern theology and the critique of theodicy

Twentieth-century theology turned toward divine co-suffering. Barth treated evil as *das Nichtige*, "nothingness," which God rejects in electing creation (Barth 1960, §50). Bonhoeffer wrote from prison on 16 July 1944 that "only the suffering God can help" (Bonhoeffer 2010 *[verify page]*). Kitamori's *Theology of the Pain of God*, published in Japan in 1946 after defeat and the atomic bombings, located God's pain in love overcoming wrath (Kitamori 1965). Moltmann made the cross the foundation and criticism of Christian theology (Moltmann 1974).

Sölle attacked what she called "theological sadism," the image of a God who sends suffering as punishment or pedagogy (Sölle 1975 *[verify wording]*). She traced a movement from mute suffering through lament to solidarity and change, and criticised Moltmann for remaining too close to the logic he rejected. Metz grounded faith in the "dangerous memory" of suffering, which disrupts bourgeois religion. His diagnosis bears directly on this review: the crisis of Christianity is less a crisis of its message than of its subjects and institutions (Metz 2007).

Latin American liberation theology gave co-suffering a social subject. Gutiérrez read Job as a model of speaking about God from the suffering of the innocent poor (Gutiérrez 1987). Ellacuría's "The Crucified People" (1978) identified the oppressed majority with the crucified Christ (Ellacuría 1993). After Ellacuría's assassination in 1989, Sobrino made the phrase central (Sobrino 1993). His essay title "*Extra pauperes nulla salus*" deliberately inverts *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* and moves the locus of salvation from the institution to the poor (Sobrino 2008).

A parallel anti-theodicy current judges theoretical justifications of evil to be morally suspect. Surin traced the "problem of evil" to Enlightenment philosophical theism (Surin 1986). Tilley argued that theodicy as a discourse practice must be abandoned (Tilley 1991). Hauerwas, writing on children's deaths, criticised theodicy and the way medicine is asked to serve as one (Hauerwas 1990). Swinton proposed lament, forgiveness, hospitality and friendship as a "practical theodicy" of resistance (Swinton 2007). Hart, after the 2004 tsunami, rejected any account that makes catastrophe part of God's plan (Hart 2005).

Not all modern theologians abandon theodicy. Hick's soul-making theodicy remains the standard foil (Hick 1966). Adams accepted the project but argued that "horrendous evils" require resources beyond moral calculation, such as purity, honour and aesthetic categories (Adams 1999). The debate thus runs between those who would explain and those who would only accompany. For the model, anti-theodicy predicts that churches offering explanations of collective catastrophe lose credibility, while churches offering presence gain it. Stoddart's typology of outlier church responses to COVID-19 does not show a retributive frame at the margins: his "rebuke" category groups prosperity-gospel spiritual-warfare responses that treat the virus as demonic and claim God's promised protection and healing, a protective rather than retributive reading (Stoddart 2021).

### 3.6 Contextual theologies

Contextual theologies mostly radicalise the solidarity frame and attach it to a critique of the church of the oppressor. What unites them is a structural position, not an ethnicity: each speaks from a group whose suffering is caused or legitimated by people who share, or once imposed, the same faith.

**Black and womanist theology.** Raboteau's "invisible institution" names a parallel, unofficial church among the enslaved (Raboteau 2004). Douglass gave the cleanest primary statement of the pattern. He loved "the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ" and hated "the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land" (Douglass 1845, appendix). Glaude traced Exodus rhetoric through the Negro Convention movement of 1830–43 (Glaude 2000). Cone read the spirituals as theology (Cone 1972), built a theology of God's partisanship for the oppressed (Cone 1975), and identified the cross with the lynching tree (Cone 2011). Roberts paired liberation with reconciliation (Roberts 1971).

A third position challenges the solidarity frame from within. Jones asked whether a God who permits maldistributed Black suffering is a "white racist," and proposed "humanocentric theism" (Jones 1973). Pinn argued that the doctrine of redemptive suffering is fundamentally flawed and turned to Black humanism (Pinn 1995). Williams, reading Hagar, rejected surrogacy models of atonement that valorise suffering (Williams 1993). Townes's collection and Copeland's theology of enslaved women's bodies widened the womanist critique (Townes 1993; Copeland 2010). These works predict that some sufferers will reject the faith, or keep the community while dropping the doctrine. They supply the model's loss-of-faith branch.

**Latin America.** Gutiérrez, Ellacuría and Sobrino (section 3.5) speak from a continent where the persecuting state and much of the elite were Catholic. Their category of the "crucified people" predicts faith rising among the poor while institutional Christianity allied with power loses legitimacy.

**Asia.** Kitamori showed that suffering-God theology arose independently of European theology. C. S. Song's *Third-Eye Theology* and *The Compassionate God* developed an Asian theology of a God who suffers with the marginalised (Song 1979, 1982). His *Jesus, the Crucified People* opened a christological trilogy (Song 1990). Koyama's *Water Buffalo Theology* modelled contextual reflection from Thai village life (Koyama 1974). Korean minjung theology made the suffering people the subject of history, and Suh Nam-dong's essay on *han* named their accumulated, unresolved wound (CTC-CCA 1983). Park reworked the doctrine of sin around the *han* of the sinned-against rather than the guilt of the sinner (Park 1993).

**Taiwan.** The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) issued three statements under martial law. The 1971 "Statement on Our National Fate" asked that the people decide Taiwan's future (PCT 1971). "Our Appeal" followed in 1975 (PCT 1975). The 1977 "Declaration on Human Rights" called for Taiwan to become "a new and independent country" (PCT 1977). Wang Hsien-chih's homeland theology (鄉土神學), developed at Tainan Theological College in the late 1970s, gave these stances a theological basis (Wang 1988 *[verify]*). Recent work examines how the PCT's human-rights narrative has changed (Rychetská 2022) and asks whether a new homeland theology is emerging amid rising nationalism (Liu 2024). Here a church gained moral authority by siding with a suffering people against the state.

**Dalit, Palestinian and Native American.** Nirmal identified Jesus as Dalit through Isaiah 53 and called for "methodological exclusivism" (Nirmal 1990). Clarke grounded Dalit theology in fieldwork among Paraiyar communities (Clarke 1998). Ateek articulated the first Palestinian theology of liberation, reading Exodus and justice from the side of the dispossessed (Ateek 1989). Tinker's case studies of Eliot, Serra, De Smet and Whipple show the missionary church as an agent of cultural genocide (Tinker 1993). Deloria set a Native view of religion against the Christianity that missions had imposed (Deloria 1973). This is the model's rejection branch: when Christianity is identified with the perpetrators and an alternative exists, faith itself can be refused.

**Africa.** Éla developed an African liberation theology from Cameroon (Éla 1986, 1988). Katongole's ethnography of lament in Congo, Burundi, Uganda and South Sudan treats lament as the root of hope and of nonviolent activism (Katongole 2017). It is the closest contemporary study to the claim that lament can sustain faith and power institutional critique.

**Holocaust theology.** Jewish responses define the outer range of possibilities. Rubenstein rejected the God of history and of election (Rubenstein 1966). Fackenheim's "614th commandment" forbade granting Hitler a posthumous victory (Fackenheim 1970). Berkovits invoked the hiddenness of God's face (Berkovits 1973). Greenberg proposed that no theological statement should be made that would not be credible in the presence of the burning children (Greenberg 1977 *[verify wording]*). Wiesel staged a trial of God (Wiesel 1979). For Christian theology these works demand self-criticism of the churches' anti-Judaism, a case in which the church stood with the perpetrators.

### 3.7 A typology of frames and predicted effects

The table summarises the argument. Its entries are analytic predictions drawn from the literature, not measured outcomes.

| Theology type | Frame of collective suffering | Predicted effect on faith | Predicted effect on institutional trust | Historical example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomistic / retributive-penitential | Punishment for collective sin; remedy is repentance through authorised mediators | Up at first; down if repentance visibly fails | Up at first (clergy as brokers); sharply down when intercession fails or laity seize penance | Gregory the Great's litany, 590; Black Death sermons; flagellants condemned 1349 (Horrox 1994; Clement VI 1349) |
| Purgatorial / intercessory | Mass death creates souls needing suffrages | Up: investment in the dead | Dependence on clergy rises, with resentment of the "sale" of grace | Post-1348 intercession economy (Le Goff 1984) |
| Providential-reformist | Judgment and fatherly correction; remedy is moral reform and vocation | Up: providence gives meaning | Shifts to preachers and magistrates; judged by service | Luther 1527; Zwingli 1519; Walsham 1999 |
| Eschatological / martyrial | Test and release to immortality; nursing equals martyrdom | Strongly up | Up for a caring minority church; down for rivals | Cyprian; Dionysius; Stark 1996 |
| Suffering-Christ devotion | God grieves and bleeds with us; mercy, not wrath | Up: affective faith | Neutral to down: less need for mediation | Julian; Ross 1997; Bynum 2007 |
| Exodus / liberation | God sides with the oppressed; suffering is injustice | Up: hope and agency | Down for the oppressor's church; up for independent churches | Douglass 1845; Raboteau 2004; Ateek 1989 |
| Crucified God / crucified people | God suffers in and with the victims | Up among sufferers | Down for churches allied with power; up for martyr-church communities | Moltmann 1974; Sobrino 2008; Cone 2011; Song 1990 |
| Lament / protest | Suffering voiced to God; God accountable to covenant | Sustained through protest | Up where lament is permitted; down where suppressed | Brueggemann 1986; spirituals; Katongole 2017 |
| *Han* / minjung / Dalit / homeland | Accumulated wound of the sinned-against; people as historical subject | Up: dignity and identity | Down for state-aligned churches; up for churches with the people | Park 1993; CTC-CCA 1983; Nirmal 1990; PCT 1971–77 |
| Anti-theodicy / practical | Refuses to justify suffering; responds with presence | Protected from the scandal of justification | Up for institutions that accompany; down for those that explain | Tilley 1991; Hauerwas 1990; Hart 2005 |
| Humanist / womanist critique | Suffering is not redemptive | Mixed: rejection (Jones, Pinn) or transformed faith (Williams) | Down for churches preaching redemptive suffering to the oppressed | Jones 1973; Pinn 1995; Williams 1993 |
| Post-Holocaust | Catastrophe breaks the God of history, or demands renewed fidelity | Polarised: rejection or defiant recommitment | Down for churches implicated in anti-Judaism | Rubenstein 1966; Fackenheim 1970; Greenberg 1977 |

The central contrast is visible in the first and seventh rows. Retributive frames tie the institution's credibility to the outcome of the catastrophe. If repentance "works," the mediating church gains; if plague returns, it is indicted. Solidarity frames tie God's credibility to God's presence with the sufferer, which no outcome can falsify. They can therefore sustain faith through defeat while shifting legitimacy away from churches seen as allied with the cause of suffering.

This contrast clarifies the two core cases. In 1347–51 the mediating church was *the* church, so its penitential monopoly was both strengthened and strained. Under American slavery the oppressor's church and the sufferers' church were different institutions. Exodus and the crucified Christ allowed faith to rise alongside distrust of the slaveholders' church and trust in the invisible institution and, later, independent Black churches. The typology alone cannot say which frame a community adopts. That depends on the variables in the working model: power position and the church's role.

### A reading path for further theological study

The path below is built from works verified in the notes. Seminary use is inferred from genre and reputation, not from a survey of syllabi.

- **Introductory.** Migliore's chapter "The Providence of God and the Mystery of Evil" (Migliore 2014). Then Wright (2006) or Carson (2006), depending on tradition. Then Brueggemann's lament essay (1986), Swinton (2007) and Hart (2005). Read Cyprian's *De mortalitate* and Luther's plague letter (1968) as primary texts on epidemic.
- **Intermediate.** Peterson's anthology (2016) and Tooley's encyclopedia entry (Tooley 2002) for the philosophical map. Newsom (2003) and Gutiérrez (1987) on Job, read together. Moltmann (1974) and Sölle (1975), read together. Then Cone (2011), Song (1990), Katongole (2017) and Hauerwas (1990).
- **Advanced.** Surin (1986) and Tilley (1991) on anti-theodicy. Adams (1999) and Stump (2010) for analytic responses. Metz (2007) for political theology. Jones (1973), Pinn (1995), Williams (1993) and Copeland (2010) on redemptive suffering. Bynum (2007) and Ross (1997) on medieval suffering piety. Walsham (1999) on providentialism. Kitamori (1965) and Park (1993) for Asian traditions of divine pain and *han*.

For a Taiwan-based reader, two lines connect this research to coursework. The first runs from Westermann through Brueggemann to Swinton and Katongole on lament. The second pairs C. S. Song with Moltmann and Sobrino in Christology.

### Implications for the model

- **Supports the model's second variable.** Interpretation matters: retributive frames bind institutional credibility to outcomes, while solidarity frames bind God's credibility to presence. The same catastrophe can therefore raise faith and lower institutional trust.
- **Refines the retributive branch.** Retribution initially empowers the mediating church; erosion follows when intercession visibly fails over repeated waves. This supports treating recurrence and duration as a separate variable, as the model's open problems suggest.
- **Delegitimation is selective.** Douglass, Cone, Tinker and the PCT statements show trust collapsing in a particular church while shifting to a sufferer-controlled body. "Institutional trust" should be measured per institution, not in aggregate.
- **The loss-of-faith branch has its own theology.** Jones, Pinn and Williams show that rejecting redemptive suffering can lead to humanism or to transformed faith. Theological critique, not only church complicity, can drive exit.
- **Theology is partly endogenous.** Which canonical voice dominates depends on who controls interpretation. Theological frame and power position are therefore not fully independent variables.
- **Frames are positional, not ethnic.** Contextual theologies from Taiwan, Korea, India, Palestine and Latin America reproduce the same structure. This supports the model's claim that situation, not race, explains the pattern.
