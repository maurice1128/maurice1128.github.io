## 7. Expanded cases across peoples and kinds of suffering

Across twenty-three cases from four continents, the church's structural position relative to sufferers and perpetrators predicts the outcome better than the kind of suffering or the sufferers' ethnicity. Where the church was the sufferers' own, devotion and institutional loyalty rose together. Where it failed them or sided with their oppressors, devotion usually survived but migrated to sufferer-controlled bodies. Outright rejection appears only where Christianity was the perpetrator's religion and a rival sacred system was still alive.

This section adds cases that section 6 does not treat. It covers plague, genocide, atomic war, colonial removal, caste, famine and state terror. Codings use the four variables of the working model; outcomes marked "unsourced" rest on general knowledge and need evidence before publication.

### Pattern A: the sufferers' own church

**Plague of Cyprian.** Stark (1996) argued that Christian nursing during the Antonine (c. 165) and Cyprianic (c. 249–270) epidemics raised survival rates, drew converts and supplied a theodicy pagans lacked. Specialists object to his uncritical use of sources and speculative arithmetic (Castelli 1998); Avalos (1999) recasts the argument as a health-care system but reviewers doubt it was a primary growth driver. Harper (2015, 2016, 2017) re-diagnoses the pathogen as a probable viral haemorrhagic fever. Huebner (2021) moves its entry to the Danube frontier and argues it cannot be tied to the Decian sacrifice edict of 249. Orsag, McKinney and Reeder (2023) add an interdisciplinary reassessment. For the model, the case matters because the church held no state power: it was a minority mutual-aid community, and the pagan civic cult was the "failing establishment." The direction fits pattern A; the size of the effect is unproven.

**Japan: Hidden Christians and Urakami.** After the 1614 ban, Kirishitan communities preserved their faith for some 250 years without clergy, around martyrdom, contrition and ancestral fidelity (Whelan 1996; Turnbull 1998; Higashibaba 2001). When toleration came in 1873, part of the community rejoined Rome, but the Kakure kept a lay tradition. Miyazaki (2014, 2018) reads that tradition as Japanese folk religion rather than Christianity. Faith was preserved; trust in the *returning* institution was conditional, because the community had become its own church.

The atomic bomb of 9 August 1945 fell on Urakami, the descendants of these communities. Nagai Takashi's *Nagasaki no kane* 長崎の鐘 (1949; trans. 1984) framed the dead as a burnt offering (*hansai* 燔祭) through which God granted peace. Diehl (2018) treats Nagai as the single most influential voice in reconstruction. Takahashi Shinji 高橋眞司 named and attacked the "Urakami hansai theory" (浦上燔祭説) for absolving the Emperor and the United States (Takahashi 2024). McClelland (2019), from oral histories of Catholic hibakusha, finds survivors "angry rather than passive," and Shijo (2015) traces the theory's reception. Nagasaki shows that an own-church theodicy can intensify faith in the short run and later lose credibility, without the institution itself being blamed.

**State perpetrators, church as co-victim or opponent.** The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church was forcibly absorbed into Moscow Orthodoxy in 1946 and survived underground until 1989 (Bociurkiw 1996). Its re-emergence as a mass church is widely reported but was not sourced here. The Holodomor (1932–33) coincided with the liquidation of the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox Church, but Soviet atheism leaves no clean measure of faith outcomes. In Vietnam, northern Catholics fled south in 1954 for varied reasons and were "not duped by CIA propaganda"; their clergy organised the exodus (Hansen 2009a, 2009b; Keith 2012). In the Philippines, repression pushed Catholic and Protestant churches from social action to liberation theology and open opposition to Marcos (Youngblood 1990). Indonesia 1965–66 is the most striking addition. Survivors and suspected leftists joined churches in large numbers; Willis (1977), a missionary source, reports over two million Javanese converts in 1966–71. Hearman (2018) shows from ex-detainee interviews that conversion was not solely compliance with the state's demand to hold a religion: detainees valued worship and pastoral relationships. The Uganda martyrs (1885–87) fit the same pattern (Faupel 1962), though the outcome coding is unsourced.

### Taiwan in depth

Taiwan offers the closest thing to a natural experiment in the review. The same Reformed tradition held different positions toward different suffering populations in one polity and one era.

**The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT), 228 and the White Terror.** The PCT (台灣基督長老教會 *Taiwan Jidu Zhanglao Jiaohui*) worshipped in Taiwanese, Hakka and Indigenous languages. It was a non-ruling church under a Mainlander-dominated party-state. Rubinstein (1991) places it at the "indigenous" end of Taiwan's Protestant spectrum and later calls it the "self-anointed conscience" of the Republic of China and an advocate of Minnan and Hakka selfhood (Rubinstein 2003). Members were among White Terror victims, yet membership reportedly more than doubled in the 1960s (*Religio* 2023 *[verify]*).

The church's turn came through three public statements: the 1971 "Statement on Our National Fate" (對國是的聲明與建議 *Dui guoshi de shengming yu jianyi* *[verify title]*), the 1975 "Our Appeal" (我們的呼籲 *Women de huyu* *[verify title]*), and the 1977 "Declaration on Human Rights" (人權宣言 *Renquan xuanyan*). The last cited the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and called for Taiwan to become "a new and independent country" (PCT 1977). General Secretary Kao Chun-ming 高俊明 was imprisoned after the 1979 Kaohsiung (Formosa) Incident. In 1987 the church weekly *Taiwan Church News* (台灣教會公報 *Tâi-oân Kàu-hōe Kong-pò*) broke the public silence on 228, and the Garrison Command confiscated the issue (Worthy News n.d. *[verify]*; Taipei Times 2025 *[verify]*). The church issued a 228 pastoral letter in 1989 and an apology to victims' families in 1990.

Suffering was turned into doctrine. Wang Hsien-chih 王憲治 proposed "homeland theology" (鄉土神學 *xiangtu shenxue*): Taiwan as a homeland given by God (Wang 1988 *[verify]*). Huang Po Ho 黃伯和 developed Chhut-thau-thiⁿ 出頭天 theology, "rising out from under oppression," from Shoki Coe's 黃彰輝 contextualisation (Huang 1996). Rychetská (2022) shows the PCT explained its human-rights activism through Christian belief from the late 1960s onward. Zavala-Pelayo (2022) traces the genealogy of its self-determination claims. Cheng Yang-en 鄭仰恩 (2005 *[verify year]*) provides the Taiwanese church-history base.

The contrast group is under-documented. One former seminary teacher reports that Presbyterians read their treatment as persecution, while many evangelicals saw it as punishment for political activity rather than faith (Worthy News n.d. *[verify]*). That is a single informant. No study compares PCT and Mainland-affiliated church membership across 1947–1987; PCT General Assembly yearbooks (總會年鑑 *Zonghui nianjian*) would supply the data.

**Indigenous mass conversion, 1945–c. 1960.** Japanese colonial policy suppressed Christianity among Indigenous peoples; the Truku evangelist Chi Oang 芝苑 kept it alive underground (sources thin). After 1945, whole villages across all ten then-recognised peoples converted. Catholic Bishop André Verineux of Hualien called it "a unique miracle"; Taiwanese writers call it "the miracle of the twentieth century" (二十世紀的神蹟 *ershi shiji de shenji*) (Fan Chiang 2019; Vicedom 1967 *[verify]*). Provincial surveys put Christians at 85.13 percent of mountain Indigenous people in 1985 and 83.94 percent in 1991; lowland figures fell from 70.70 to 61.43 percent (Fan Chiang 2019). Indigenous congregations now make up nearly half of PCT churches. The PCT opened its Mountain Mission Office (山地宣道處 *Shandi xuandao chu*) in 1953; church and state both diverged and cooperated, with the state using churches to spread policy (Chou 2015).

### Pattern B: failing or perpetrator church, faith migrates

**Rwanda 1994.** Rwanda is the clearest modern case of church as establishment and perpetrator. Linden (1977) traces the Catholic Church's role in the 1959 "Hutu revolution." Longman (2010) argues the churches "consistently allied themselves with the state and played ethnic politics," giving them substantial responsibility for the genocide. Rittner, Roth and Whitworth (2004) reach mostly critical conclusions about national church leaderships. Tutsi victims were members of the same churches whose hierarchies were aligned with Hutu Power.

Devotion did not collapse; it moved. Pentecostalism had no real foothold before 1994. About 150 new Christian communities existed by 1997 and over 300 by 2000 (van 't Spijker n.d. *[verify]*). Kubai (2007a) links their spread to needs for healing, a rebuilt social fabric and a post-ethnic identity, though she finds Catholicism still dominant. The Catholic share fell from about 63 percent in the mid-1990s (Kubai 2007a) to roughly 50 percent in a later Pew estimate (Foreign Policy 2014 *[verify]*); the methods differ, so the drop is approximate. A seminary professor reported that many Rwandans felt "betrayed" by the church (Christianity Today 2011 *[verify]*).

A smaller stream turned to Islam, citing church complicity and gratitude to Muslims who sheltered victims (Kubai 2007b). Some conversions were for protection or support, and they tapered after 1997. A 2002 press figure of 14 percent Muslim is widely considered inflated; the 2022 census reports about 2 percent *[verify against NISR tables]*. Tiemessen (2005 *[verify]*) is the most cited study of these conversions. Theological work on suffering has begun (Griener 2023). No survey measures survivors' personal faith as distinct from affiliation.

**African Independent Churches.** Aladura arose among Yoruba Anglicans during the 1918–19 influenza and the crises of the 1920s, when mission churches would not practise prayer-healing (Turner 1967; Peel 1968). Ohadike (1991) estimates about 500,000 Nigerian deaths, a figure later questioned. Simon Kimbangu's 1921 healing ministry in the Belgian Congo produced a church that state and missions repressed; it is now one of Africa's largest (Martin 1975; Mokoko Gampiot 2017). South African Zionists built churches of healing and purity among dispossessed workers (Sundkler 1948); Comaroff (1985) reads them as resistance that "does not amount to submission." In each case faith rose, mission trust fell and African-controlled churches grew. "Own community" here means institutional control, not race.

**Dalit mass movements.** Dalits converted as communities, under caste oppression and famine, from the 1860s (Pickett 1933; Webster 1992). Bauman (2008) shows a distinct Satnami-Christian identity emerging. Forrester (1980) and Mosse (2012) document caste persisting inside churches. Viswanath (2014) argues that missionaries framed Pariah suffering as a religious problem, obscuring an agrarian economy built on Pariah labour. Clarke (1998) and Dalit theology are the response. The case shows a liberating outsider church turning into a partial reproducer of oppression: pattern A decaying into pattern B within one institution.

**Canada and Australia.** Churches co-administered state policies of child removal (Miller 1996; Milloy 1999; TRC 2015; HREOC 1997). Australian church responses showed "an overwhelming tendency toward apology," shaped by mostly non-Indigenous memberships (HREOC 1998). Canada's 2021 census found 47 percent of Indigenous people reporting no religion and 26.9 percent Catholic, with 81,000 Canadians reporting Indigenous spirituality (Catholic Register 2022 *[verify]*). Devotion bifurcated: disaffiliation and traditional revival on one side, persistent Christian identity on the other. At Galiwin'ku (Elcho Island) in 1979, Yolŋu minister Djiniyini Gondarra led a revival that raised active attenders from about 25 to about 200 within weeks (Blacket 1994). Within the same denomination, shifting control to the sufferers moved the outcome from B to A.

**Hawai'i and Samoa.** Archer (2018) argues that epidemic depopulation bred fatalism that made some Kānaka Maoli more receptive to Christianity. Through the ali'i the mission became an establishment church (Thigpen 2014) and its descendants later backed annexation, which Native Hawaiians resisted (Silva 2004). In Western Samoa, New Zealand's failure to quarantine killed 7,542 people by the end of 1918 (Tomkins 1992). Blame fell on the colonial state, not the Samoan-led church; religious outcomes were not retrieved.

### Pattern C: rejection and re-coding

The Taki Onqoy ("dancing sickness," c. 1564–80) is the clearest rejection case. Its preachers taught that the huacas were punishing Andeans with disease for accepting the Christian god and called for a return to huaca worship (Stern 1982; Millones 1990). It spread from Huamanga to Cusco and La Paz and faded by about 1580. In Mexico, by contrast, Nahuas adopted Christianity after catastrophic epidemics but re-coded it in Nahua moral categories (Ricard 1966; Burkhart 1989). Friars read disease as punishment for idolatry; Andean prophets read it as punishment for apostasy. The same suffering supported opposite theodicies, and institutional power decided which prevailed. Partial rejection also appears in Canada's Indigenous "no religion" share and among Rwanda's converts to Islam.

### Which cases most strengthen the series

1. **Taiwan.** The PCT, the KMT-aligned churches and the Indigenous churches hold religion, era and state roughly constant while varying the church's situation. Chinese-language scholarship (黃應貴, 邱韻芳, 周沛忻, 范姜曉涵, 鄭仰恩) gives a Taiwan-based author a local base and an original contribution: a membership comparison across 1947–1987.
2. **Rwanda.** Paired with the enslaved African American case, it tests "situation, not race" cleanly. Both populations were African-descended, subordinate and mission-Christianised. Where the church was the sufferers' own, faith and institution rose together; where it sided with the killers, faith migrated.
3. **Aladura or the Canadian schools.** Aladura shows the migration mechanism under a natural disaster comparable to the Black Death. Canada supplies census-level disaffiliation where the church was perpetrator. The Taki Onqoy serves as a short limit case.

### Implications for the model

- Rwanda and the Taiwan PCT both involve subordinate sufferers but opposite church roles, so together they help separate power position from church role, the confound in the US slave case.
- Theodicy follows institutional credibility. Martyrdom, sacrifice and Exodus frames work when the institution delivering them is trusted; Nagasaki shows even an own-church theodicy can later be contested.
- Church role can change within one institution over time (Dalits, Elcho Island), so codings should be time-indexed.
- Mass conversion often follows the lifting of constraint (Taiwan after 1945, Indonesia after 1965), echoing Fountain (2010); timing deserves a separate hypothesis.
- Rejection requires a living alternative (huacas, Indigenous spirituality, Islam in Rwanda); without one, devotion migrates rather than disappears.
- Outcome evidence is mostly affiliation, not belief. Few cases measure personal faith, a gap the series should acknowledge.
