## 11. Methodological debates: comparing distant cases without a race variable

A Black Death–slavery comparison is defensible if causal weight rests on within-case process tracing, not on a two-case Millian contrast. It must also define its variables independently of either case and unbundle "race" into situational components that can be observed in both. The method literature supplies warrant for each step. It also supplies five serious objections that the article must answer in its own methods section: proximity, small N, incommensurability, entanglement, and the bracketing of race.

### 11.1 Logics of comparison: why distance is not the problem

Marc Bloch set the terms of the debate. He distinguished comparison of distant societies, as in Frazer's universal anthropology, from comparison of neighbouring, contemporaneous societies, and he preferred the latter for historians (Bloch 1928). A reviewer can turn this preference against a fourteenth-century/nineteenth-century pairing. Sewell's reading of Bloch is the standard reply. Comparison is a logic for testing explanatory hypotheses by checking whether a supposed cause co-varies with its effect. Its usefulness depends on the problem posed, not on the distance between cases (Sewell 1967).

Skocpol and Somers (1980) identified three logics: parallel demonstration of theory, contrast of contexts, and macro-causal analysis on Mill's methods. Tilly (1984) distinguished individualizing, universalizing, variation-finding, and encompassing comparison. The proposed article is best framed as contrast of contexts plus a modest variation-finding claim. One principle, the situational configuration of the sufferers, should explain why faith and institutional trust diverge in some settings and move together in others. Mahoney and Rueschemeyer (2003) and Mahoney (2004) show that comparative-historical analysis now rests on explicit causal mechanisms and temporal process, not on co-variation across a handful of units.

The transnational turn raised a different objection: cases are not independent units. Kocka (2003) argued that comparison abstracts cases from their contexts, which is both its strength and its cost. He recommended combining comparison with the study of entanglements. Werner and Zimmermann (2006) went further: their *histoire croisée* treats the observer, the cases, and the categories as mutually constituted. Seigel (2005) and Stoler (2001) added that choosing comparative units is itself political, a point that bears directly on choosing "race" or "situation" as the unit of analysis.

|---|---|---|---|
| Proximity preference | Bloch 1928 | Compare neighbouring, contemporaneous societies; distant comparison is speculative | Must be answered explicitly; it is the first objection a historian will raise |

The entanglement critique is less damaging here than it first appears. The same scriptural repertoire, including Exodus, Job, Lamentations, and providential punishment, runs from late-medieval preaching through Reformation Protestantism into Anglo-American evangelicalism. "Theological interpretation" is therefore a genuinely shared variable, transmitted rather than coincidental. That supports the model's second variable, but it also means the two cases are not fully independent tests of it.

### 11.2 Small-N critiques and the turn to process tracing

Lieberson (1991) supplied the sharpest critique. Mill's methods applied to a few cases assume deterministic causation, no interaction effects, no measurement error, and a complete list of causes. A two-case design meets none of these assumptions. Mahoney (2000) answered by combining nominal, ordinal, and within-case strategies, so that cross-case contrast is only one leg of inference. Steinmetz (2004) engaged Lieberson from the other side, arguing that "odious comparisons" fail not for want of cases but because cases are embedded in incommensurable wholes.

Process tracing is now the standard response. Collier (2011) and Mahoney (2012) set out hoop, smoking-gun, and doubly decisive tests. Bennett and Checkel (2015) and Beach and Pedersen (2013; 2019) turned the method into explicit procedures for tracing causal mechanisms. George and Bennett (2005) linked it to theory development across small sets of cases. Applied here, process tracing asks within each case whether the sequence the model predicts actually occurred. For example: did clerical absence precede lay complaint, and did lay complaint precede the shift of devotion to lay-controlled bodies?

Case selection is the second front. Gerring (2004) defined the case study as intensive study of a unit meant to illuminate a larger class. Seawright and Gerring (2008) offered a menu of typical, diverse, extreme, deviant, influential, most-similar, and most-different cases. Przeworski and Teune (1970) originated the distinction between most-similar and most-different systems designs. Slater and Ziblatt (2013) defended controlled comparison as indispensable even after the process-tracing turn.

The two core cases are a most-different pair. They differ in era, continent, economy, denomination, and type of catastrophe, yet both show intensified devotion alongside strained relations with an establishment church. Read as a most-different design, the pair points to a common factor: low sufferer power, an establishment perceived as absent or allied with power, and available lay-controlled religious structures. But a most-different design with a shared outcome is Mill's method of agreement, the variant most exposed to Lieberson. It also risks selecting on the dependent variable, which Geddes (1990) showed can manufacture spurious relationships.

The remedy is a nested design. Across cases, the pair is most-different. Within each case, sub-populations form most-similar comparisons that vary the explanatory variables while holding era and region constant. Jews and Christians in 1348–51, Black-controlled and white-controlled congregations in the American South, and Brazilian brotherhood members and non-members are such pairs. Ragin's configurational logic (Ragin 2014, first published 1987) fits the model's claim that combinations of variables, not single variables, produce the outcome.

### 11.3 Comparison in the study of religion

Comparative religion has its own methodological canon, and reviewers in religious studies will expect it. J. Z. Smith criticized comparison driven by "similarity and contiguity" as a form of magical association (Smith 1982). He called for comparison disciplined by theory and by the scholar's stated interest. In *Drudgery Divine* he described comparison as triadic: x resembles y more than z with respect to some stated feature (Smith 1990). Comparison is thus a scholarly construction, not the discovery of essential sameness.

Patton and Ray (2000) gathered responses to Smith that tried to bridge postmodern rejection and defence of comparison. Paden's "new comparativism" in that volume stressed comparison at different scales and for different purposes (Paden 2000). Segal (2001) defended the comparative method against its postmodern critics. Freiberger (2018; 2019) now offers the most usable procedure. Comparison is set by goals, modes, scales, and scope, and it proceeds through selection, description, juxtaposition, redescription, and rectification. Freiberger (2020) and Yelle (2020) continue the debate on how far comparison must be "disciplined."

Applied to this project, Smith's formula forces a precise *tertium comparationis*. A defensible version would read: late-medieval Latin Christianity under plague resembles enslaved African American Christianity more than it resembles, say, mainline Protestantism after the 1918 influenza, with respect to high collective mortality or domination, low sufferer power, and an establishment clergy perceived as absent or allied with power. In Freiberger's terms the study is explanatory, cross-cultural, diachronic, and meso-scale. His "redescription and rectification" step is where the article can show that "race" redescribes the cases poorly while "power position" and "church role" redescribe both well.

### 11.4 Why race is not the explanatory variable

A broad consensus across history, sociology, and causal-inference methodology holds that "race" is not a well-defined causal variable. Holland's dictum of "no causation without manipulation" excludes race-as-attribute from potential-outcomes causation (Holland 1986). Sen and Wasow (2016) proposed treating race as a "bundle of sticks": separable, manipulable components such as signals, place, religion, and status. Greiner and Rubin (2011) shifted the treatment to *perceived* race at a defined moment. VanderWeele and Robinson (2014) showed how adjusting for mediators changes what a "race effect" means.

Historians and sociologists reach the same point by another route. Fields (1990) argued that racial ideology in the United States followed from slavery as an institution of labour exploitation rather than causing it. Fields and Fields (2012) extended this into the concept of "racecraft." Zuberi (2001) traced the joint origins of the race idea and social statistics and argued that statistical analysis must be deracialized. Zuberi and Bonilla-Silva (2008) and Omi and Winant (2015) treat race as historically produced through racial projects, not as a fixed attribute.

A specific warning comes from the history of religion. Evans (2008) showed that the belief in Black people's "natural religiosity" was itself a racial trope used to justify inequality. Any claim that enslaved people "turned to God" because of who they were would reproduce that trope. Coding situation rather than race protects the article against it.

There are serious counter-positions. Kohler-Hausmann (2019) *[verify]* argues that race cannot be split into sticks without losing what it is, because race is constituted by the social meanings the sticks carry. Recent philosophy of science continues this argument over variable selection and causal claims about race (Tolbert 2024; Malinsky and Bright 2026). Black theology makes race constitutive of the theodicy question itself (Jones [1973] 1998). The article should concede that race is context and part of how power position was produced in the American case. Its claim is narrower: race does not explain the *variation* the model targets, since the same configuration appears among non-racialized sufferers.

Two classic comparisons supply templates. Kolchin (1987) compared American slavery with Russian serfdom, in which the unfree were not racially distinguished from their owners. Patterson (1982) compared slavery across dozens of societies and defined it by domination, natal alienation, and dishonour rather than by race. Both show that the structure of domination can be the comparative unit.

### 11.5 Medieval racialization and the Jews of 1348–51

The "no race" design must not imply that the medieval case was race-free. Heng (2011; 2018) argues that race-making existed in medieval Europe and targeted Jews, Muslims, Romani, Mongols, and Black Africans. Hahn (2001) introduced the founding special issue of the *Journal of Medieval and Early Modern Studies* on medieval race. Bartlett (2001) countered that medieval *gens* and *natio* language was largely cultural, though with descent-based elements. Whitaker (2019; 2025) traces modern racism to medieval race-thinking and narrates the rise of premodern critical race studies.

The plague massacres of Jews are central to this literature. Cohn (2007) placed the 1348–51 burnings within late-medieval revolt and found elites, town councils, and princes often involved. Barzilay (2022) traced well-poisoning accusations from 1321 and found municipal councils promoting them, partly for material gain. Nirenberg (1996) read the violence as contingent and locally structured rather than as timeless hatred. Jedwab, Johnson, and Koyama (2019) and Voigtländer and Voth (2012) measure persecution's links to plague mortality and its long-run persistence.

The design implication is to code Jews' position directly rather than through a race label. Inside the plague case, Jews form a third, within-period contrast: very low power, a non-Christian theology of suffering, and Christian authorities as complicit persecutors or unreliable protectors. The model then predicts consolidation of faith within Jews' own communal institutions, not attraction to the dominant church. Foa (2000) warns against reading Jewish history after 1348 only as catastrophe, which cautions against coding Jews solely as victims. This shadow case tests whether the mechanism generalizes beyond Christianity; it does not test the Christian outcome directly.

### 11.6 Dating and recurrence: a four-year shock against a 250-year regime

The two cases differ sharply in temporal shape. The first plague wave struck Western Europe in roughly 1347–51 and returned repeatedly from the 1360s. North American slavery lasted from the early seventeenth century to 1865. Comparing "the Black Death" with "slavery" as wholes compares an event with a regime. Kreuzer (2023) argues that comparative-historical analysis must make such temporal properties explicit rather than treat time as background.

The evidence suggests that the decisive religious changes in both cases came late. Cohn's testamentary data from six central Italian cities place the main shift in pious giving after the 1363 recurrence, not in 1348 (Cohn 1992). Robinson (2019) found English ordinations surging in 1349–50 and then falling below half the pre-plague level for the rest of the 1350s. On the slavery side, mass conversion came with evangelical revivals after about 1760 (Frey and Wood 1998). Fountain (2010) found that fewer than 40 percent of WPA narrators with datable conversions had converted before freedom.

Fountain's finding is a direct challenge to the working model. If mass Christian commitment followed emancipation, then collective *deliverance*, not collective suffering, drove institutional growth. Harper (2016) reads emancipation through the eschatological expectations of freedpeople, which supports that reading. The WPA narrators, however, were mostly children under slavery when interviewed in the 1930s, which biases the sample toward late conversions. The question is open.

The practical solution is to compare episodes, not wholes. Each case can be divided into phases indexed by generation since onset: the first wave and its recurrences to about 1400; and in the American case, pre-1760, the revival era to 1830, 1830–65, and 1865–1900. The model then makes phase-specific predictions. Recurrence matters because it converts a shock into a regime and turns institutional performance into a repeated test.

### 11.7 Measuring "faith" against "institutional trust" in historical sources

Neither faith nor institutional trust is directly observable in historical sources. Both must be inferred from behaviour, and most behaviours load on both. A pious bequest for perpetual masses, for instance, expresses devotion and also dependence on clergy. The two constructs separate only where sources record a *choice between* establishment and alternative channels. Gorski (2000) adds a baseline warning: there was no medieval "age of faith," so a decline must be measured against a realistic pre-crisis level.

| Proxy | Case | What it measures | Main bias or limit | Key sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pious bequests in wills | Plague | Investment in institution-mediated intercession | Propertied testators; conflates faith and institutional dependence | Cohn 1992; Chiffoleau 1980; Wray 2009 |
| Confraternity membership and statutes | Plague; Brazil | Lay-controlled devotion inside the church | Survival of statutes uneven; membership lists rare | Henderson 1994; Chiffoleau 1979; Russell-Wood 1974; Soares 2011; Kiddy 2005; Reginaldo 2011 |
| Ordinations | Plague | Clerical supply and vocations | Reflects career prospects as much as piety | Robinson 2019 |
| Church membership counts | US South | Formal institutional affiliation | Recorded by white-led churches; masters' permission shaped access | Frey and Wood 1998; Finke and Stark 1986 |
| Separations into independent churches | US South, after 1865 | Faith retained, establishment trust withdrawn | Visible only once separation was legally possible | Montgomery 1993; Walker 1982 |
| Conversion narratives | US South | Timing and content of personal commitment | 1930s interviews of former child slaves; interviewer effects | Fountain 2010 |
| Anticlerical or heterodox lay movements | Plague | Lay piety bypassing clerical mediation | Episodic; sources mostly hostile | Kieckhefer 1974 |
| Collective defence of church practice | Brazil | Trust in lay-controlled church structures | Single events; urban bias | Reis 2003 |

Two indicators per case are needed: one for devotional intensity regardless of channel, and one for the allocation of devotion between establishment and sufferer-controlled bodies. Post-1865 separations are the cleanest measure of the model's central outcome, faith up with establishment trust down. Reis's 1836 Cemiterada, in which Bahians rioted to defend church burial, is the clearest case of faith and trust in one's own church structures rising together.

The plague evidence is more ambiguous than the working model assumes. Much post-plague intensification ran *through* the institution, which grew busier and richer in intercessory services (Cohn 1992; Henderson 1994). Duffy (1992) found late-medieval English religion vigorous up to the Reformation. Stearns (2009) warns against coding any community's plague response as monolithic. The plague case may therefore show faith up with trust *redistributed* to lay-run bodies inside the church, rather than eroded.

### 11.8 The five strongest arguments for comparability

1. **Comparison is a logic of hypothesis-testing, not of proximity.** Bloch's preference for neighbouring societies was pragmatic. Sewell showed that comparison tests explanations wherever the relevant variables vary, and Skocpol and Somers's contrast of contexts and Tilly's variation-finding accommodate distant cases (Sewell 1967; Skocpol and Somers 1980; Tilly 1984; Mahoney 2004).
2. **Cases are matched on theorized variables, not on era or race.** Power position, theological interpretation, and church role are defined independently of either case. "Race" is unbundled into observable components, following Sen and Wasow (2016), Fields (1990), and Zuberi (2001). Kolchin (1987) and Patterson (1982) are templates for comparing American slavery with non-racial hierarchies.
3. **Causal claims rest on within-case process tracing.** Each case gets its own predicted sequence, tested with hoop and smoking-gun evidence. That answers Lieberson (1991) by not relying on two-case elimination (Mahoney 2000; 2012; Bennett and Checkel 2015; Beach and Pedersen 2013).
4. **Entanglement makes theology a shared variable.** The same scriptural theodicy repertoire circulates from Latin Christendom into Protestant evangelicalism. Histoire croisée and Smith's triadic comparison turn the link into an explicit, theorized basis for comparison (Werner and Zimmermann 2006; Kocka 2003; Smith 1990; Freiberger 2019).
5. **Long-durée precedents are accepted when variables and mechanisms are explicit.** Voigtländer and Voth (2012) link Black Death pogroms to Nazi-era violence. Cohn compares epidemics from Athens to AIDS (Cohn 2012; 2018). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011) link the slave trade to later mistrust; Bentzen (2019) links disasters to religiosity; Gorski (2000) writes long-period historical sociology of religion.

### 11.10 Design recommendation for the core article

The core article should pair two cases and use several shadow cases. Core case one is Latin Christendom under plague, about 1348–1400, with Tuscany and England as focal regions where wills, confraternity records, and ordination lists survive. Core case two is enslaved and newly freed African Americans in the US South, about 1760–1900, spanning the revivals, the late antebellum decades, and post-emancipation separation. The time window must include emancipation so that the deliverance hypothesis can be tested.

Shadow cases serve as variable-separating checks rather than full cases. The Jews of 1348–51 test the power-position variable outside Christianity. Brazil is the key variable-separating case. Its establishment church was itself a large slaveholder (Schwartz 1982), yet enslaved and freed Africans held their own chartered brotherhoods (Russell-Wood 1974; Soares 2011; Reginaldo 2011). The model predicts faith up and trust in one's own church structures up, despite establishment complicity. Reis's Cemiterada supports that prediction; the Malê uprising and Inquisition prosecution of African healing show the rejection branch where alternatives existed (Reis 1993; Sweet 2003). Stark's account of early Christian growth through epidemic care is a further shadow case for the "own church" pathway (Stark 1996).

Within each core case, process tracing should test a specified sequence with named evidence. A hoop test: if the model holds, devotion should shift to lay-controlled bodies only *after* evidence of establishment absence or complicity. A smoking-gun test: explicit contemporary statements linking clerical failure or masters' religion to a choice of alternative channel. The cross-case comparison then becomes an argument about whether the same configuration produces the same sequence in very different settings.

### Implications for the model

- The working model survives the comparability objections only if it is presented as a configurational, mechanism-based claim tested within cases, not as a two-case correlation.
- "Race is not a variable" should be restated as "race is unbundled": race is context and part of how power position was produced, while the explanatory variables are situational.
- The outcome variable needs refinement. The plague case points to trust *redistributed* to lay-controlled bodies inside the church, not simply eroded; the model should distinguish redistribution from exit.
- Duration is a fourth variable, not a nuisance. Recurrence (1363) and regime length turn institutional performance into a repeated test, and the model should predict phase-specific outcomes.
- Fountain's post-emancipation timing makes deliverance a rival hypothesis that the core article must test, not dismiss.
- Brazil and the Jews of 1348–51 should enter the design explicitly as variable-separating shadow cases.
