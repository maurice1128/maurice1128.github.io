## 8. Continental European scholarship

Continental scholarship mostly finds that plague-era suffering intensified religious investment; what varied was which body captured that investment. The candidates were the parish clergy, lay confraternities, communes, or movements the hierarchy condemned. Erosion of *faith* is rarely argued in these traditions. Erosion of trust in *clergy*, and the transfer of devotion to lay-run bodies, is argued often. This supports the working model's middle outcome (faith up, establishment trust down, faith migrating to sufferer-controlled bodies). It also shows that the outcome depended heavily on local power arrangements.

Most of these works have no English translation. English-language articles therefore often cite them through intermediaries. A note on evidence: summaries marked **[GK]** rest on general knowledge of the historiography, not on a reading of the texts for this review. Bibliographic details were checked against the DNB, BnF, Crossref and Open Library catalogues (see the reference list).

### German-language scholarship: crisis, catastrophe and the politics of the pogroms

**The crisis concept.** František Graus's *Pest – Geissler – Judenmorde* (1987) is the anchor text. Its subtitle frames the fourteenth century as a *Krisenzeit* (age of crisis). Graus treats plague, the flagellant processions and the murder of Jews as linked responses. He also questions whether "crisis" is a usable analytic category at all, rather than a label historians impose afterwards [GK]. The book has never been translated into English; a Czech translation appeared in 2020 (Graus 2020). For the model, Graus matters because he places the religious response and the violence inside one explanatory frame. Faith did not simply rise or fall: it was mobilized in competing directions.

**Research reports and catastrophe studies.** Neithard Bulst's *Saeculum* article (1979) remains the standard German research report on demographic, economic and cultural consequences of 1347–52 [GK]. Arno Borst's essay on the earthquake of January 1348 (Borst 1981) founded German *Katastrophenforschung* (disaster research). Borst shows contemporaries holding theological readings (divine punishment, eschatological sign) alongside naturalistic ones [GK]. This coexistence matters for the model. Providential interpretation was not the only frame available, yet it was not displaced either.

**The pogroms: power, not panic.** Alfred Haverkamp's long essay (1981, 27–93) dissected the 1348–50 persecutions in German towns. It analyses the role of each social group and offers a chronological table of the pogroms; its pages on the flagellants are 43–46. Haverkamp's central and widely cited thesis is that many pogroms preceded the plague's arrival in a given town [GK]. Town councils, lords, debt relations and urban factional conflict shaped the violence more than plague panic did [GK]. The reference infrastructure for this argument is *Germania Judaica* (vol. 3, Maimon, Breuer and Guggenheim 1987–2003), Haverkamp's atlas (2002), and Toch's handbook (1998). Samuel Cohn's English-language article (2007) engages this German literature directly [GK].

For the model, Haverkamp shifts the explanatory weight to the first variable: position in the power structure. Religious language legitimized violence, but those with civic power decided whether violence happened. The Jewish communities' situation also illustrates the model's third outcome in reverse. For them, Christianity was the religion of the perpetrators, and conversion under threat was coerced rather than chosen [GK].

**Flagellant-song scholarship.** Arthur Hübner's edition and study of the German *Geisslerlieder* (1931; reprint 2020) is the primary-source base for lay penitential religion in 1349. The songs and the "heavenly letter" (*Himmelsbrief*) legitimated a movement led by laymen, not priests [GK]. Paul Runge's edition of the songs and melodies recorded by Hugo of Reutlingen is available in a 1969 reprint (Runge 1900). Teresa Proto's 2014 philological and musicological study renewed this field. Peter Segl's encyclopedia article (1984) summarizes the movement and Pope Clement VI's condemnation of October 1349 [GK].

The flagellants are the clearest continental case of *faith up, institution bypassed*. Sufferers created their own ritual body, with its own leaders, rules and scripture-like letter. The hierarchy then suppressed it. The model predicts this when the sufferers lack institutional office and the establishment is seen as failing.

**Syntheses and urban studies.** Klaus Bergdolt's *Der Schwarze Tod in Europa* (1994) is the standard German one-volume synthesis [GK]. His shorter *Die Pest* (2006; 2nd ed. 2021) has a chapter on flagellant processions and Jewish persecutions (65–70). Mischa Meier's edited volume (2005) runs from antiquity to the modern era. Volker Reinhardt (2021) offers a recent synthesis strong on Italian chroniclers [GK]. Peter Dinzelbacher's *Angst im Mittelalter* (1996) is the German counterpart, and partial critique, of Delumeau on fear [GK].

Kay Peter Jankrift's study of Westphalian and Rhenish towns (2020) covers 1349–1600. Its original title quotes a civic plea "that God would take mercy on us" (*up dat god sich aver uns verbarmen wolde*). The book examines how councils combined processions, vows and patron saints with administrative measures [GK]. Its long time-span lets the reader test the model's duration problem. Repeated outbreaks did not obviously erode civic religion; they routinized it [GK].

**Recent theology-inflected work.** Volker Leppin's article "Den Tod neu denken" (2021a) addresses late-medieval piety after the plague. His book *Repräsentation und Reenactment* (2021b) appeared in English as *Medieval Spirituality* (Leppin 2025). Leppin reads late-medieval piety as a search for direct, personal re-enactment of salvation, a trajectory that later fed the Reformation [GK]. For a reader interested in theology, this is the main bridge from plague religiosity to Reformation critique of the institution.

### French-language scholarship: mentalités, testaments and the pastoral of fear

The French *Annales* and *histoire des mentalités* traditions supply the strongest theoretical tools. They treat religion as collective behaviour that can be counted, mapped and periodized.

**Biraben's survey.** Jean-Noël Biraben's two volumes (1975–76) remain the reference survey for France and the Mediterranean. Volume 2, *Les hommes face à la peste*, covers processions, plague saints such as Sebastian and Roch, votive churches and civic measures from 1348 to the eighteenth century [GK]. Biraben's chronology of outbreaks underpins any argument about recurrence.

**Chiffoleau's "accounting of the hereafter."** Jacques Chiffoleau's *La comptabilité de l'au-delà* (1980) is the most important continental work for this project. Chiffoleau studied thousands of testaments from Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin, c. 1320–c. 1480 [GK on the size of the corpus]. He argued that plague and migration uprooted urban populations from lineage and ancestral graves [GK]. In response, testators bought masses for the dead in ever larger, cumulative numbers. They also joined confraternities and arranged elaborate funerals [GK]. His related articles treat confraternities (Chiffoleau 1979) and the economics of the church as an institution (Chiffoleau 1984).

The thesis cuts against a simple faith-versus-institution dichotomy. Suffering *intensified* religious investment, and the investment flowed *through* the church's sacramental economy. Yet the investment was quantified, anxious and individualized: a kind of insurance [GK]. Chiffoleau's 1984 article on church economics raises the corollary. An institution financed by fear-driven foundations could later be attacked as mercenary. This anticipates the Reformation critique of the mass economy, a link historians often draw but which Chiffoleau's Avignon data do not test directly [GK].

Samuel Cohn tested a related question with Italian wills (Cohn 1992). He argued that the decisive shift came after the second plague of 1363, not 1348 [GK]. The two studies together suggest that recurrence, not first shock, drove the new piety.

**Ariès as counterweight.** Philippe Ariès ([1977] 1981) located the late-medieval "death of the self" in long-term change, not in plague [GK]. He downplayed plague as a cause of the macabre. His work is a necessary check against reading every late-medieval devotion as plague trauma.

**Delumeau's pastoral of fear.** Jean Delumeau's *La peur en Occident* (1978) has a celebrated chapter on plague. It traces denial, flight, scapegoating and penitential processions [GK]. *Le péché et la peur* ([1983] 1990) develops the "pastorale de la peur." Clergy, he argues, amplified guilt and fear of damnation from the thirteenth to the eighteenth century, partly in answer to collective calamity [GK]. *La peur* has no English translation; *Le péché* does (*Sin and Fear*).

Delumeau's contribution to the model is that the church does not merely *receive* the sufferers' response. It *shapes* the theological interpretation of suffering (variable 2) in its own interest. "Divine punishment for collective sin" was not a spontaneous popular reading. It was taught. Dinzelbacher (1996) qualifies the thesis by stressing popular and iconographic sources [GK]. A testable implication follows: where clergy controlled interpretation, the punishment frame dominated. Where laity controlled it (the flagellants), it took more apocalyptic and self-authorized forms.

**Carpentier's Orvieto.** Élisabeth Carpentier's micro-study (1962a; 2nd ed. 1993) followed one Italian commune through 1348. It documents brief post-plague moral and religious legislation that faded within a few years [GK]. Her *Annales* article (1962b) placed plague among recurrent fourteenth-century famines and epidemics. Carpentier's evidence suggests that civic-religious reform after the first shock was short-lived.

**Purgatory, sainthood, lay piety and the dead.** Jacques Le Goff ([1981] 1984) explains the doctrinal background: purgatory made the masses Chiffoleau counted meaningful. André Vauchez's work on lay piety ([1987] 1993) and on late-medieval sainthood ([1981] 1997) shows laypeople building devotional forms and new cults, including plague intercessors [GK]. Catherine Vincent's study of confraternities in the kingdom of France (1994) documents their growth after 1348 [GK]. Michel Vovelle's *La mort et l'Occident* (1983) gives the long-run French synthesis; it has no English translation that this review could verify. Jean-Claude Schmitt ([1994] 1998) shows how the living negotiated obligations to the dead, a relationship that plague mortality intensified [GK]. Alberto Tenenti's French-language study of fifteenth-century death art (1952) belongs to the debate on macabre iconography.

### Italian-language scholarship: recurrence, confraternities and civic religion

**Demography and recurrence.** Lorenzo Del Panta's *Le epidemie nella storia demografica italiana* (1980) is the standard chronology of Italian plague waves [GK]. It establishes that Italians lived with recurring plague for three centuries. Any claim about "the" response to suffering must specify which wave. Guido Alfani's English-language work (2013) carries this Italian demographic tradition to a wider audience.

**Tenenti's counter-thesis.** Alberto Tenenti's *Il senso della morte e l'amore della vita nel Rinascimento* (1957) argued for a fifteenth-century turn from Christian fear of death toward humanist love of life and fame [GK]. It is the main Italian alternative to the "plague produced morbid piety" story. It is also the nearest continental analogue to a secularizing branch, though Tenenti describes a cultural shift among elites, not a loss of faith.

**Confraternities.** Gilles Gérard Meersseman's three-volume *Ordo fraternitatis* (1977) is the foundational work on Italian lay confraternities, including the *disciplinati* (flagellant confraternities) [GK]. Thomas Frank and Luca Gufi's synoptic edition of the Viterbo *disciplinati* statutes of 1355 and 1365 (2007) gives direct post-plague evidence. Giovanni Cherubini's essays on late-medieval Italian society (1974) include rural religious life [GK; essay titles not verified].

The Italian contrast with Germany is the key comparative finding. In Italy, penitential energy was absorbed into permanent, statute-governed confraternities that communes and clergy tolerated or sponsored [GK]. Daniel Bornstein's English-language study of the Bianchi of 1399 (1993) stresses the orthodoxy of a mass penitential movement; that clergy and communes co-opted it is not confirmed in the descriptions and reviews consulted *[verify]*. In Germany in 1349, a comparable movement broke with Rome. The model reads this as a difference in the church's role. Where institutions offered sufferers an office (confraternal membership, procession leadership), faith and institutional trust rose together. Where they did not, the two diverged.

**The 1994 Todi volume and the Meiss debate.** The proceedings of the thirtieth Todi conference, *La peste nera* (1994), are the major Italian collective statement on 1348. They include Irma Naso's essay on diagnostic identification, and an essay by Gabriele Zanella on the historiography of 1348 in Italy, France and Germany *[verify title and pages]*. Millard Meiss's thesis (1951) that Florentine and Sienese painting turned conservative and penitential after 1348 is English-language but central to Italian art history. Its critics are mostly anglophone [GK]. A recent philological study of fifteen Venetian women's testaments from 1348 (Modolo and Tomasin 2025) shows the Chiffoleau-type testamentary source still yielding new evidence.

### Iberian scholarship: royal documents and the 1348 pogroms

Iberian work is documentary and archaeological rather than theoretical. Amada López de Meneses published the fundamental collection of Crown of Aragon documents on the Black Death (1956). Her later *Sefarad* article on the Catalan pogrom of 1348 is widely cited; its pages are confirmed only through a 2022 bibliography (López de Meneses 1959 *[verify]*). Antoni Cardoner i Planas (1973) covers medical responses, including Jewish physicians [GK].

The excavation at Tàrrega found six communal graves, apparently holding Jewish victims of the 1348 assaults (Colet et al. 2015). It gives rare material confirmation of plague-time anti-Jewish violence. Royal documents show the Crown ordering protection of Jews after the attacks [GK]. Guillermo Castán Lanaspa (2020) critically re-examines how Spanish historiography constructed 1348 as a demographic catastrophe, region by region.

The Iberian pattern parallels Haverkamp's German findings [GK]. Violence was religiously framed but locally organized, and royal authority sometimes restrained it. For the model, this again points to power position as the decisive variable for violent outcomes.

### Dutch-language work and Latin editions

Paul Fredericq's study of the flagellant and dancing "sects" in the fourteenth-century Low Countries (1896) is the classic regional account [GK]. Ursmer Berlière (1908) edited three unpublished Latin treatises on the 1349 flagellants. They show clergy debating the movement's legitimacy, which is direct evidence of institutional unease with lay-led penance.

Leo Noordegraaf and Gerrit Valk's *De gave Gods* (1988) runs to the last Dutch plagues of the 1660s. Its title records the common Dutch name for plague: "God's gift." Plague is recorded somewhere in Holland in 107 of the 219 years between 1450 and 1668 (Noordegraaf and Valk [1988] 1996). This durable providential label, persisting across the Reformation, suggests long-run continuity of religious interpretation, not erosion.

### Continental-language scholarship on slave religion

Continental-language work is also central to the Atlantic comparison. It helps separate the model's two confounded variables, power position and church role.

**Brazil (French and Portuguese).** Roger Bastide's *Les religions africaines au Brésil* ([1960] 1978) distinguished a white, seigneurial institutional Catholicism from the brotherhood Catholicism that Africans made their own [GK]. Pierre Verger (1968) documented two-way Atlantic circulation of people and orixá cults between Bahia and the Bight of Benin. João José Reis's *A morte é uma festa* ([1991] 2003) opens with the 1836 Cemiterada. Bahians, led by lay brotherhoods, rioted to defend church burial against a secularizing reform [GK on brotherhood leadership]. His study of the 1835 Muslim uprising ([1986] 1993) shows resistance organized through a rival, non-Christian religious community.

Mariza de Carvalho Soares (2000) and Lucilene Reginaldo (2011) show African ethnic communities rebuilt through Rosary and other black brotherhoods. Laura de Mello e Souza ([1986] 2004) shows the institutional church as prosecutor of Afro-Brazilian healing and ritual. Together, these works show that Catholic establishment alone did not predict outcomes. Access to lay-controlled corporate bodies did.

**Moravian and Dutch Caribbean (German and Dutch).** The unabridged critical edition of C. G. A. Oldendorp's 1767–68 history of the Moravian mission in the Danish West Indies (Oldendorp 2000–2002) restores material cut from the 1777 printing. Only the abridged 1777 version exists in English (Oldendorp 1987). Jan Hüsgen (2016) offers a critical study of slaveholding *within* the Moravian Caribbean mission. He argues that Moravian historiography long downplayed this complicity. Armando Lampe (2001) compares German Moravian and Dutch Catholic missionaries in Suriname and Curaçao and finds both supporting Dutch colonial policy, which delayed emancipation. J. M. van der Linde (1956) and Maria Lenders (1996) wrote the Dutch-language histories of the Moravian mission in Suriname.

For the model, this literature supplies the "church as slaveholder" case on the Protestant side. Moravian missions owned enslaved people yet produced durable Black congregations with Black helpers. Ownership did not prevent faith intensification where converts held congregational office. It does seem to predict later institutional critique and historiographical silence (Hüsgen 2016) [GK on the general pattern].

### Where the traditions agree, disagree and fall silent

**Agreement.** All traditions agree that plague raised the demand for intercession: masses, processions, saints and confraternities. This supports the model's first premise. They also agree that the institutional church did not lose its sacramental monopoly in the short run. Chiffoleau, Meersseman and Biraben all describe more investment in church-mediated rites, not less [GK].

**Disagreement.** The traditions diverge on *who* drove the response. The German tradition stresses local power and conflict (Graus, Haverkamp). The French stresses mentalities and clerical pedagogy (Delumeau) or demographic uprooting (Chiffoleau). The Italian stresses institutional absorption through confraternities and communes. They also disagree on causation. Ariès and Tenenti doubt that plague caused the macabre or a lasting penitential turn. Meiss, Delumeau and in part Chiffoleau see a stronger link [GK].

**Silences.** No continental tradition offers a robust case of mass loss of faith after plague. This may reflect the absence of alternatives in fourteenth-century Latin Europe; Jews were victims, not an available option. It may also reflect source bias, since testaments and statutes record believers. The Low Countries evidence (Noordegraaf and Valk) suggests continuity, not erosion, over three centuries. Whether repeated suffering eventually fed the Reformation's attack on the mass economy is a plausible but untested link between Chiffoleau and Leppin.

### Implications for the model

- **Supports the middle outcome.** The flagellants (Hübner; Graus) and Italian *disciplinati* (Meersseman) show faith intensifying while moving into lay-controlled bodies. The decisive difference was whether the hierarchy absorbed or condemned them.
- **Refines variable 2.** Delumeau shows that the theological interpretation of suffering was partly a clerical product. The model should treat "punishment for collective sin" as contested between clergy and laity, not as an exogenous belief.
- **Complicates the faith/institution dichotomy.** Chiffoleau's quantified piety shows faith intensifying *through* institutional channels while commodifying them. That may plant later distrust without immediate erosion.
- **Strengthens variable 1 for violent outcomes.** Haverkamp and the Iberian evidence locate the decision to persecute in civic and royal power, not in suffering as such.
- **Addresses duration.** Del Panta, Jankrift, Noordegraaf and Valk, and Cohn's 1363 thesis all point to recurrence as the driver of new piety. First-shock responses (Carpentier's Orvieto) were short-lived.
- **Helps separate the confound in the slave case.** Continental-language work on Brazil and the Moravian Caribbean shows faith attaching to sufferer-controlled bodies even under a slaveholding church. This favours church role over power position as the predictor of faith intensification.
