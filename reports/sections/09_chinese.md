## 9. Chinese-language scholarship

Chinese-language scholarship holds one strong historical cluster on the Black Death, a rich Taiwan-specific literature on churches, suffering and identity, and almost no historical work on African American slave religion; no Chinese-language study compares these cases. The gap is real, but it is uneven. A Taiwan-based author can cite Sinophone work as independent corroboration on the medieval side and as primary evidence on the Taiwan side, while acknowledging that the African American side reaches Chinese readers mainly through translated theology.

This section rests on catalogue and repository checks run in November 2026. CNKI, Airiti Library and the NDLTD thesis database could not be queried (NDLTD now requires a CAPTCHA). Verification therefore used author and university pages, journal PDFs, Douban book records, bookseller pages, Crossref and a few Google Scholar listings. Items seen only in a Google Scholar listing are marked *[verify]*.

### Black Death, religion and the institutional church in mainland historiography

Mainland research on the Black Death is substantial and appears in top journals, but one scholar and his circle produce most of it. 李化成 (Li Huacheng), dean of history at Shaanxi Normal University, has published on the plague since 2006 in 《中国社会科学》, 《历史研究》 and 《世界历史》. His faculty page gives exact titles for the core corpus (Shaanxi Normal University 2020). Most of this work is social and medical history of England, but three articles address religion directly.

The first is his study of the English Church during the plague (Li 2008), on clerical mortality and institutional strain. The second is his study of the flagellant movement of 1348–1349 (Li 2013). Secondary summaries describe it as treating the flagellants as a crisis piety that broke away from clerical control and was then suppressed by the papacy *[verify content from the article]*. The third, with 郭超 (Guo Chao), extends this into the longer history of monastic change and collective flagellation in the high and late Middle Ages (Li and Guo 2024).

A fourth article matters for the model's theological variable. Its title promises an intellectual history of plague that compares Chinese and Western theories of divine or Heavenly punishment, 天（神）谴论 (Li 2007c) *[verify content]*. If its argument matches the title, it is the only Chinese-language work found that sets the "punishment for collective sin" reading of epidemics in cross-cultural comparison. Li's COVID-era article, by contrast, frames the plague through knowledge, mechanisms and society, which is a governance story rather than a religious one (Li 2020a).

Other mainland authors exist but are less central. Google Scholar lists 赵立行 (Zhao Lixing) on Western historiography of the Black Death in 《历史研究》 (Zhao 2005), 李荷 (Li He) on cultural change after the plague (Li He 2004), and 龙秀清 (Long Xiuqing) and 李锦荣 (Li Jinrong) on English monasteries (Long and Li 2012) *[verify all three]*. 王旭东 (Wang Xudong) and 孟庆龙 (Meng Qinglong) wrote a general world history of epidemics (Wang and Meng 2005). Lower-tier journals carry short essays linking the plague to the Reformation or humanism; these add little.

Taiwan has no peer-reviewed article on the Black Death and religion that I could find. The leading medical historian, 李尚仁 (Li Shang-jen), wrote a public-history essay (Li Shang-jen 2020). 張家麟 (Chang Chia-lin) surveys penitential and plague-expulsion theologies (懺悔觀; 懲奸觀與驅瘟觀) before analysing state regulation of religion in Taiwan under the COVID-19 Level 3 alert (Chang 2021). Whether it explicitly compares medieval flagellant penance with Taiwanese plague-god rituals is not shown by its abstract or table of contents *[verify]*.

For the model, this cluster supports the "faith up, institution strained" branch for 1348–1349. It does not test the duration problem raised by Cohn, because no Chinese work found studies the recurrences after 1361. It also does not separate the sufferers' power position from the church's role.

### African American religion and Black theology in Chinese

This literature is thin and almost entirely theological or musicological. I found no Chinese-language historical monograph or peer-reviewed historical article on antebellum slave religion. Raboteau, Genovese, Levine and Frey and Wood appear to be untranslated, and no Chinese translation of James Cone's books was found.

The most important item is a translation of Anthony Pinn's *Why, Lord?* (Pinn 2006). According to a bookseller's description, it treats theodicy in Black theology through sermons, folklore, spirituals, blues and rap, and Pinn challenges cherished beliefs of Black Christian communities and defends a Black humanist tradition (bookseller description of Pinn 2006). No library catalogue record has yet confirmed the title, publisher or translator: the ISBN's Douban record is a dead page, and the translator's name, 周輝 (Zhou Hui), comes only from a search summary *[verify]*.

Mainland journals add a few survey articles. 雷雨田 (Lei Yutian) wrote on the historical origins of American Black theology (Lei 1999) *[verify]*. Musicological and literary pieces treat spirituals as the expression of Black religious experience (Jing 2008; Zhang Jingjing 2015) *[verify both]*. Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk* has been in Chinese since 1959 (Du Bois 1959, reissued 2024). Theology textbooks such as the translated Grenz and Olson survey give Chinese readers their usual route to Cone (Grenz and Olson 2004).

In Taiwan, the only academic work found is an MA thesis at the Taiwan Graduate School of Theology. It compares African American spirituals with Taiwanese-language hymns in civil-rights movements (Hsu 2022).

This pattern matters for the model. Through Pinn, Chinese readers meet the **loss-of-faith or humanist branch** of Black religious thought before they meet the historical **intensification** story of slave Christianity. A Sinophone reader could therefore draw a skewed picture of how slavery affected faith. An English article written from Taiwan can correct this simply by naming the asymmetry.

### Theodicy and suffering in Chinese-language theology

Chinese theology of suffering has two centres: Hong Kong's Sino-Christian theology and Taiwan's Presbyterian contextual theology. The first is mostly philosophical. The second is political and maps directly onto the model's Exodus pole.

On the Hong Kong and mainland side, 劉小楓 (Liu Xiaofeng)'s 《拯救与逍遥》 is the classic contrast between Christian redemption through suffering and Chinese traditions of serene detachment (Liu 2007; first ed. 1988). The Institute of Sino-Christian Studies has made Moltmann and Kitamori available in Chinese, as the table shows. Recent work includes 黃路蘋 (Huang Luping) on theodicy in illness and existential crisis (Huang Luping 2021) *[verify]*. 周海金 (Zhou Haijin) published a monograph on suffering as a theological problem in an African-studies series (Zhou 2014); its scope needs checking. None of these studies collective suffering historically or asks how it affects trust in churches.

Taiwan's Presbyterian theology is different. It grew out of shared political suffering under martial law and reads that suffering through Exodus. 邱凱莉 (Chiu Kai-li) traces four key texts across thirty years (Chiu 2021, 121–146). They are 王憲治 (Wang Hsien-chih)'s homeland theology (Wang Hsien-chih 1988), 黃伯和 (Huang Po-ho)'s *chhut-thau-thiⁿ* ("rising up out of oppression") theology (Huang Po-ho 1991), 陳南州 (Chen Nan-jou)'s theology of identification (Chen Nan-jou 2003 *[unverified]*), and 羅光喜 (Lo Kuang-hsi)'s theology of nation-building (Lo Kuang-hsi 2018 *[unverified]*).

Chiu reports that Chen reads the Bible as the story of God identifying with human suffering, with Exodus as the basis for identifying with the oppressed (Chiu 2021, 131–132). This is the "God suffers with the afflicted" interpretation in a Taiwanese voice. C. S. Song (宋泉盛) supplied much of the method. His Chinese-language presence includes the translation of *The Tears of Lady Meng* (Song 1994) and an edited documentary history of the Taiwanese self-determination movement (Song 1988).

Chiu also offers a critique that complicates the model. She argues that the original three dimensions of *chhut-thau-thiⁿ* (political-social, cultural, and spiritual) narrowed over time into a single political goal of independence (Chiu 2021, 121). In this case, an Exodus reading of suffering did more than sustain faith. It also turned a church into a carrier of national identity. That may raise institutional trust within one constituency while lowering it among others.

### Taiwan-specific literature

Taiwan supplies the richest Sinophone material for the model, because one polity held churches in different positions at the same time. Three bodies of work are relevant.

**The Presbyterian Church, 228 and the White Terror.** 曾慶豹 (Tseng Ching-pao)'s *Joseph and His Brothers* uses archival sources on 1965–2015 (Tseng 2017). The publisher's description says it shows how state intervention turned the 1965 Protestant centenary into a conflict between the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) and anticommunist "party-state Christians" (黨國基督徒). It says this split produced today's divide between the PCT and the non-Presbyterian churches. This is close to a direct test of the church-role variable. Two Protestant camps faced the same regime, and one sided with it while the other was persecuted. 鄭仰恩 (Cheng Yang-en)'s essays on Taiwanese church history (Cheng 2005) and 蔡榮芳 (Tsai Jung-fang)'s study of Shoki Coe (Tsai 2020) supply the historical background.

**Indigenous mass conversion after 1945.** The anthropological literature links conversion to crisis, but crisis in a local, social sense. 黃應貴 (Huang Ying-kuei) explicitly ties Bunun religious change to social crisis (Huang Ying-kuei 1991). 王梅霞 (Wang Mei-hsia) shows that Seediq conversion followed the shock of forced village relocation under Japanese rule and rising conflict after 1945. She argues that the Christian discourse of a "changed heart" or repentance helped damp jealousy and conflict (Wang Mei-hsia 2018, 1–2). 邱韻芳 (Chiu Yun-fang) studies Truku conversion and revival (Chiu Yun-fang 2013). 周沛忻 (Chou Pei-hsin) shows that the PCT's postwar mountain mission both diverged from and cooperated with the state (Chou 2015). Survey data in 范姜曉涵 (Fan Chiang Hsiao-han) put Christian affiliation among mountain Indigenous people at about 85 percent in 1985 (Fan Chiang 2019).

**Relief, refugees and institutional decline.** Richard Madsen's Chinese-language study of two Maryknoll districts is the strongest single test of the model in this corpus (Madsen 2012). Catholic numbers grew more than sevenfold within ten years of 1949, among Mainlander refugees and others in crisis *[verify figure against Madsen 2012, 62]*. The church distributed US surplus flour, milk and clothing. Missionaries worried about "flour Christians" (麵粉基督徒), and some families were baptized to secure relief (Madsen 2012, 62). Madsen argues that decline after the mid-1960s came from reduced dependence on US relief and loss of the church's paternal authority, not from materialism. It also came from conflict after Vatican II and from the barrier between Mandarin-speaking parishes and Taiwanese or Indigenous people.

This supports the model's claim that suffering raises demand for intercession and provision. It also adds a point the model lacks: affiliation won through material provision in a crisis can fade when the crisis ends. 王政文 (Wang Cheng-wen)'s work on nineteenth-century converts gives the long baseline of family conflict over ancestor rites (Wang Cheng-wen 2012).

**Disaster religiosity.** I found no Chinese-language study that measures change in Christian faith or church trust after the 1999 Chi-Chi (921) earthquake. Google Scholar lists a study of religious charity in 921 reconstruction (An 2009) *[verify]*. It also lists 鄭筱筠 (Zheng Xiaoyun)'s analysis of religious response systems at disaster sites (Zheng 2012). Its author is a mainland scholar at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, and its title does not mention 921, so its case may be a mainland earthquake (Wenchuan or Yushu) rather than Taiwan *[verify case before citing it as Taiwan evidence]*. For Typhoon Morakot (2009), it lists a 2023 Taiwan Graduate School of Theology thesis on rebuilding the Rukai Presbytery's mission at the Kucapungane (Haocha) church (Wu 2023) *[verify]*. Post-Morakot Indigenous studies exist in social science. 林津如 (Lin Chin-ju), for example, studies Indigenous women's sovereignty practices after the disaster (Lin 2023), but I did not check whether it treats churches.

### Assessment: how thin is the field?

| Area | Density | Main venues | Main gap | Opportunity for a Taiwan-based author |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Black Death and religion | Moderate; one school | 《历史研究》, 《世界历史》, 《安徽史学》 | No study of plague recurrence and religion; no comparative frame | Cite Li 2008, 2013, 2024 as independent corroboration |
| African American religion | Very thin | Translations; teaching journals | No history of slave religion; Cone untranslated | Name the asymmetry; bring the intensification story to Sinophone readers |
| Chinese theodicy | Moderate, philosophical | 道風 (Hong Kong), 《輔仁宗教研究》 | Little on collective or historical suffering | Link theodicy to institutional trust |
| Taiwan church and politics | Rich | 台灣教會公報社, 人光, 輔仁宗教研究 | Advocacy and theology dominate; little quantitative history | Use membership data to compare PCT and KMT-aligned churches |
| Indigenous conversion | Rich, anthropological | 《考古人類學刊》, NTU theses | Conversion and crisis rarely set in comparison | Treat it as a third case beside slavery and plague |
| Disaster religiosity (921, Morakot) | Thin for Christianity | Theses, disaster-management journals | No study of faith or church trust after disaster | Original fieldwork or survey reanalysis |

The honest conclusion is that a comparative claim must rest mostly on English-language scholarship. Sinophone work supplies corroboration and Taiwanese cases. The contribution claim can be stated precisely: "Chinese-language scholarship has treated the medieval plague (Li 2008, 2013; Li and Guo 2024) and Taiwanese church suffering (Tseng 2017; Chiu 2021; Madsen 2012) separately, but no Chinese-language study compares collective suffering across these cases or asks why it sometimes strengthens and sometimes erodes trust in churches."

### Implications for the model

- **Supports the church-role variable.** Tseng (2017) and Madsen (2012) show that, within one polity and era, churches allied with the regime and churches of the persecuted had different trajectories. This is the cleanest Sinophone evidence that situation, not ethnicity or doctrine alone, drives the outcome.
- **Adds a provision mechanism.** Madsen's "flour Christians" show that crisis affiliation can depend on material relief and decline when relief ends. The model should separate faith intensification from affiliation bought by provision.
- **Complicates the Exodus pole.** Taiwanese Presbyterian theology shows that a suffering-with reading can harden into national identity, raising trust in one constituency and lowering it in others (Chiu 2021).
- **Supports the crisis-conversion branch, in a social key.** Huang Ying-kuei (1991) and Wang Mei-hsia (2018) tie Indigenous conversion to social crisis and relocation. The theological content stresses repentance and social repair, not divine punishment.
- **Does not resolve the duration problem.** Mainland plague work centres on 1348–1349; no Sinophone study tests Cohn's point about 1363 and later recurrences.
- **Leaves the loss-of-faith branch under-documented.** Chinese readers know Pinn's humanist critique but not the historical record of slave Christianity's growth.
