## 16. Implications for the contemporary church

Today's evidence mostly fits the model's institutional branches, but the main driver of modern trust loss is not external collective suffering. It is institutional misconduct, above all clerical abuse, and the perceived alignment of churches with one political coalition. Belief generally outlasts belonging, exit is often aimed at a specific institution, and churches that serve their own communities retain members best. The "suffering raises demand for meaning" step, however, is weak in wealthy, pluralist societies, and believing without belonging decays across generations.

This section reads 21st-century survey and quasi-experimental evidence through the working model. It covers the United States, Germany, England and Wales, South Korea and Taiwan, with a short look at the global South. On contested social and political questions it describes churches' positions and their measured association with trust and affiliation; it does not evaluate the positions.

### Belief persists while institutional trust falls

**United States.** The cleanest long series is Gallup's confidence in "the church or organized religion" (share answering "a great deal" or "quite a lot"). It peaked at 68% in 1975, fell from 60% in 2001 to 45% in 2002, reached a series low of 31% in 2022 and stood at 33% in 2026 (Gallup n.d.-a). Gallup's own analysts link the late-1980s dip to the televangelist scandals and the 2002 drop to the Boston abuse and cover-up crisis, but in hedged language and without causal tests (Saad 2012) [WF: high, 3-0]. Ratings of clergy honesty fell even further: 67% "very high" or "high" in 1985, 52% in 2002, 37% in 2018 and 27% in 2025 (Gallup n.d.-b).

Belief has fallen much less. In Pew's 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study (N = 36,908), 83% believe in God or a universal spirit, but only 33% attend services at least monthly (Pew Research Center 2025a). Most religious "nones" believe in God or another higher power; "just 29%" reject any higher power or spiritual force (Pew Research Center 2024). Asked why they are nonreligious, 47% of nones cite dislike of religious organizations and 30% bad experiences with religious people (Pew Research Center 2024). The exit is thus partly aimed at institutions and their representatives, not only at doctrine. The pattern holds cross-nationally: in most of 22 countries at least one in five nones say they believe in God (Pew Research Center 2025d) [WF: high, 3-0].

**Germany.** The German case gives the most direct survey measurement of the model's mechanism. In the 2023 ALLBUS (N = 5,246), former Catholics rated as important: "I find the church untrustworthy" (83%), the church "did not protect children and vulnerable people from sexual abuse" (82%), irritation at church statements (76%) and "I can believe without the church" (58%) (fowid 2025a). Former members of the Protestant EKD gave similar answers: 74% cited the handling of abuse and 52% said belief is possible without the church (fowid 2025a).

The exit data match these reports. Catholic exits reached a record 522,821 in 2022 (fowid 2025b), and the two large churches together held 43.8% of the population at the end of 2025 (fowid 2026). Two peer-reviewed studies move beyond coincidence of timing. An Arellano–Bond panel for 1953–2015 attributes much year-to-year variation in exits to shocks, including the 2010 abuse revelations and the 2014 Limburg finance scandal (Frick and Simmons 2017) [WF: high, 3-0]. A diocese-level event study finds that Catholic scandals raised exits from the uninvolved Protestant church as well (Frick, Moser, and Simmons 2021) [WF: high, 3-0]. Its data end before the EKD's own 2024 abuse study, so later Protestant exits are not pure spillover.

**England and Wales.** A YouGov survey of 3,120 self-identified Catholics (June–July 2022) found that about one-third of former Mass-goers had cut back or stopped attending because of the abuse crisis (Pound and Ryan 2024) *[verify]* [WF: medium, 3-0]. About a third of former donors stopped giving, and 79% said the Church must change "a great deal." The authors call this "intentional distancing from the Church." The sample is an online panel, the causes are self-reported and COVID is a confounder. The respondents nonetheless still identify as Catholic: belonging fell, identity remained.

This replicates the US pattern. In 2019, 37% of US Catholics said abuse news made them question whether to remain in the Church, against 22% in 2002 (Jones 2019). That year 27% of Catholics scaled back Mass attendance and 26% reduced giving (Pew Research Center 2019). The strongest causal evidence comes from US dioceses: abuse scandals produced a long-lasting fall in participation and giving but no significant fall in belief (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015), and some losses moved to rival denominations (Hungerman 2013).

**Why Americans say they left, 2016 and 2023.** PRRI's reasons for leaving a childhood religion allow a seven-year comparison. The 2016 figures come from the PRRI "Exodus" report (Jones et al. 2016) [WF: medium, 2-1] and are quoted as baselines in PRRI 2024.

| Reason (rated important) | 2016 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Stopped believing the teachings | 60% | 67% |
| Negative teaching about or treatment of gay and lesbian (LGBTQ) people | 29% | 47% |
| Clergy sexual-abuse scandals | 19% | 31% |
| — among former Catholics | 32% | 45% |

Loss of belief remains the most cited reason. Yet both institution-centred reasons, abuse and the church's teaching on sexuality, grew faster than any other. This supports the model's claim that institutional conduct, not only doctrinal doubt, drives exit.

### Believing without belonging as a stage

Davie (1990; 1994) described Britain as "believing without belonging": belonging fell fast, belief slowly. Voas and Crockett (2005) challenged this with British panel and attitude data [WF: medium, 3-0]. They found belief eroding "at the same rate" as belonging, mainly by generational replacement. Only "about half of parental religiosity is successfully transmitted, while absence of religion is almost always passed on." Molteni and Biolcati (2023) likewise attribute European attendance decline to cohort replacement.

Voas and Chaves (2016) extended the argument to the United States: each successive cohort is less religious, so "America is not an exception." Schnabel and Bock (2017) replied that only moderate religion is declining. Voas and Chaves (2018) answered that even the intensely religious segment is shrinking. Pew's cohort data point the same way: among 18–24-year-olds raised in highly religious homes, only 28% are highly religious now, while 76% of those raised non-religious remain so (Pew Research Center 2025a).

The cross-sectional evidence (most nones still believe) and the cohort evidence (belief follows belonging down) can be reconciled. Believing without belonging looks like a lagged decay: belonging falls first and belief follows about a generation later. Counter-positions exist: Callum Brown's argument for a 1960s period shock *[verify citation]* and the general difficulty of separating age, period and cohort effects.

This suggests a fifth element for the model, alongside the demand for meaning and the three conditions (power position, theology, church role). We propose: **intergenerational transmission requires community.** Faith that survives an institutional failure without a gathered community may persist in the generation that experienced the failure, but is rarely passed on. The historical cases are consistent with this. Where enslaved Christians kept faith while rejecting the slaveholders' church, they also built their own gathered bodies (sections 5 and 7). Whether "faith migrates" or "faith decays" may therefore depend on whether a rival community is available to receive it.

### "The church sides with power" as partisan sorting

In the United States, the modern analogue of "the church sides with power" is partisan alignment rather than alliance with a state or slaveholding class. Hout and Fischer (2002) showed that the 1990s rise of the nones was "confined to political moderates and liberals," as "a symbolic statement against the Religious Right." Most of the new nones were "unchurched believers." GSS panel data later indicated that the causal direction "likely runs from political identity … to religious identity" (Hout and Fischer 2014). State-level data show none rates rising faster when the Christian Right enters salient public conflict (Djupe, Neiheisel, and Conger 2018). Campbell, Layman and Green (2021) argue that secular identity is "in part a reaction to the Religious Right."

The current numbers show sorting, not uniform decline. Among self-described liberals, Christian identity fell from 62% in 2007 to 37% in 2023–24; change among conservatives was much smaller (Pew Research Center 2025a). Confidence in organized religion stood at 64% among Republicans and 21% among Democrats in 2025 (Brenan 2025). Among unaffiliated Democrats, 61% cited LGBTQ-related teaching or treatment as a reason for leaving, against 18% of unaffiliated Republicans (PRRI 2024). Meanwhile white evangelical retention rose from 66% to 76% between 2016 and 2023 (PRRI 2024). White evangelical Protestants voted 81% for Donald Trump in 2024 (Pew Research Center 2025e), an indicator of how closely one tradition's members align with one party.

Inside a coalition, belonging can strengthen. Gallup finds the share of men aged 18–29 calling religion very important rose from 28% (2022–23) to 42% (2024–25), a rise "concentrated among young Republicans" (Newport and Saad 2026). Southern Baptist membership fell for the 19th consecutive year in 2025, while attendance and baptisms rose (Lifeway Research 2026). Perry, Whitehead and Grubbs (2020) add a useful distinction: during COVID, Christian nationalism predicted incautious behaviour, while religiosity predicted precaution. A church's politicized wing and its devotional core can respond in opposite ways to the same crisis.

**South Korea, 2024–26.** Korea is a new and sharp case. The Christian Ethics Movement of Korea's eighth trust survey (N = 1,000, fieldwork 5–10 January 2026) found 19.0% trust and 75.4% distrust in the Protestant church (Christian Ethics Movement of Korea 2026). The trust–distrust gap was 56.4 points, against 31.9 in 2020 and 52.3 in 2023. Of respondents, 47.1% see the church as wholly or partly far-right. Among the reasons cited, 64.5% named the church's defence of the 3 December 2024 martial-law declaration and 58.0% an "exclusionary, hateful attitude." Some 88.5% oppose pastors' political participation, including 83.0% of Christians.

Rival institutions in the same society gained. Between 2023 and 2026, the share naming Catholicism as the most trusted religion rose from 21.3% to 25.3%, and Buddhism from 15.7% to 24.4%; Protestantism fell from 16.5% to 13.6% (Christian Ethics Movement of Korea 2026). Among the non-religious, only 2.2% named Protestantism. CEMK's analyst also traces the fall to COVID, when some churches resisted public-health policy and became infection clusters, while noting that most churches complied. This is descriptive survey evidence without a causal design, but it fits the model's prediction of migration to rivals closely.

### The church as the sufferers' own community

Branch B predicts that faith and institution grow together where the church belongs to the sufferers. US Black Protestantism is the strongest contemporary case. Black Protestants have the highest retention of any group, at 82% (PRRI 2024). Most Black adults see opposing racism as essential to their faith, and 60% of Black attenders worship in congregations where most attendees and clergy are Black (Pew Research Center 2021a). Black nones are 90% believers in God or a higher power, and 66% of them credit Black churches with helping Black Americans toward equality (Pew Research Center 2021a). During COVID, Black Protestants reported a net positive religious impact (17% mostly positive, 11% mostly negative), while white Catholics reported a net negative one (Pew Research Center 2025b).

The verified run qualifies this branch [WF: medium, 3-0]. Black Americans value houses of worship mainly for spiritual comfort (72%) and fellowship (71%), and much less for political sermons (25%) (Pew Research Center 2021b). Only 29% say Black churches have done "a great deal" for equality, against 60% for civil rights organizations. Legitimacy here rests on direct care for sufferers more than on advocacy. Branch B is also eroding: 28% of Black Gen Z and 33% of Black Millennials are unaffiliated (Pew Research Center 2021a). Among Black adults, the share calling religion very important fell 22 points to 63% between 2001–05 and 2021–25 (Brenan 2026).

**Global South.** Sub-Saharan Africa now holds 30.7% of the world's Christians, against Europe's 22.3% (Pew Research Center 2025c). Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity accounted for at least a quarter of the world's Christians in 2006 estimates (Pew Research Center 2006) [WF: medium, 3-0]. Jenkins (2002) and Miller and Yamamori (2007) read this growth partly as Christianity among the poor and its social ministry. But Pew attributes the shift in Christianity's centre mainly to "much higher fertility rates in Africa" and disaffiliation in Western Europe (Pew Research Center 2025c). The fertility caveat is decisive: without decomposing growth into births, conversion and retention, global-South growth cannot be read as suffering-induced faith.

### Taiwan in depth

Taiwan shows the model's signature in miniature, with an important twist. In the Taiwan Social Change Survey (TSCS), the Protestant share was 4.0% in 2009, 5.5% in 2018 and 5.6% in 2023, with Catholics at 1.0–1.5% (Fu et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2024). The Christian share was thus stable or slightly rising. Over the same years, trust in "religious groups and churches" fell sharply.

| TSCS trust in religious groups and churches | 2009 | 2014 | 2018 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete or very much trust | 21.5% | 13.3% | 10.7% |
| Distrust (little or none) | 16.0% | 22.0% | 26.9% |

Source: Fu et al. 2019, appendix 4, 222. Figures appear unweighted; the item covers all religions, not only churches; the 2023 wave did not repeat it.

Disapproval of religious leaders speaking publicly on politics also peaked in 2018, the year of the same-sex-marriage referendums. On "whether the country is democratic," 62.3% disapproved in 2014, 67.6% in 2018 and 60.6% in 2023. On "whether society is fair," the figures were 57.5%, 64.1% and 58.9% (Wu et al. 2024, 326–27). This is a year-to-year association; the referendums are not causally identified as the cause.

The 2018 campaigns had a Christian dimension. Huang (2017) *[verify English title]* shows how conservative Taiwanese Christianity, "politically inactive for decades," mobilized from the early 2010s through state change, transnational East Asian networks and religious entrepreneurs, and provoked a secularist backlash. Ho (2019) describes a "church-based countermovement" that the marriage-equality movement eventually overcame. Tseng Ching-pao (2017) supplies the historical background: a genealogy of anti-communist, party-state-aligned Christians and the formation of Taiwanese fundamentalism. It is not a study of the 2018 referendums.

The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) stands in contrast, but on a specific axis. (Translations from the PCT documents are mine.) Its 1971 statement on national affairs argued that because "human rights are given by God, the people have the right to determine their own destiny" (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1971). Its 1977 human-rights declaration held that Taiwan's future should be decided by its seventeen million inhabitants and urged making Taiwan "a new and independent country" (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1977). In 1990 the PCT apologized to victims of the 228 Incident and their families: under martial law, "the church as a whole did not give the victims' families the support they deserved" (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1990). This is a rare primary source in which a church names its own failure to stand with sufferers.

The PCT also acted as a disaster church. After the 1999 Chi-Chi (921) earthquake it held memorial services across the affected areas and called for long-term reconstruction (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1999). After Typhoon Morakot in 2009 it quoted 1 Corinthians 12:26 ("if one member suffers, all suffer together"), mobilized its presbyteries using its 921 experience and blamed "improper economic development and land-planning policies" (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 2009).

On marriage, by contrast, the PCT General Assembly's 2014 pastoral letter defined marriage as the voluntary union of one adult man and one adult woman. It also called churches to accept people of same-sex orientation (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 2014). The PCT is therefore not the opposite pole to the conservative coalition on marriage. The contrast concerns human rights, democratization, national self-determination and disaster ministry. Whether the PCT took a formal position on the 2018 referendums is unverified.

Three cautions apply. First, the PCT's Branch-B stance did not produce visible numerical growth: Protestants remain about 4–6% of TSCS respondents. Branch B may yield loyalty and legitimacy rather than numbers in a pluralist society. Second, Taiwan's most prominent religious disaster responder is the Buddhist Tzu Chi movement (Huang 2009) *[verify 921 coverage]*. Any Christian institutional gain from disaster is probably confined to indigenous PCT and Catholic communities, which is a hypothesis, not a finding. Third, no quantitative study of religiosity after 921 or Morakot was located, and no peer-reviewed study of Taiwanese Christian youth disaffiliation; Yi et al. (2021) is the best starting point.

### Where the historical model fails today

1. **Exit costs are low.** Medieval and enslaved Christians could rarely leave Christendom. Leaving now costs little, and in Germany it saves church tax, which 63% of former EKD members cite (fowid 2025a). Discontent therefore shows up at once as exit, rather than as heterodoxy inside the church.
2. **Secular meaning is available.** Taylor (2007) describes a society in which faith is "only one human possibility among others." Demand for meaning after suffering can be met by therapy, politics or nation. In a 2025 Pew report, 69% of Americans said the COVID-19 pandemic had no impact on their religious life (Pew Research Center 2025b). Google searches for prayer did rise globally as churches closed (Bentzen 2021). Yet the net change in US practice was small: 13% attend in person less than before the pandemic and 7% more (Pew Research Center 2025b).
3. **Abuse is harm done by the church itself.** The French CIASE report found that the Church's "immediate reaction was to protect itself as an institution," showing "complete, even cruel, indifference" to those abused (CIASE 2021). This is the "church fails the sufferers" branch in its starkest form. But the sufferers here seek justice from the institution, not a theodicy, so the meaning-demand step is not engaged.
4. **Politics, not suffering, is the main proximate driver** of institutional exit in the United States and Korea. The model needs a political-sorting pathway alongside its suffering-to-meaning pathway.
5. **Migration and generalized exit both occur.** In the United States and Korea, failure sends people partly to rivals (Hungerman 2013; Christian Ethics Movement of Korea 2026). In Germany it raises exit from the uninvolved church too (Frick, Moser, and Simmons 2021). Where the two large churches read as one state-linked establishment, the German pattern resembles the single-church structure of medieval Christendom.

### Questions for churches

These are research-derived questions that churches and researchers might ask. They do not recommend positions on contested issues.

- **Care in crises.** Is the institution perceived by those affected as delivering spiritual comfort, fellowship and material help when a crisis strikes? Black Americans' priorities (Pew Research Center 2021b) suggest these, more than advocacy, sustain legitimacy.
- **Where trust sits.** Is trust concentrated in local clergy while distrust targets the hierarchy, as among US Catholics in 2019 (Jones 2019)? How does the institution's handling of misconduct by its own officials shape that gap?
- **Coalition and audience.** Which publics read the church's public statements as aligned with one party, and what do surveys show about trust among those outside that coalition (PRRI 2024; Christian Ethics Movement of Korea 2026; Wu et al. 2024)?
- **Transmission.** Is faith being passed on through gathered communities to the next cohort, given that only about half of parental religiosity is transmitted in Britain (Voas and Crockett 2005)?
- **Acknowledging failure.** Does a public acknowledgement of past failure, such as the PCT's 1990 apology, measurably change trust among those affected? No study tests this.
- **Who responds to disaster.** In a given society, which religious bodies does the public see as disaster responders, and does that perception translate into trust or affiliation?

### Implications for the model

- **Supported:** institutional failure produces exit from the failing institution while belief largely persists, with some migration to rivals. Abuse crises are the cleanest modern test (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; Frick, Moser, and Simmons 2021; fowid 2025a).
- **Amend the power variable:** today "the church sides with power" mostly means perceived partisan alignment. Exit is asymmetric: those outside the coalition leave, and those inside may intensify, producing polarization rather than uniform decline.
- **Add a fifth element:** intergenerational transmission requires community. Believing without belonging is a stage, and faith migrates rather than decays only when a receiving community exists.
- **Qualify Branch B:** the sufferers' own church retains members through care more than advocacy, and it slows rather than stops generational decline. In pluralist Taiwan it yields legitimacy more than numbers.
- **Weaken the first step for high-security societies:** collective shocks such as COVID left most Americans' religious lives unchanged, and global-South growth is confounded by fertility.
- **Exit form depends on market structure:** migration where rivals are distinct (US, Korea); generalized exit where churches read as one establishment (Germany).
