## 2. Theoretical frameworks: why suffering strengthens faith or erodes institutional trust

No single theory predicts both outcomes. Demand-side theories (deprivation, existential insecurity, coping, control and terror management) explain why collective suffering raises the demand for religion. Supply-side and authority theories (Weber on hierocracy, Bourdieu, religious economies, secularization as declining authority) explain which institution captures that demand or loses it. Theories of power and meaning (Weber's stratified theodicies, Berger, Scott, Walzer, Lincoln) explain how a group's position shapes the interpretation it adopts. The working model is best read as a synthesis that links these three families through explicit conditions. Theology itself is treated in section 3 and is cross-referenced rather than repeated here.

### 2.1 Weber: theodicy, social stratum and hierocracy

**Core claim.** Weber held that every religion of salvation must answer why fortune and suffering are distributed as they are. The answer differs by social stratum. Privileged strata want a "theodicy of good fortune" that shows their fortune is deserved. Negatively privileged strata are drawn to salvation religions that promise compensation, often through a future reversal (Weber 1946a; 1963; 1978). Weber also argued that archaic religion read suffering as a sign of divine disfavour, and that salvation religions revalued it as meaningful (Weber 1946a, 271).

Weber's sociology of domination adds the institutional half. A church is a compulsory hierocratic organization whose staff claims a monopoly of the legitimate use of "hierocratic coercion," the granting or withholding of religious benefits (Weber 1978, pt. 1, ch. 1, §17). Charisma, by contrast, arises in times of distress and challenges routine authority; to survive a generation it must be "routinized" (Weber 1946a; 1978).

**Predictions for the model.** *Power position:* the stratum's position selects the theodicy. *Theology and remedy:* where a hierocracy controls the remedy (sacraments, intercession), crisis raises dependence on it. *Church role:* failure to deliver the remedy opens space for prophets and lay movements. *Recurrence:* repeated failure turns a crisis of supply into a crisis of legitimacy. *Transmission:* only routinized movements outlive their founders. On these terms the flagellants of 1349 were charisma that was never routinized; the African Methodist Episcopal Church was charisma that was (sections 4 and 5).

**Critiques.** The stratum–theodicy pairing is static and functionalist. Morgan and Wilkinson (2001) argue that sociology turned theodicy into "sociodicy," legitimating inequality while ignoring the meaning of affliction. The dominated also adopt the theodicies of the powerful, as Genovese (1974) argued for slaves. Weber names the hierocracy, the sect and the prophet, but not the case where the sufferers' own church competes with an establishment that sides with the perpetrators. Riesebrodt (2010) gives a Weberian alternative: religion is defined by practices that promise to avert misfortune, cope with crisis and provide salvation. On that definition, collective suffering engages the core of religion, not its margins.

The novelty review (section 18) treats Weber as the closest prior art. The model's additions are the third variable (church role), the faith/institution split as the outcome, and a controlled comparison.

### 2.2 Berger: theodicy as legitimation and the plausibility structure

**Core claim.** In *The Sacred Canopy*, every social world (nomos) is threatened by suffering, evil and death. Theodicies explain these anomic events in the terms of the nomos and so protect it (Berger 1967). Berger stresses that theodicies legitimate inequality for both sides, giving meaning to the poverty of the poor and to the wealth of the rich. He calls the most radical form "masochistic": the self surrenders to the sacred other, which is right even when it destroys. Job is the paradigm. Christianity eases the tension through the incarnation, since God himself suffers (section 3.5). Every world also needs a "plausibility structure," a social base of people who share it and talk about it.

**Predictions.** *Theology:* a punishment frame can intensify faith, because self-abasement before God is itself a resolution. Flagellant penance fits. *Church role and care:* a theodicy holds only while its plausibility structure holds. Mass clerical death in 1348–49 or a master's ban on slave gatherings removes that base, unless a substitute community supplies it. The "invisible institution" was such a substitute (Raboteau [1978] 2004). *Recurrence:* repeated disconfirmation strains plausibility. *Transmission:* worlds pass to children only through ongoing conversation within a community.

**Critiques.** Berger later abandoned secularization theory as "essentially mistaken" (Berger 1999). Only later did he revise the pluralism argument itself: pluralism relativizes religion rather than secularizing it (Berger 2014). The US case contradicts it, as religious-economy theorists stress (Finke and Stark 1992). "Masochism" describes a stance from outside and may misread lament and protest (section 3). Still, Berger's plausibility structure is the most direct theoretical warrant for the model's claim that faith survives institutional failure only where a gathered community survives (section 17).

### 2.3 Durkheim, collective effervescence and collective trauma

**Core claim.** For Durkheim, assembly produces "collective effervescence," which renews the sacred and the group's attachment to it (Durkheim 1995). His analysis of piacular rites covers misfortune directly. Mourning and expiation after death or disaster bring the group together and restore its strength. Páez et al. (2015) gave the first full test. Perceived emotional synchrony in gatherings, including protest marches, raised collective identity, efficacy and positive social beliefs. Collins (2004) generalizes the mechanism as "interaction ritual chains" that charge symbols with emotional energy.

Disaster sociology adds the opposite case. Erikson's study of the 1972 Buffalo Creek flood, caused by a coal company's dam, defined collective trauma as damage to the bonds of community itself, separate from individual injury (Erikson 1976). He later argued that human-caused and toxic disasters form "a new species of trouble" that breeds distrust of institutions (Erikson 1994). The cultural-trauma school holds that a collectivity's trauma is made, not given. Carrier groups must narrate an event as a wound to identity (Alexander et al. 2004). Eyerman (2001) treats slavery as a cultural trauma formed mainly after emancipation, through memory, by generations that had not been enslaved.

**Predictions.** *Theology and church role:* suffering raises the demand for collective ritual, and solidarity accrues to whoever actually gathers the mourners. That may be the parish, the flagellant procession, the hush harbor or the ring shout. *Power and perpetrators:* natural catastrophe can produce solidarity; human-caused catastrophe erodes trust in institutions linked to the perpetrators. *Transmission and timing:* cultural trauma is built in the second generation. This fits Fountain's evidence that mass Black conversion came largely after 1865 (Fountain 2010; section 5).

**Critiques.** Durkheim neglects conflict and power: effervescence also fuels pogroms (section 2.9). The trauma concept is modern and clinical, so applying it to 1348 risks anachronism. Constructivism can understate the event itself.

### 2.4 Deprivation, compensation and religious economies

**Marx and deprivation.** Marx called religious suffering "the expression of real suffering and a protest against real suffering," as well as "the opium of the people" (Marx 1994). Both readings run through the slave historiography (Genovese 1974; section 5). Glock (1964) distinguished economic, social, organismic, ethical and psychic deprivation. He argued that deprivation produces religious movements only when it is shared and felt, when no secular remedy is available and when leaders emerge. Economic deprivation favours sects. Stark (1971) found that psychopathology was, if anything, negatively related to religious commitment, the reverse of what deprivation theory assumed. Social-movement theory shifted attention from grievances to resources and political opportunity (McAdam 1982).

**Compensators.** Stark and Bainbridge (1987) defined religion as a system of "general compensators," promises of reward that cannot be checked in this life. The privileged control churches and receive rewards. The deprived accept more compensators and form sects (Stark and Bainbridge 1980; 1987), defined as movements in high tension with society (Stark and Bainbridge 1979). Stark (1999) later rebuilt the theory around exchange with the gods and set the compensator idea aside.

**Religious economies and clubs.** Supply-side theory holds that monopolies are lazy, regulation depresses participation and pluralism invites entrepreneurs (Finke and Stark 1992; Stark and Finke 2000). Iannaccone (1992; 1994) explains strict groups as clubs. Costly demands screen out free riders and raise the value of membership. His concept of religious capital explains why faith is learned and transmitted (Iannaccone 1990). Economists have measured the insurance function directly. Common rainfall risk raised church membership in nineteenth-century US counties, and more than a third of the difference persists today (Ager and Ciccone 2018). The 1997–98 Indonesian crisis raised Islamic religious intensity, which worked as ex post insurance; credit access weakened the effect (Chen 2010). Religious attendance insured happiness against income shocks more strongly for Black than for white Americans (Dehejia, DeLeire, and Luttmer 2007). Ghanaian Pentecostals given formal insurance gave less to their church (Auriol et al. 2020).

**Predictions.** *Power:* the deprived seek compensators and mutual aid through bodies they control. *Church role and care capacity:* demand flows to suppliers that deliver care, and away from monopolies that do not. *Recurrence:* chronic risk raises belonging, while surprise shocks raise belief (Ager and Ciccone 2018, summarizing Bentzen's working paper). *Transmission:* religious capital and club membership persist across generations.

**Critiques.** The pluralism–participation link has not held up. Chaves and Gorski (2001) found no general positive relation, and Voas, Olson, and Crockett (2002) showed that earlier positive findings were artefacts of measurement. Marwell (1996) questioned whether strict churches are in fact strong. Insurance models explain belonging, not belief or theology. They cannot say why the enslaved embraced Exodus rather than any available club.

### 2.5 Existential security and its critics

**Core claim.** Norris and Inglehart (2004; 2011) argue that religiosity persists where life is insecure and erodes where human development makes survival secure. Multilevel studies support the link at both the individual and the national level. Insecurity predicts attendance across sixty countries (Ruiter and van Tubergen 2009). In Europe it predicts both public and private religiosity, and individual experiences of insecurity, such as unemployment and war, matter alongside national conditions (Immerzeel and van Tubergen 2013). Welfare spending substitutes for church provision (Gill and Lundsgaarde 2004).

**Predictions.** Collective suffering should raise religiosity, most where secular security is absent. The theory predicts a level of religiosity, not its location. It says nothing about which institution gains.

**Critiques.** Höllinger and Muckenhuber (2019) find a strong macro correlation but "only a very small impact" of individual insecurity. Molteni (2021) finds the evidence "far less clear than expected." Frijters et al. (2024) find near-zero effects of US natural disasters on the importance of religion and attendance, with a short-term fall in attendance as recovery work crowds out worship. In an experiment, Lang and Chvaja (2024) found that insecurity raised willingness to join normative groups, but the result was inconclusive when the group was framed as religious, though switching participants between secure and insecure environments gave strong support to the insecurity theory. Bentzen refines the thesis: unexpected shocks raise religiosity more than expected ones (Bentzen 2019), and the availability of religion, meaning prior religiosity, matters more than the material "need" for it (Bentzen 2021). For the model, existential security is a background condition. It explains why the meaning-demand step weakens in secure societies (section 17), not how faith and institutional trust diverge.

### 2.6 The psychology of religious coping, meaning and control

**Coping.** Pargament (1997) defines coping as a search for significance under stress, in which religion can conserve or transform what matters. Pargament et al. (1998) separated positive coping (benevolent reappraisal, seeking spiritual support) from negative coping (punishing-God reappraisal, spiritual discontent). In the RCOPE, punishing-God and spiritual-discontent reappraisals predicted poorer adjustment, while benevolent reappraisal and seeking religious support predicted better (Pargament, Koenig, and Perez 2000). The RCOPE also measures discontent with clergy and congregation separately from discontent with God (the "Interpersonal Religious Discontent" subscale). Flood victims' religious coping predicted later religious as well as psychological outcomes (Smith et al. 2000).

**Struggle and anger.** Exline's research on religious and spiritual struggles distinguishes six domains: divine, demonic, interpersonal, moral, doubt and ultimate meaning (Exline et al. 2014; Exline 2013). Anger toward God is common after harm. It is predicted by holding God responsible and by seeing oneself as a victim, and it harms adjustment when unresolved (Exline et al. 2011). Struggles predict distress in national samples (Abu-Raiya et al. 2015).

**Meaning and growth.** Park (2010) models stress as a violation of global meaning by a situational appraisal. Recovery comes either by assimilating the event to existing beliefs or by revising the beliefs. Posttraumatic growth theory counts spiritual change among growth domains (Tedeschi and Calhoun 2004), and trauma can deepen religion (Shaw, Joseph, and Linley 2005). Frazier et al. (2009) show that self-reported growth often does not match measured change.

**Control, attachment and death.** Lowered personal control raises belief in a controlling God and support for external systems (Kay et al. 2008). Kay et al. (2010) found a "hydraulic" relation: when government seems unstable, belief in religious sources of control rises. Attachment theory treats God as a safe haven activated by distress (Kirkpatrick and Shaver 1990; Kirkpatrick 2005). Two pathways compete. Under *compensation*, people with insecure human attachments turn to God, often by sudden conversion. Under *correspondence*, secure children of religious caregivers adopt their faith (Granqvist 1998; Granqvist and Kirkpatrick 2004; Granqvist, Mikulincer, and Shaver 2010; Granqvist 2020). Terror management theory holds that mortality salience strengthens afterlife belief and worldview defence, including hostility to outsiders (Greenberg et al. 1990; Vail et al. 2010).

**Predictions.** *Theology:* punishment frames are a negative-coping pattern and should be psychologically costly if suffering continues. Benevolent and solidarity frames sustain adjustment. *Church role:* the psychology already separates the relationship with God from the relationship with religious people. That is the model's faith/institution split at the individual level. *Power:* low control raises belief in God's control, and the hydraulic finding implies that a failing human institution can push belief toward God directly. *Recurrence:* repeated violation of global meaning forces revision rather than assimilation. *Transmission:* correspondence predicts transmission through caregivers; compensation predicts first-generation conversion.

**Critiques.** Most samples are modern, Western and individual. Collective, historical suffering is inferred, not measured. Mortality-salience effects have failed to replicate in recent work (Sætrevik and Sjåstad 2022). Positive coping can also dampen protest. Schnabel and Schieman (2022) found that religion protected US mental health in March 2020 but reduced support for public-health measures.

### 2.7 Power and legitimacy: the religious field and the hidden transcript

**Bourdieu.** The religious field is a struggle among specialists over the monopoly of legitimate management of the goods of salvation (Bourdieu 1971). The laity are dispossessed of religious capital. Priests defend orthodoxy, prophets challenge it, and the practices of the dominated are labelled magic. *Prediction:* crisis raises lay demand; if the priesthood cannot meet it, prophets and "magicians" gain. Conjure in slave religion (section 5) and the flagellants fit. *Critique:* the model is strategic and economistic, and Bourdieu wrote little empirical sociology of religion (Dianteill 2003).

**Scott.** Subordinates perform a public transcript of deference and keep a hidden transcript offstage (Scott 1990). The invisible institution is a hidden transcript. Slaves attended masters' churches in public and sang Exodus in the hush harbor. *Prediction:* outward attendance is a poor measure of trust in the establishment, and hidden transcripts go public when power shifts. The rapid exodus of freedpeople from white-controlled churches after 1865 fits (section 5). *Critique:* Mitchell (1990), responding to Scott's earlier *Weapons of the Weak* (Scott 1985), argues that Scott understates how domination shapes consciousness itself, the Gramscian point behind Genovese (1974).

**Walzer and movement theory.** Walzer (1985) reads Exodus as the Western paradigm of revolutionary politics: oppression, wilderness, covenant and promised land. *Prediction:* an Exodus theology turns suffering into collective agency and into building a covenanted community, so faith and the sufferers' own institution rise together. Said (1986) answered with a "Canaanite reading": the same narrative licenses conquest. Who reads Exodus therefore depends on position (section 3.6). Christian Smith's *Disruptive Religion* (1996) shows how religion supplies movements with motivation, organization, identity and legitimacy. Morris (1984) and McAdam (1982) show the Black church as the indigenous base of the civil rights movement. *Prediction:* sufferer-controlled churches become infrastructure for resistance; establishment churches allied with power dampen it.

**Lincoln and Hirschman.** In "Notes toward a Theory of Religion and Revolution," Lincoln (1985) distinguishes religions of the status quo, of resistance, of revolution and of counterrevolution. The last type, an establishment religion that mobilizes to defend or restore the old order, suits the model's church allied with perpetrators. Authority, he argues, is an effect of the fit between speaker, setting and audience. It corrodes when the audience withdraws its trust (Lincoln 1989; 1994; 2003). Hirschman's (1970) exit, voice and loyalty complete the picture. Members of a failing organization may protest from within or leave. Loyalty delays exit and makes voice more likely, and exit needs an available alternative. Dittmar and Meisenzahl (2020) find exactly this for plague: institutional change followed only once the Reformation offered one.

### 2.8 Institutional authority versus belief

**Core claims.** Chaves (1994) redefines secularization as the declining scope of religious authority, not of religion. Belief and authority can therefore move apart. Davie (1990; 1994) described Britain as "believing without belonging." Voas and Crockett (2005) found instead that belief eroded at the same rate as belonging, by generational replacement. Only about half of parental religiosity is transmitted, while absence of religion is almost always passed on.

Casanova (1994) separates three secularization theses: differentiation, decline and privatization. He shows that churches tied to the state lose, while churches that defend civil society can gain public influence. Martin (1978) argued that secularization follows church–state configurations. Where a monopoly church is allied with elites, anticlerical secularism follows. Where religion carries national identity against an outside oppressor, as in Poland and Ireland, religion strengthens. Taylor (2007) describes an age in which belief is one option among others, so suffering can be met by secular meaning (section 17).

**Predictions.** *Church role:* Martin's and Casanova's configurations are the model's church-role variable at macro scale. They are the closest prior art for it. *Faith versus institution:* Chaves allows faith to rise while religious authority shrinks. Black Death testaments, lay confraternities and lay confession in extremis fit that pattern (section 4). *Transmission:* Voas and Crockett imply that "faith migrates" only where a gathered community receives it; otherwise it decays across generations.

**Critiques.** These are theories of long-run change in modern societies. They do not model shocks. Martin's patterns are typological and were built inductively, so they risk circularity if used to explain the same cases. Bruce (2011) defends classical secularization against all of them.

### 2.9 Persecution and scapegoating

**Core claims.** Moore (1987) argued that between about 950 and 1250 clerical and royal elites built a "persecuting society." Jews, heretics and lepers were persecuted to consolidate power, not out of popular frenzy. Girard (1982; English 1986) opens *The Scapegoat* with Guillaume de Machaut's account of the Black Death, which blames Jews for poisoning wells. He reads it as a "text of persecution" with recurring stereotypes: social crisis, accusations of crimes that dissolve difference, victims marked by signs, and violence that restores unanimity.

**Predictions.** Collective suffering raises the risk of violence against a marked minority, and the violence can briefly reinforce the persecutors' solidarity and piety. Terror management theory predicts the same through worldview defence (section 2.6). For the model, the theory describes the perpetrators' side. When the church sides with persecutors, the persecuted are pushed toward rejection of its faith. The Jews of 1348–51 (section 14) and enslaved Africans facing a proslavery church are the relevant cases.

**Critiques.** Nirenberg (1996) argues that medieval violence was strategic, local and part of coexistence, not the eruption of a universal mechanism. Cohn (2007) finds that elites and town councils often led the Black Death burnings. Cohn (2018) shows that many epidemics produced compassion, not hate. Quantitative work finds that persecution was most intense where authority over Jews was contested (Finley and Koyama 2018) and where states were weak (Anderson, Johnson, and Koyama 2017). It was less likely where plague mortality was higher and Jews were economically needed (Jedwab, Johnson, and Koyama 2019). The church was divided: the papacy condemned the massacres while some prince-bishops profited (section 4.5).

The table shows a division of labour. Demand theories predict that suffering raises religiosity, but not where it goes. Authority theories predict which institutions gain or lose, but rarely start from suffering. Power theories explain interpretation, but treat "the church" as a single actor.

**How the model combines and goes beyond these theories.** The model takes the demand step from deprivation, coping and insecurity theory. It takes the power variable from Weber and Scott, the theology variable from the theodicy literature (section 3), and the church-role variable from Martin, Casanova and the religious-economy work on care. It goes beyond them in four ways. First, it makes the outcome two-dimensional: faith and institutional trust are measured separately, as Chaves and the coping psychology already allow but seldom apply to history. Second, it disaggregates "the church" into the sufferers' own body, a failing establishment and a church allied with perpetrators. Weber, Berger and Bourdieu each assume one hierocracy. Third, it treats who controls the remedy as the hinge between theology and institution. A punishment frame empowers whoever administers penance, while an Exodus frame needs no mediator. Fourth, it adds recurrence and transmission, which most theories treat only implicitly. Weber's routinization, Iannaccone's religious capital, Voas and Crockett's transmission rates and Eyerman's second-generation trauma become explicit variables. The cost is that several variables are endogenous: power shapes which theology prevails, and theology shapes which church people trust. The comparative design (section 14) must therefore separate them with Brazil and the shadow cases rather than assume independence.

### Implications for the model

- **Supports the demand step, with limits.** Deprivation, insecurity and coping theories agree that suffering raises religious demand. Recent micro-level and disaster studies show the rise is often small and short in secure societies (Höllinger and Muckenhuber 2019; Frijters et al. 2024).
- **Supports the faith/institution split.** Chaves's authority concept, the RCOPE and Exline's struggle domains all separate the relationship with God from the relationship with religious people. The model should borrow their measures when coding sources (section 14.7).
- **Sharpens church role.** Martin and Casanova supply macro precedents. Insurance studies add care capacity as a measurable sub-variable: demand flows to bodies that deliver aid.
- **Adds a fourth condition: an available alternative.** Hirschman, Glock and Dittmar and Meisenzahl (2020) agree that exit requires a rival body. "Faith migrates" only where one exists or can be built.
- **Recurrence and transmission need mechanisms, not just dates.** Routinization, plausibility structures, religious capital and correspondence attachment specify how faith survives a generation.
- **Weber is the main prior-art risk.** Presenting the model as an operationalized extension of Weber, with the third variable and the split outcome, answers it.
