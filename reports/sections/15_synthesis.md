## 15. Synthesis: why suffering strengthens faith or redistributes trust

Collective suffering rarely drives Christians away from God; it redistributes devotion toward whichever body holds the prescribed remedy and delivers care. Where that body is the sufferers' own, faith and institution rise together. Where the establishment fails or sides with perpetrators, devotion moves to lay or sufferer-controlled bodies. Rejection follows only when a rival faith is alive.

### 15.1 The refined model

**Demand step.** Suffering almost always raises demand for meaning, intercession and community. Deprivation, coping and insecurity theories agree on this step, but none says where the demand goes (section 2). In secure societies the rise is small and short (Höllinger and Muckenhuber 2019; Frijters et al. 2024). Prayer searches rose in early 2020, then faded in most regions by about October (Bentzen 2021).

**Conditions.** Seven conditions decide where the demand flows.

1. **Power position.** Sufferers may be a ruling majority, subjects, a tolerated minority or enslaved. Stratum shapes which theodicy a group adopts (Weber 1946b *[verify]*; Scott 1990).
2. **Perpetrator's religion.** Suffering inflicted by outsiders of another faith binds sufferers to their own church. Suffering inflicted by co-religionists pushes devotion toward alternatives (section 6).
3. **Theology of suffering, and who controls the remedy it prescribes.** A punishment frame empowers whoever administers penance; an Exodus frame needs no mediator (section 2.10). After 1348, punishment theology bound people to the clergy because the church held the remedy (section 4).
4. **Church role, including capacity to deliver care.** The church may be the sufferers' own community, a failing establishment or an ally of the perpetrators. Trust after 1348 frayed where no priest came to the deathbed (Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46).
5. **An organised alternative.** Exit requires a rival body (Hirschman 1970). Plague outbreaks changed German church law only once an organised Protestant alternative existed (Dittmar and Meisenzahl 2020).
6. **Duration and recurrence.** Durable change in Tuscan and Umbrian bequests followed the 1363 recurrence, not 1348 (Cohn 1992). Recurrence turns a shock into a repeated test of institutional performance (section 11.6).
7. **Transmission through community.** Faith that leaves an institution survives a generation only if a gathered community receives it (Voas and Crockett 2005; section 16).

**Outcomes.** Six outcomes follow, plus a minor branch.

- **A. Faith and institution rise together.** The church is the sufferers' own, and the perpetrator stands outside it. Cases: the Irish Famine, the Armenian church, the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) under martial law.
- **B. Devotion redistributed within the church.** Lay bodies gain inside the institution, and remedies are delegated or absorbed. Cases: confraternities, lay confession in extremis, perpetual masses after 1363 (Henderson 1994; Cohn 1992).
- **C. Devotion migrates to sufferer-controlled bodies outside the establishment.** This is exit: the invisible institution, the AME and Black Baptists after 1865, Rwandan Pentecostalism (sections 5 and 7).
- **D. Rejection of Christianity.** Christianity is the perpetrators' religion and a rival sacred system is alive, as in the Andean Taki Onqoy (Stern 1982b; Millones 1990).
- **E. Functional displacement.** State and medicine take over explanation and protection, while churches keep consolation (Rosenberg 1962; Neiman 2002).
- **F. Institutional self-correction.** The church admits failure and shares control of the failing ministry with the neglected group, as in Acts 6:1–7.
- **Minor branch: loss of faith.** Some read suffering as unredeemable and move to humanism or a transformed theism (Jones [1973] 1998; Pinn 1995). Before 1877 the branch is thinly documented; one bereaved mother prays, "God has hid himself from me" (Jacobs 1861, ch. XIII).

Two contrasts carry most weight. Brazil and Rwanda set against the PCT separate church role from power position, the confound of the US slave case (sections 5 and 7). Korea shows that an outside perpetrator does not guarantee outcome A: a compliant establishment lost its resisters (Kim Sung-Gun 1989; section 10). The PCT's outcome A is loyalty within its constituency more than numbers, since its 1955–65 growth campaign predates its political turn (section 7).

### 15.3 Avoiding circularity

Church role must be coded from records of control and care, never from whether people stayed. If "own church" is inferred from loyalty, the model only restates its outcome. Five indicators, each recorded independently of the outcome, carry the coding.

| Indicator | What it shows | Plague sources | Slavery and Atlantic sources | Other cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who ordains or appoints clergy | Whether sufferers can supply their own ministers | Ordination lists (Robinson 2019); episcopal registers (Holmes 1896) | AME bishops from 1816 (Newman 2008; Dickerson 2020); Lee's wait for a preaching licence (Lee 1849); elected brotherhood officers (Russell-Wood 1974) | PCT General Assembly yearbooks (總會年鑑), not yet compiled (section 7) |
| Who owns property | Whether exit means leaving buildings and endowments behind | Bequests to church institutions (Cohn 1992) | Southern Methodist deeds at Nashville, 1866 (*Christian Recorder* 1866–69); religious orders as slaveholders (Schwartz 1982) | Not yet coded |
| Who administers sacraments and rites | Who holds the remedy itself | Lay confession delegated and reclaimed (Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46); flagellant penance and funerals (Lemaître 1906, 240, 251–52); bull against self-governing penance (Raynaldus 1880, 25:470–72) | White-controlled and Black-led worship in 31 and 26 of 51 coded WPA narratives (Federal Writers' Project 1941); Turner's self-baptism (Turner and Gray 1831); brotherhood burial (Reis 1991) | Kakure lay tradition without clergy (Whelan 1996; Miyazaki 2014) |
| Who controls funds | Who pays for, or profits from, the remedy | Clergy's profit from death-bells at Tournai (Lemaître 1906, 255); curial redistribution of funds (Rollo-Koster 2015) | Community-paid funeral sermons (Federal Writers' Project 1941); brotherhood manumission aid (Russell-Wood 1974) | US relief channelled through Catholic districts in Taiwan (Madsen 2012) |
| Documented care delivery | Capacity to deliver care | No priests for the dying (Wilkins 1737, 2:745); curates dying at work (Lemaître 1906, 258); Hôtel-Dieu sisters (Géraud 1843, 2:211–12) | African Church nursing and burial in 1793 (Jones and Allen 1794, 3, 15–16) | Christian nursing in epidemics (Stark 1996); PCT disaster ministry (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1999; 2009) |

Three rules follow. First, code each indicator from records dated before the outcome is measured. Second, code per institution and per phase, since roles change: Korea's sufferers' church became an establishment, and Ireland's became the harming institution (section 6).

Third, name refuting results in advance. An own church that delivers care yet loses its members would count against the model. So would devotion moving to lay bodies before any recorded establishment failure, the hoop test of section 11.10. Outcomes then need paired indicators, one for devotional intensity and one for its allocation between channels (section 11.7).

### 15.4 Rival explanations and how they are tested

Six rivals can explain the same outcomes without the model's conditions, and each implies a distinct test.

| Rival | Claim | Test | Current evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deliverance or emancipation timing | Formal commitment followed freedom, not suffering (Fountain 2010) | Phase-indexed comparison; date conversion and institutional moves separately (section 11.6) | Five of seven dated WPA conversions fall after 1865, yet 26 of 51 narratives report Black-led worship before. Supports a postwar institutional exodus more than a postwar first conversion |
| Fading after the danger passes | "Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio" (li Muisis, in Lemaître 1906, 258) | Require change that outlasts the waves | Testamentary change after 1363 outlasts single waves (Cohn 1992); acute peaks alone prove little |
| Provision or relief | Affiliation follows material aid and falls when aid ends (Madsen 2012) | Separate care delivered from sufferer control of the body delivering it | Madsen attributes decline to lost dependence on US relief and paternal authority, not materialism. In 1793 the Black church gave relief (Jones and Allen 1794). Taiwan's "flour religion" claim has no scholarly source |
| Political alignment | Trust tracks partisan or national alignment, not suffering | Compare coalition insiders and outsiders within one church | The AME charged the Southern church with treason (*Christian Recorder*, 13 Mar. 1869). US nones grew among liberals and moderates (Hout and Fischer 2002; 2014) |
| Demographic composition | Post-crisis piety reflects who died or left (Miller 1975) | Compare within regions against pre-crisis baselines | Pre-Famine attendance was high in the east, low in the west (Miller 1975). Larkin (2014) dates beginnings to parish missions of 1825–46 |
| Functional displacement | State and medicine absorb the demand, so church role is moot | Code whether a secular competitor existed | Cholera moved from judgment to sanitation between 1832 and 1866 (Rosenberg 1962). Most Americans report no COVID impact on religious life (Pew Research Center 2025b) |

Deliverance is the strongest rival, and it overlaps the model. Freedom gave sufferers control of churches, so the test is whether commitment follows control without deliverance. Brazil's brotherhoods under slavery and the Cyprianic Christians without state power supply that test (Russell-Wood 1974; Stark 1996).

Political alignment is the main proximate driver today (section 16). It belongs in the model as a modern form of "the church sides with power," not as a replacement for it. Displacement appears only from the eighteenth century, so it limits the model's reach in modern secure societies (section 6).

### 15.5 What remains open

1. **Does control of the remedy predict outcomes independently of care delivery?** Article 2, "Who Owns the Remedy?", tests this on plague and slavery, with Brazil as the separating case.
2. **Did deliverance or suffering drive formal commitment?** Article 2 runs phase-indexed tests for 1348–1400 and 1760–1900, with the time window extended past emancipation.
3. **Within one state and one faith, does church role rather than power position set the outcome?** Article 3 compares the PCT, party-state-aligned churches and Catholic districts in Taiwan, 1947–1990.
4. **Does institutional self-correction prevent or reverse migration?** Article 3 treats the PCT's 1990 apology as a test case for outcome F.
5. **Does belief persist while trust in religious organizations falls, and does perceived politicization drive the fall?** Article 4 tests this in the Taiwan Social Change Survey, 2009–2018 (H1–H3).
6. **Does transmission require a gathered community?** Article 4 tests whether Christian identification depends on childhood participation (H4).
7. **How common is the loss-of-faith branch, and can belief be measured apart from belonging?** Article 1 maps this evidence gap; no article in the series yet tests it.
