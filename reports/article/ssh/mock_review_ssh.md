## Calibration: what *Social Science History* publishes

**Basis.** Crossref returned 226 records for ISSN 0145-5532 from 2022 onward. I read the titles and abstracts of the first 60 records, the titles of 54 further records found by a keyword search of the rest (religion, slavery, epidemic, comparison, text, coding, wills, guilds), and the full abstracts of 14 articles closest to this manuscript. I did not read full texts. Statements about designs rest on abstracts, or on titles where marked, and nothing here describes how any article reports coding validity in its body. The author-instructions page on Cambridge Core was read through a web fetch.

**Journal rules that bear on this manuscript**

- Main text at most 12,000 words, including footnotes and references. Abstract at most 250 words.
- Use of AI to analyze data must be declared in the methods section, with the tool's name and version, the dates of use, and how it was used.
- Deposit of data in a repository is encouraged. The page states no mandatory replication policy.

**Typical designs, 2022–2026**

- Most articles are quantitative historical studies with an explicit outcome to explain: demography and child mortality (a 2023 special issue), economic history, elections, labor.
- Comparative-historical articles choose cases to answer a stated puzzle. George (2023) compares four Protestant denominations to explain why some split over slavery. Lange (2025) tests a stated framework on the countries of Zomia. Dilling (2022) selects a negative case on purpose. Chouhy (2022) and Adereth (2024) are paired-country comparisons, to judge by their titles.
- Text-as-data articles work with large corpora and describe the method in the abstract: Grajzl and Murrell (2024), topic models on 57,863 texts; Sabaté and Goenaga (2025), automated analysis of parliamentary debates; Krawatzek and Moreton (2025), more than 12,000 letters read at macro, meso, and micro levels.
- Mixed-method articles tie interviews to a quantitative frame. Elman et al. (2025) pair a purposive sample of WPA and Behind the Veil interviews with complete-count census data for one state.
- Source-critical quantitative work is welcome. Lemercier and Trivellato (2022) present a "replicable analysis" of notarial records across six cities.

**How religion is treated.** Religion appears as an institution or a variable inside an explanatory design: denominational authority and schism (George 2023), revival and reform societies (Masters and Young 2022), church establishment and liberty (Kulkarni and Pfaff 2022), denomination and child mortality (Scalone, Pozzi, and Kennedy 2023), missions and women's activism (Won 2026). None of the abstracts frames a question theologically.

**What this implies for the manuscript**

- The topic and the mix of texts and coded data fit the journal.
- A typology offered without an outcome to explain is unusual here. Reviewers will ask what varies and what would count against the scheme.
- Two cases chosen for maximum difference will draw more questions than the paired or negative-case designs the journal usually prints.
- No abstract in the sample reports coding by a language model. The journal's AI rule means the declaration will be read closely, and the manuscript's placeholder "[TOOL, VERSION, DATES]" does not yet meet it.

## Reviewer A

*Field: historical sociology of religion; comparative-historical methods; typology building.*

### Summary of the submission

The manuscript asks how lay people respond toward faith and toward the church after collective suffering. It proposes a concept, "who holds the remedy," measured by five indicators (ordination, property, rites, funds, care). From two "deep" cases it derives four types of lay response, three kinds of answer by authorities, and five "regularities" (G1–G5). Six "shadow cases" from secondary literature are added. The evidence is a set of contemporary texts and three coded datasets. A language model did the collection, coding, and analysis. The author states that the study is descriptive and makes no causal claim.

### Strengths

- The distinction between a response toward faith and a response toward a church is drawn clearly and kept throughout.
- The idea of coding control of religious practice from records of ordination, property, rites, funds, and care is useful. It could travel to other cases.
- Classifying the answers of authorities together with lay action is a real gap in the supply-side literature.
- The manuscript is unusually candid about its limits. It reports results that weaken its own case, such as the collapse of the interview association under a stricter measure.
- Rival accounts get a section of their own.

### Major concerns

**A1. The typology is presented as induced, but the text shows it was imposed.**

The abstract says the article "induces a typology from two distant cases," and section 1 says "The aim is inductive." Three statements in the manuscript contradict this.

- "The codebooks were written before coding" (section 3.5). For the interviews, "The codebook was written before any sampled narrative was read" (supplement S1.3). That codebook's first variable is "who controlled worship before 1865." The central dimension of the typology was therefore fixed before the data were read.
- "Both cases were also chosen with their outcomes in view" (section 3.1).
- The model "wrote the codebooks" (section 3.5). The operational form of the concept was drafted by the same tool that then applied it.

A scheme defined first, applied to cases chosen for their known outcomes, and then reported as what "the two cases yield" (section 6.1) is a demonstration of a prior concept. In the terms of Skocpol and Somers (1980), whom the author cites, this is "parallel demonstration of theory." It is not the "contrast of contexts" the author claims in section 3.1, which is a logic for bringing out what is particular to each case.

I ask for one of two things. Either give a dated account of the order in which the concept, the case choice, the codebooks, and the coding were done, and call the result a proposed typology. Or show real induction: an open first reading, categories that changed, and a record of what changed and why. Nelson (2020) gives one documented procedure for the second.

**A2. "Who holds the remedy" is not operationalized independently of the outcome.**

The claim is made three times: the indicators "can be read without knowing how people later responded" (section 1); they "are coded from records that do not depend on the response" (section 3.1); "None is inferred from whether people stayed in a church or left it" (Table 1, notes). The narrow claim holds. The broad one, that condition and response are measured apart, does not.

- Section 6.1: "In London the record of sharing is the placing of chantry endowments and the hiring of clergy by gilds." Placing endowments with lay bodies and hiring clergy are the Type 2 response ("Lay people fund, organize, and staff part of the remedy," Table 5). The response is the record of the condition.
- Table 1, indicator 1, England: "Gilds maintain or seek a chaplain of their own in 37.6% of 500 returns." This is a lay response entered as an indicator.
- Table 1, indicator 3, Plantation South: "Some worship under Black control in 92 of 206 (44.7%). Secret worship is mentioned in 28 of 400." Secret worship is the Type 3 response.
- Table 1, indicator 2, Plantation South: "A church built by enslaved people in the woods at Edenton is demolished after 1831." This is an answer by authorities.
- Table 1, indicator 1, Philadelphia: "The African Methodist Episcopal Church is formed in April 1816." This is the Type 4 outcome.
- Section 6.1 offers "110 narratives of worship under white supervision alone" as "the record of guarding." The same interviews supply the responses.

Section 3.3 says "The indicators are coded for each body and each period." No such coding appears. Table 1 is a list of evidence, with no values assigned.

Required: a matrix with one row per body and period and one column per indicator, each cell holding a value from a fixed set (for example clerical, shared, lay, contested, not recorded), a date, and a source. No source used to code a condition may be used to code a response. The condition must be dated before the response it is said to sort.

**A3. The scheme is not yet a typology in the technical sense.**

A typology needs stated dimensions and a property space (Lazarsfeld 1937; Elman 2005; Collier, LaPorte, and Seawright 2012). The "conditions" in Table 5, Panel A, mix at least five dimensions:

- whether clergy are present ("present," "scarce or stretched," "absent");
- the church's posture ("sharing," "guarding");
- whether lay action is licensed ("lay bodies allowed," "lay action unlicensed");
- whether the church is "allied with the oppressor";
- whether exit is possible, which enters only in section 7.1 ("Separation appears where a body could be built").

The limit case adds a sixth: "a rival religion alive."

The types are neither exclusive nor exhaustive. The author says so: "The types describe acts, not persons. One community, and one person, can appear in several," and "One church can stand in different conditions at different levels." If any act can be assigned to some type and any body can occupy several conditions at once, no observation can contradict the scheme.

"Guarding" needs particular attention. A church that reserves sacraments to an ordained clergy always guards the remedy. That is Weber's definition of a church, which the author quotes in section 2. In 1349 the English church was at once "sharing" (Type 1), "scarce or stretched" (Type 2), and guarding. A condition that is constant cannot sort responses.

Required: name the dimensions, cross them, show which cells are observed and which are empty, and give the rule that assigns an observation to a cell. The answers of authorities should be a separate dimension. At present "suppress" is attached only to Type 3, yet section 4.4 itself describes a Crown "suspicious of gild property" inquiring into Type 2 bodies.

**A4. Case selection and comparability are not justified.**

The reason given is that the cases "differ in almost every circumstance" and "share only the structure of interest" (section 3.1). Four problems follow.

- *The concept of collective suffering is stretched* (Sartori 1970). One case is an epidemic that killed clergy and laity alike within months. The other is a system of domination lasting generations, imposed by fellow Christians. In the first the church is a fellow victim. In the second churches are parties to the harm. Calling both "collective suffering" hides the difference that the typology then rediscovers as "alliance with the oppressor."
- *"After" has no meaning in the second case.* The question is how people respond "after collective suffering." The plague has a before and an after. Slavery, as studied here, has responses during it and after emancipation. G1 says demand "persisted or rose." For England the wills give a series. For the United States the evidence offered is that "44.7 percent of narratives that describe worship report some of it under Black control." That is a cross-section with no baseline, and it does not measure demand. Most enslaved Africans and many of their descendants were not Christians to begin with, so "persisted" misdescribes a history of conversion.
- *The units are heterogeneous.* The "English case" is London testators, gilds that are three-quarters East Anglian and Lincolnshire, one mandate from Bath and Wells, and flagellants at Tournai. The "United States case" is the plantation South, one North Carolina town, free Philadelphia, and the South after 1865. Types 1 and 2 are documented almost wholly in England. Type 3 rests on Tournai and on 28 narratives. Type 4 is American only. No type is shown in both deep cases with evidence of similar weight. A "regularity in two cases" needs the same thing observed twice.
- *The stated falsifiers are already met in the manuscript's own evidence.* Section 6.3 names "lay bodies that formed outside a church before any recorded absence or guarding." At Tournai, where Type 3 is observed, li Muisis "recorded that many curates and chaplains who heard confessions and visited the sick died" (section 4.5). The clergy were present and serving. Unless "guarding" is read so widely that it is always true, the companies formed without the stated condition. The same holds for bodies inside the church: 51 dated gilds predate 1349, 14 predate 1300, and 36 claim "time without memory" (Table 3), so Type 2 bodies existed before any recorded scarcity of clergy.

Required: state why these two cases and not others; state scope conditions; and say what observation in each case would have led to a different typology.

**A5. Two cases cannot carry five "regularities."**

The author writes that "Two cases cannot establish covariation" (section 3.1) and then states that responses "varied with who held the remedy" (G2, and the abstract). The label G and the word "regularities" promise more than the evidence lists:

- G3: "The evidence is one mandate, one bull and its enforcement, one demolished church, and 509 returns."
- G4: "The evidence for fading is thin, and it comes from Tournai and from one verse."
- G5: "The evidence is slight."

Three kinds of answer by authorities are distilled from four observations of different kinds, and the actors are not all church authorities. At Edenton the church was demolished by slaveholders. At Tournai "proclamations by the town and the king came first" (section 4.5). "Absorb" has no recorded act behind it. It is inferred from the fact that "Nothing in the returns shows suppression" (section 4.4).

G4 also contradicts the rest. The London wills show no falling back, as section 7.1 concedes. In the second case the "danger passed" at emancipation, after which religious organization grew.

Required: recast G1–G3 as propositions for testing, with scope. Remove G4 and G5 from the abstract and the list. They can be named as open questions in one paragraph.

**A6. The shadow cases do evidential work that the design says they do not do.**

Section 3.1: "They illustrate, and they carry no evidential weight for the regularities." Section 6.3 then draws inferences from them: "The shadow cases suggest that the types recur beyond the two deep cases. They also mark limits. Separation is reported in Korea, where the oppressor was not Christian. A church's alliance with power may therefore matter more than the oppressor's creed. Rejection appears where a rival sacred system was alive." The abstract repeats that they "mark the typology's reach and limits."

One row of the typology rests on shadow evidence alone. Table 5, Panel A, defines the limit case by the condition "Church identified with the oppressor; a rival religion alive." That condition comes only from the Andean entry in Panel B.

Further problems:

- The cases "were chosen from a wider review of the literature to span the conditions of interest." They were selected to fit. None was chosen because it might fail.
- Several codings rest on one work. Korea rests on an unpublished 1989 dissertation. The Andes rest on Stern (1982), with no notice of the later debate over how far Taki Onqoy was constructed by its extirpators (Ramos 2002; Heilman 2002; reviewed by Mumford 1998).
- Two entries contradict the scheme, and the text does not say so. In colonial Brazil the established church was the slaveholders' church, which is the Type 4 condition, yet the entry is coded Type 2. In Rwanda the entry is "4, partial" while "Catholicism remained the largest body."
- The Taiwan case takes about 200 words of section 7.2 and a footnote of about 100 more, and ends: "The case is therefore an instance for G5 to be examined, not evidence for it."

Required: either delete Panel B, section 7.2, and footnote 5, or rebuild the set on the logic of negative cases (Mahoney and Goertz 2004; Emigh 1997), with each case coded on the five indicators from at least two independent studies and with cases included because they are expected to fail.

**A7. The contribution is overstated against the sociology of religion.**

- The "remedy," defined as "the practices that a community's theology prescribes against suffering and death," is very close to Riesebrodt's (2010) account of religion as practices that avert misfortune and manage crises. He is not cited.
- The four types map closely onto Hirschman's loyalty, voice, and exit, with Scott's hidden transcript for Type 3. The inside/outside line is the old church–sect distinction of Troeltsch and Niebuhr. Niebuhr's *Social Sources of Denominationalism* has chapters on the churches of the disinherited and on the color line. Neither author appears.
- G2's alliance condition restates Martin (1978), whom the author cites in one sentence. Later work makes the same point with evidence: Gill (1998), Trejo (2009), Grzymala-Busse (2015).
- Gorski (2000) on church, state, and society in late-medieval and early modern Europe is the obvious bridge between the secularization debate and the English case. Chaves and Gorski (2001) is the standard assessment of supply-side evidence. Both are absent.
- Work on disaster and religiosity is represented by Bentzen (2021) alone. Sibley and Bulbulia (2012), Bentzen (2019), and Henrich et al. (2019) measure responses, which section 2 says the literature rarely does.
- The sociological literature on the Black church as an institution, from Du Bois (1903) to Lincoln and Mamiya (1990), is missing.
- This journal recently published George (2023) on how denominational authority structures shaped the slavery schisms. It bears directly on the author's point that "one church can stand in different conditions at different levels."

The three contributions claimed in section 1 should be narrowed. The indicator set is a useful checklist. The classification of authorities' answers is the most original part. The inside/outside distinction is not new.

**A8. Several claims do not match the evidence offered for them.**

- G1, "abandonment of faith is rare in the record." The author concedes that "Neither source was built to record unbelief." Wills are a pious genre. The interview sample was drawn only from narratives that pass a religion keyword screen, which removes 33.8 percent of the collection. The claim should leave the abstract.
- The abstract's three sentences beginning "Where the church..." are conditional generalizations in law-like form. They do not fit a study in which "Each is an observed regularity in two cases, not a law."
- Table 1 records the care indicator and the funds indicator as "Not recorded in the sources coded" for the plantation South. That is the setting that supplies the quantitative evidence for the second case. G2 nonetheless says responses varied with "who delivered care."

**A9. The division of labor between author and model is unclear, and part of the literature has not been read.**

Section 3.2 says "For England I read..." and "Translations from Latin are mine." Section 3.5 says "A large language model performed the data collection, coding, and analysis." The reader cannot tell who did the "close reading of contemporary texts" that the abstract promises.

Section 7.3 says: "Several monographs cited for context are known to this study through reviews and abstracts, and they are cited only for their general positions." Section 2 builds its framing ("Two readings therefore compete") on monographs. I cannot assess that framing without knowing which works were read. A journal article may not characterize a historiography from abstracts. The author should list which cited works were read in full, and read the rest or cut them.

### Minor concerns

- Section 2's "three expectations" (demand alone, supply alone, position alone) are positions no cited author holds. The paragraph can go.
- "Religious goods" and "demand" are used without definition or measure.
- Table 5, Type 1, "Answer of authorities: None needed," is not an observation.
- Table 1 merges "Free Black Philadelphia and the postwar South," two settings seventy years apart, in one column.
- The title promises "the United States under Slavery," but about a third of the case concerns free Philadelphia and the years after 1865.
- Section 3.1 says the typology "claims nothing beyond Christianity." The Andean limit case concerns a return to non-Christian deities.
- The descriptive stance sits oddly with some fifty p-values across the tables and the supplement. Table 2 and Table 3 are complete enumerations of a calendar. The author should say what chance model the tests assume or remove them.
- The manuscript is at about 11,400 of 12,000 words. The additions I ask for must be paid for by cuts. Section 7.2, footnote 5, Table 5 Panel B, and the "three expectations" paragraph are the obvious ones.

### Recommendation

**Major revision.** The concept and the classification of authorities' answers deserve development. The manuscript cannot be accepted while the typology is circular in its coding (A2), lacks a property space (A3), and is presented as induced (A1). I would want to see the revised version.

## Reviewer B

*Field: late-medieval English social history; wills, gilds, plague.*

### Summary of the submission

The English half of the manuscript uses a small set of printed episcopal and papal letters, two chronicles, a coding of all 1,979 entries in Sharpe's calendar of Husting wills for 1320–1400, and a coding of the 509 gilds in Westlake's calendar of the 1388–89 returns. It argues that devotion after the plague "stayed inside church institutions, and reached more of them," that lay people took "part of the remedy into their own hands inside the church" through gilds and chantry trusts, and that the "outside" form appears only at Tournai. Bishops are said to have "delegated and reclaimed," the pope to have "suppressed," and parishes to have "absorbed."

### Strengths

- The whole calendar is coded for the period, with no sampling.
- The author sees the main recording problem in Sharpe's calendar and reports it: before 1349 "most enrolled wills dealt with land only," and the summaries lengthen from a median of 74 to 154 words.
- The survivorship problem in the gild returns is stated plainly and given a simple calculation. The conclusion that the dates are "compatible with a modest excess of foundations after 1349 and equally with survivorship alone" is correct.
- The reading of Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate notes that it "applies established doctrine on necessity and announces nothing new."
- The massacres of Jews are named and kept outside the typology.

### Major concerns

**B1. The headline result from the wills is produced by the period boundaries, and the supplement contradicts the text.**

Section 4.3: "The steps after 1361 exceed chance (parish +20.8 points and friars +13.1, both p < .001; fraternities +6.6, p = .005)."

The second period, 1349–60, holds 549 wills. Of these, "In 1349, 356 wills were enrolled." Two-thirds of the period is one plague year. These are the wills of people who died in the epidemic, many made in haste and enrolled in bulk.

The supplement, S3.1, row "Excluding the 356 enrollments of 1349," gives for parish gifts among all wills: "9.5 / 61.1 / 63.0 / 76.4." With 1349 removed, the share is 61.1 percent in 1350–60 and 63.0 percent in 1361–75. There is no step at 1361. The break lies between 1349 and the 1350s.

A rise from 9.5 percent to about 61 percent inside a few years cannot be a change in piety. It is far more likely a change in what the court enrolled, in what plague-year enrollments included, or in what Sharpe abstracted. The main text does not mention this row.

Required:

- Show Panel B with 1349 separated from 1350–60, and 1361 separated from 1362–75.
- Plot the series by year or by five-year groups, with counts.
- Rewrite section 4.3, G1, and Table 5 Type 1, which cites "between 1349–60 and 1361–75" as its evidence.
- Remove the framing "after the second plague" unless it survives.

**B2. Sharpe's calendar is treated as if it were the rolls.**

- *An editorial boundary sits two years from the period boundary.* The reference list shows that Part 1 of the calendar covers 1258–1358 and Part 2 covers 1358–1688. The median summary rises from 98 to 129 words across periods 2 and 3. The author must test for a break at the volume boundary.
- *The coded variable is partly Sharpe's judgment.* He "left out bequests that he judged unimportant" (section 4.3). What he judged unimportant may have changed over two volumes.
- *The court enrolled devises of City property.* Cash gifts to friars, lights, and fraternities normally sat in the testament of movables, which went to the church courts. Registers of London testaments from those courts survive from the 1370s. They are the obvious external check for the last period. They are not mentioned.
- *The burial-clause filter is untested.* The clause is said to be "a sign that the part of the will concerned with the soul was enrolled and abstracted." Its frequency moves from 11.2 to 49.7 to 84.1 to 85.1 percent (Table 2, Panel A). In the first period it selects 83 of 739 wills. Those 83 are unlikely to be typical. The filter conditions on something close to the outcome.
- *Unspecified "pious uses" may substitute for named gifts.* S3.1 reports "Unspecified 'pious uses' 32.9 / 49.0 / 49.4 / 36.8." In a third to a half of wills the pious destination is left to executors. The share falls in the last period as the named categories rise. The author has not considered that named gifts may replace the unspecified formula in the abstract with no change in giving.
- *Bequest kinds track summary length.* The mean number of kinds rises from 1.49 to 3.41 while the median summary doubles. Kinds per hundred words should be reported.

The author lists comparison with the original rolls as further work (section 7.3). For a series offered as evidence, I regard a sample check against the rolls at the London Metropolitan Archives as a condition of publication. Forty wills per period would do.

**B3. Wealth, composition, and the death of heirs are not considered.**

Survivors of 1349 inherited, and wages and wealth per head rose (Hatcher 1977; Dyer 1989). Richer testators leave more kinds of bequest. The author's own quotation from Venette explains larger gifts to churches by demography: the dying "saw their own heirs die before them" (section 4.5). Neither point is brought to bear on the wills. The supplement shows that occupation was recorded. It should be used as a rough control for status.

**B4. Panel C, on who held chantry property, cannot bear the weight placed on it.**

- *The category is mislabeled.* Text and table say "Private only: wife, heirs, or executors." The supplement, S1.1, defines it as "private (wife, heirs, executors, or no holder named)." As summaries lengthen, "no holder named" must fall for mechanical reasons.
- *A rector is not a lay person.* "Parish body: rector, churchwardens, or parishioners" is added to lay corporations to give the headline "4.3 percent... to 29.4 percent." A chantry vested in a rector and wardens is held by the parish as an ecclesiastical body.
- *All corporate holders rise.* "Church corporate body" goes from 8.0 to 15.0 percent. The simplest reading is a move from mortal heirs to undying corporations after families were cut down. That is not a move from clergy to laity.
- *The legal setting is absent.* The statute of mortmain, the custom by which citizens could devise City property in mortmain, and the role of the Mayor and Commonalty as trustee are not mentioned anywhere.
- *The measure is weak.* It "rests on words that occur together in a sentence, and it was not double-coded."
- *Perpetual and temporary chantries are pooled.* The supplement shows separate sub-codes and a perpetual share of "0.9 / 2.4 / 2.0 / 6.4." A priest hired for a year and a perpetual foundation are different institutions (Wood-Legh's study remains the standard account).

Panel C supplies Table 1, indicator 2, and half of Table 5, Type 2. It should be rebuilt or removed.

**B5. The gild returns are used without the scholarship on how they were made.**

- *The source is a digest.* Westlake's appendix is a brief tabular summary, by an early-twentieth-century clergyman, of returns in Latin, French, and English. It was coded "by keyword rules on text read by optical character recognition." No error rate for the character recognition is given. Agreement for feasts was κ = 0.39. The second coder was the same model.
- *Full texts exist for many returns.* Toulmin Smith printed the English-language returns, and Barron and Wright (1995) edited the London ones. The author's cross-check with Smith compared only foundation dates ("agreed in 37" of 42). It should compare purposes, founders, and clergy.
- *The circumstances of the inquiry shaped the answers.* The returns followed the Cambridge Parliament of 1388, where the Commons attacked liveries and gilds (Tuck 1969). Gerchow (1996) studies how the returns were produced. Gilds had reason to present themselves as poor, devout, and lay. The author gives this one sentence. It bears on the two figures that carry the argument: founders are described in only 104 of 509 entries, and a cleric governs in 11.
- *The returns are not independent observations.* They were written in batches by town, often by the same clerks and to the same pattern. The supplement notes that "Cross-references in the calendar ('as in 166') are expanded." Some units are therefore copies of others. Wilson intervals on 500 independent units are too narrow. The author should cluster by place.
- *The interval on the foundation dates should go.* "82.2 percent; 95% CI 77.3–86.2" puts a precise interval on a figure that, by the author's own account, may mean nothing.
- *On this evidence the gilds do not belong to a plague story.* Eight of 509 entries mention pestilence. Purposes do not differ by period of foundation. Where they lean, older gilds are more concerned with the dead: masses 74.5 against 58.4 percent, chaplains 51.0 against 36.5 percent (S3.2). The Type 2 condition in Table 5 is "Clergy scarce or stretched; lay bodies allowed." Nothing connects these gilds to a scarcity of clergy. Parish fraternities were an old and general institution (Rosser 2015; Hanawalt and McRee 1992; McRee 1993; Bainbridge 1996).
- *"Absorb" names no act.* The gilds were never outside the parish to be absorbed. The documented act of authority toward them in 1388–89 is the Crown's hostile inquiry. The extension of mortmain to gilds followed in 1391. The author's own example, a chaplain kept "to assist the parson daily," shows cooperation.

**B6. The period ends in 1400, yet Wycliffite dissent is not mentioned.**

Section 4.5: "The English texts read here supply no documented case of English lay people taking the remedy outside the church."

From about 1380 England has the best-known case of the kind: lay groups reading Scripture in English, doubting confession to priests, and attacking endowed prayer (Hudson 1988). It arose in a well-staffed church, which bears on the "absent or guarding" condition. The rising of 1381 and its anticlericalism fall in the period too.

The mechanism the author cares about has a famous contemporary witness. In the Prologue to *Piers Plowman*, parsons and parish priests ask their bishop for leave to live in London and sing for silver, their parishes being poor "sith the pestilence tyme." Knighton's account of the men ordained after 1349 is another.

The author should either confront Lollardy as a candidate for Types 3 and 4, or end the English case in 1375 and say why. As written, Tournai is imported to fill an English gap. It is called "comparison only," yet all the medieval evidence for Type 3 in Table 5 comes from Tournai, and G3's instance of suppression is the papal bull.

**B7. The flagellant material lacks its historiography and undercuts the typology.**

- Lay flagellation began in Italy in 1260. There it was largely taken into confraternities inside the church (Henderson 1978). The same lay practice was absorbed in one region and suppressed in another. That contrast is a better test of G3 than anything in the manuscript, and it shows that "outside" is not a property of the lay act.
- At Tournai the clergy were serving and dying, on the author's own citation of li Muisis. The condition "church absent" is not met in the one medieval place where Type 3 is observed.
- Footnote 3 says: "I have not verified the title *Inter sollicitudines*, which some modern works give it." This should be checked against a modern register or edition, or the title should be dropped.

**B8. The episcopal evidence is thin and one-sided.**

- One mandate from one diocese carries the label "delegate and reclaim." The author concedes that confession to a lay person in extremity was established teaching. Nothing was delegated, since a lay hearer could not absolve. Nothing was reclaimed, since the duty to confess again to one's priest was the ordinary rule. The label implies a strategy where the text shows a pastoral reminder.
- The church also extended the remedy. In 1348–49 the pope granted the dying a confessor of their choice and plenary remission at the hour of death. One such grant for York is printed in the pages of Raine that the author cites for other purposes. These grants are the opposite of guarding and need equal space.
- The quantitative evidence on absence lies in the bishops' registers: institutions to benefices and ordinations (Hamilton Thompson 1914 for York; Dohar 1995 for Hereford; Robinson 2019, whom the author cites). In many dioceses vacancies were filled fast. The author uses none of it.
- *Effrenata* of 1350 and 1362 is read as evidence of priests' "unwillingness." It is wage regulation, parallel to the labor legislation (Putnam 1915). It should be named and read in that setting.
- The standard short assessments are not cited: Harper-Bill's essay on the English church after the Black Death in Ormrod and Lindley (1996), Swanson (1989), and Horrox (1994), whose sourcebook translates most of the texts used here.

**B9. The historiography is framed as a contest that specialists do not recognize, and part of it is known at second hand.**

Section 2: "older syntheses held that clerical deaths and failures damaged the standing of the church. Later studies doubt a general collapse... Two readings therefore compete: devotion redistributed within the church, or trust in the church collapsed."

No current specialist argues for collapse. The live questions are different: how far wills can index piety at all (Burgess 1987; Burgess and Kümin 1993), the character of fraternity (Rosser; Barron), Cohn's thesis on remembrance, anticlericalism and Lollardy, and the quality of the post-plague clergy.

Burgess (1987) must be confronted directly. A will records a fraction of a testator's pious provision. Much was arranged in life or left to executors by word of mouth.

The comparison with Cohn (1992) should be deleted. Cohn's argument concerns the distribution of value across bequests and the turn to lasting memorials. The author's codes "record presence, not sums." A count of bequest kinds in an editor's abstract cannot speak to it, as the author half admits ("no test of Cohn").

Section 7.3 says that "Several monographs cited for context are known to this study through reviews and abstracts." I need to know whether Cohn, Duffy, Henderson, and Platt were read.

**B10. Earlier work on these same sources is not acknowledged.**

- Barron's study of the London parish fraternities draws on wills and on the 1388–89 returns.
- Röhrkasten (1996; 2004) studies Londoners' bequests to the friars.
- Thomson (1965) studies London piety and charity from wills.
- Sloane (2011) uses the Husting wills for the plague years.

The series for fraternities (2.4 to 23.4 percent) and friars (6.0 to 38.9 percent) must be set beside their findings. The England datasets are not new ground.

**B11. Statistical practice.**

- Table 2, Panel B, prints 24 tests on 12 rows with no adjustment, on a complete enumeration. The author should state the chance model or drop the tests.
- Wills cluster by family, parish, and plague year. Gild returns cluster by town and clerk. No interval allows for this.
- A descriptive model would help more than the tests: each bequest kind against period, summary length, sex, clerical status, occupation group, and volume.

**B12. Coding of the medieval sources.**

- The rule-based coding of the wills is reproducible, which I welcome. The "blind second reading" by the same model is not validation by a reader.
- For parish gifts the rules gave 8 false positives and 5 false negatives in 198 entries. The supplement says these "arise from churches named only as the site of a chantry." The parish code is therefore contaminated by the chantry code. "Rule errors were not corrected afterward."
- "Named chaplain" has κ = 0.57. No human has read any coded entry.
- A graduate student with palaeographic training is not needed for the calendar. A competent reader could check 200 entries in two days.

**B13. The word "remedy."**

Footnote 1 says the term "is analytic and is not taken from the sources," and notes one use of *remedium* in li Muisis. But *pro remedio animae* is the stock phrase of medieval pious giving. Chantries and obits were, in their own Latin, remedies of the soul. The disclaimer suggests distance from the originals. The term should be grounded in that usage or the footnote rewritten.

The definition also omits the viaticum and anointing, which Ralph's mandate addresses, and indulgences.

### Minor concerns

- Section 4.4 cites "certs. 180, 182" for the chaplain's salary and election. Table 1 cites "certs. 180, 213."
- Locators mix Westlake's page numbers (section 4.4: "1919: 199," "1919: 184") and certificate numbers (supplement: 279, 203).
- Table 3 accounts for 300 dated gilds, 130 undated, and 36 "time without memory." That is 466 of 509. The other 43 are not explained.
- "Fabric or ornaments of a church | 73 | not reported." The percentage should be given.
- "Burial, at least, was provided" (section 4.2). By whom? The new London cemeteries of 1349 were founded by the bishop of London and by a lay knight, Sir Walter Manny. A layman buying consecrated ground for the plague dead is the kind of act the "care" indicator should record.
- The start date of 1320 is not explained.
- "The testators are Londoners with property" should read: holders of City real property, mostly citizens.
- "1369, the third pestilence: no rise" raises the question why the period boundaries stand where they do.
- The section on the massacres of Jews is right to stand. It could be shorter.

### Recommendation

**Reject in the present form.** The English half does not meet the standard of source criticism expected for this material. Its main quantitative claim does not survive the author's own supplement (B1). The gild evidence is not tied to the plague (B5). The typology's medieval "outside" type rests on a Flemish town while England's own dissent is left out (B6). I would read a recast version that treated the calendar as a calendar, checked a sample against the rolls, and engaged the work on London wills and fraternities.

## Reviewer C

*Field: African American religious history; the WPA narratives; content analysis and language-model coding.*

### Summary of the submission

The American half rests on a planter-minister's handbook, narratives and pamphlets by Black authors, three issues of the AME weekly, and a coded sample of 400 WPA interviews. The sample is a state-stratified random draw from the 1,418 narratives, of 2,143 segmented, that pass a religion keyword screen. A language model wrote the codebook, coded the narratives from keyword-selected extracts, and ran the analysis. A separate session of the same model recoded 40 narratives from full texts. The headline figures are that 92 of 206 narratives describing worship report some of it under Black control, that obedience-only preaching is reported in 25 of 37, and that stated faith before freedom is associated with Black control of worship (odds ratio 4.06).

### Strengths

- The sample is random, stratified, and seeded. Every code carries a verbatim passage and a locator.
- The author reports what weakens the case: the odds ratio falls to 1.81 and loses significance under a stricter measure, and "Much of it arises because one passage describes both a meeting and the people praying in it."
- The second coder is described honestly as "a separate session of the same model... It was not made by a human."
- Positive testimony is kept in view: "At least 39 of 174 narratives speak well of white preachers or of a master's religion."
- The 1794 pamphlet of Jones and Allen is read as a defense against Carey, which is right.
- The treatment of Fountain is fair on the narrow point that the sample cannot test his thesis.

### Major concerns

**C1. The coding by a language model has been checked for consistency and has not been validated.**

*What is reported.* A codebook written in advance. Verbatim passages verified against the source by script. A second pass on 40 narratives by another session of the same model, with κ from 0.64 to 1.00.

*What is missing.*

- **A human-coded standard.** "No human coder has yet checked the codings" (section 3.6). The norm in text analysis is to validate against human labels and to report precision and recall for each category (Grimmer and Stewart 2013; Krippendorff 2018). The recent work on coding with language models says the same with more force (Ziems et al. 2024; Than et al. 2025; Pangakis and Wolken 2025). Agreement between two sessions of one model measures reliability. It does not measure validity, and errors shared by both sessions are invisible to it. The author knows this: "two sessions of one model may share habits of reading."
- **Model, version, dates.** Section 3.5 and the supplement read "[TOOL, VERSION, DATES]." The journal requires these, and they do not identify the author. Closed models change or are withdrawn, which limits replication (Ollion et al. 2024; Palmer, Smith, and Spirling 2024).
- **Prompts and settings.** The supplement gives codebook summaries. It does not give the instructions the model received, the text it was shown, or any sampling settings. It does not say whether the same input gives the same codes on a second run (Reiss 2023 reports that it may not).
- **Like-for-like comparison.** The first coding "used keyword-selected extracts, capped at about 3,200 characters." The second read "each narrative in full." The two passes differ in input, so the comparison confounds coder and text. In "all 20 disagreements on substantive variables, the first coding has no code or a neutral code where the second has a substantive one," and "5 arise from text outside the keyword extract." The extract method misses content in a known direction. Calling the counts "lower bounds" repairs the marginal figures. It does not repair associations between two codes drawn from the same keyword windows.
- **Enough positive cases.** In the 40 recoded narratives the positive counts are 3 for Black-led worship, 6 for secret worship, 1 for forbidden worship, and 5 for obedience preaching (S2.3). A κ of 1.00 on three positives has a wide interval, which should be shown. With prevalence this low, κ should be reported with a prevalence-adjusted index (Byrt, Bishop, and Carlin 1993; Gwet 2008).
- **An untested code is still interpreted.** "The code for prayer for freedom could not be tested, because it did not occur in the 40." Section 5.2 nonetheless reports "13 narratives, 10 of them among those that report Black control."
- **Contamination.** The WPA narratives and the scholarship on them are almost certainly in the model's training data. The model may code toward the canonical reading, Raboteau's "invisible institution." Only a human standard can show whether it does.
- **One agent at every stage.** The model "wrote the codebooks, drew the random samples... applied the codes, and wrote and ran the analysis scripts." The script that "verified each interview passage against the source text" checks that a quotation exists. It does not check that the quotation supports the code.
- **The screen.** The keyword list is not given in the supplement. The narratives render speech in dialect spellings chosen by interviewers. The author should give the list and estimate what the screen missed by coding a random sample of the 725 excluded narratives.

**C2. The source criticism of the WPA collection stops short of the field's standard.**

- *Edition.* The text is "the Project Gutenberg transcription of the Library of Congress deposit." The supplement adds: "The supplements later edited by Rawick and the Virginia interviews published separately are not included." State offices edited narratives before sending them to Washington, and Musher (2001) shows how. Stewart (2016) treats the politics of the project's writers and editors. Rawick's supplement series printed thousands of pages held back in state files, above all from Texas and Mississippi. Where two versions survive they differ.
- *Two of the four quoted narrators are from Texas.* Susan Merritt and William Mathews are both cited to "XVI, Texas, part 3" (S4). Texas supplies 61 of the 400. The author should compare the quoted passages with the supplement-series versions, or choose other quotations, and report results with Texas excluded.
- *The most relevant corpora are absent.* The interviews by the all-Black Virginia unit are in *Weevils in the Wheat* (Perdue, Barden, and Phillips 1976). The sample holds 3 Virginia narratives, though Nat Turner's Virginia frames the chapter. The Fisk collection of conversion accounts, *God Struck Me Dead* (Johnson 1969), was gathered by a Black interviewer in the late 1920s. It is the single best body of testimony on "faith before freedom" and on the timing of conversion. Cade (1935) is another early Black-led collection.
- *Interviewer effects.* The author finds 7 of 21 negative judgments with Black interviewers against 15 of 153 otherwise, and concludes that "criticism of white religion is understated in the collection." The supplement shows how fragile this is: "Two of them crossed p = 0.05 only after six interviewer attributions were corrected. One reclassified narrative carries obedience preaching, a negative attitude, and a postwar Black-run church at once." Black interviewers are "concentrated in Arkansas (30 of 53) and Florida (12)." The conclusion has been the standard view since Blassingame (1975) and Escott (1979). Escott coded the collection by hand and measured the effect of the interviewer's race on a far larger base. The author should cite his figures and drop the p-values.
- *Interviewers as clusters.* Individual interviewers produced many narratives each, with their own questions and their own rendering of speech. Narratives are not independent observations. No interval in Table 4 allows for this.
- *Age and memory.* The median age was 83, "so the typical informant was about eleven in 1865." Age is "recorded for 301." A child's account of adults' secret meetings is second-hand even when it is not flagged as "hearsay only." The literature on memory in these interviews should be used (Yetman 1967, 1984; Woodward 1974; Bailey 1980; Spindel 1996).
- *The prompt.* The questionnaire asked "whether the slaves had a church on the plantation" (S3.3). The 206 narratives that "describe how worship was organized" are largely answers to that prompt. The 194 that do not are non-responses, and nothing follows from their silence.
- *State of interview and state of enslavement differ.* The sample includes Indiana 10, Ohio 7, Oklahoma 18, and Missouri 15. Many narrators were enslaved elsewhere. Arkansas and Texas together supply 155 of 400, and they describe late slavery on the cotton frontier. The texts in sections 5.1 and 5.3 come from Georgia in the 1840s, North Carolina, and Maryland. "The association holds within states" stratifies by where the interview took place.
- *The count of narratives.* The Library of Congress describes the collection as more than 2,300 accounts. The author parsed 2,178 entries and segmented 2,143. The difference needs a sentence.

**C3. Denominators.**

The figure 44.7 percent is conditional twice: on passing the keyword screen (1,418 of 2,143) and on describing how worship was organized (206 of 400). As a share of the sample it is 23.0 percent. That is acceptable if stated each time. Three uses go beyond it.

- G1 offers it as evidence that "demand for religious goods persisted or rose." It measures neither demand nor change.
- Type 3 rests on "28 of the 400 narratives," which is 7 percent of a screened sample.
- "White-supervised only" (110) means that the narrator mentioned nothing else. It does not mean that nothing else took place.

The intervals "reflect sampling only." They ignore the stratified design and the clustering by interviewer.

**C4. The association in Table 4, Panel C, should leave the main text.**

A report of a prayer meeting is a report of people praying. The author's own checks show the association to be largely definitional. The odds ratio is 4.06 for any stated faith, 2.06 when the faith passage is not the worship passage, and 1.81 (interval 0.88–3.72) for the narrator's own faith or a relative's. A Mantel–Haenszel estimate and five robustness rows add apparatus to a near-tautology. Section 5.2 opens with "about four times the odds" and ends with "association within testimony." G2 then cites it. Readers will remember the first number.

The same applies to "Negative judgments cluster with obedience preaching. They occur in 14 of 23 such narratives and in none of the 9." The sentence that reports obedience preaching is usually the negative judgment itself, as in the Mathews quotation. Two codes are being drawn from one utterance.

**C5. The frame leaves out those who never came in, and the scholarship on them.**

Table 5 has a limit case, "rejection," and says of it: "Not observed in the deep cases." Section 6.3 adds that "Rejection appears where a rival sacred system was alive."

- A rival sacred system was alive in the American South. Conjure and root work ran alongside and inside Black Christianity (Chireau 2003). African-derived practice persisted in the Lowcountry (Creel 1988; Young 2007). Muslims were among the enslaved (Gomez 1998; Diouf 1998).
- How far the enslaved were Christians at all is a standing debate. Butler (1990) wrote of an "African spiritual holocaust." Raboteau, Sobel (1979), and Frey and Wood trace a slow and partial conversion. Fountain argues that most were not Christians before freedom.
- The category the typology lacks is non-adoption. People who never accepted the masters' religion did not "reject" it or "abandon" it. The author's own finding points there: "The three denials in the interview sample concern not having religion in slavery, not rejecting it."
- The coding could not see any of this. The screen kept only narratives with Christian vocabulary. No code records conjure, charms, spirits, or Islam.

G1's claim that "abandonment of faith is rare in the record" is therefore produced by the frame. It should be withdrawn or restated as a property of the sources.

On Fountain, the author should engage his evidence and not only his thesis. His figure for narrators with a datable conversion rests on a much larger base than "28 of the 400." The reviews of his book should be used to state the criticisms of his method.

**C6. "The church" in the plantation South is three different actors.**

Table 1 gives as the first indicator: "Masters may forbid meetings and instruction and choose the churches attended." A master is not a church. After 1831 state laws restricted Black preaching and assembly. A legislature is not a church either. The denominations varied. They licensed Black exhorters, kept Black deacons and watchmen, and in several Southern towns housed African Baptist congregations with a large measure of self-government before 1865 (Raboteau; Sobel 1979).

The typology needs to say which of these three holds the remedy in each observation. At Edenton, who demolished the church? The text says only that "their request was denied, and the church was demolished."

Two well-documented American instances would serve G3 better than the present ones.

- *Suppression.* The African Church in Charleston was closed and its building destroyed after the Vesey affair of 1822 (Egerton 1999).
- *Absorption.* The plantation mission after 1831 was a deliberate effort to bring Black worship under white supervision (Mathews 1977; Cornelius 1999). The author quotes Charles Colcock Jones at length and does not treat his mission as an answer by authorities. "Absorb" is illustrated only by English gilds.

**C7. The "remedy" is defined in medieval and sacramental terms.**

It is "confession and absolution, burial and intercession for the dead, preaching and gathered worship." For enslaved Baptists and Methodists the answer to suffering lay in the conversion experience, the Word, prayer, and song. What could be held was the Bible and the ability to read it, the license to preach, the right to assemble, baptism and church membership, and burial.

The author's best quotation is about the Bible: "the law forbids it, and the churches withhold it" (Jacobs). The five indicators have no place for access to Scripture. The spirituals, a principal source for the theology of the enslaved in Raboteau and in Levine (1977), are not used.

**C8. The Philadelphia case rests on one memoir and misses its own best comparison.**

- The account of the walkout from St. George's comes from Allen's memoir, published in 1833. Footnote 4 notes the dispute over the date and cites no one. Nash (1988) and Newman (2008) should be used.
- The walkout produced two bodies. One, under Absalom Jones, joined the Episcopal Church in 1794 as the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. The other, under Allen, stayed Methodist until 1816. Here are one community, one grievance, and two paths, one inside a white-governed denomination and one out of it. The design promises that "Comparisons are made mainly within each case." This is the comparison to make, with the deeds, the ordinations, and the lawsuits coded on the five indicators.
- The nursing of 1793 is entered under "who delivers care." It was organized by the Free African Society and its leaders. It says nothing about whether a church held the remedy.

**C9. After 1865.**

"Control could be judged for 46 of them, and 41 were Black-run." That is 46 of 400, on a code with κ = 0.64. The exodus is well established and the manuscript adds nothing to it (Montgomery, whom the author cites; Walker 1982; Stowell 1998; Dvorak 1991; Turner 2020).

The author's own scheme would find more of interest in the white churches' answers. The Southern Methodists helped to set up a separate Colored Methodist Episcopal Church in 1870. That is an answer by authorities of the "delegate" or "absorb" kind. Northern white-led denominations also competed for freedpeople. "Bodies they controlled" is too simple a description of where people went.

**C10. Published narratives are used as if they were responses of the enslaved.**

Type 4's evidence begins: "Douglass stated the separation." Douglass wrote in the North, for abolitionist readers, in a genre with its own conventions. The author notes this of Jacobs and not of Douglass. Walker was a free man in Boston. These are arguments by Black writers about slaveholding religion. They are not observations of what enslaved communities did.

Nat Turner appears only as the cause of repression. By his own account he was a prophet who baptized outside the church when the church refused him. He is the clearest American instance of taking the remedy into one's own hands, and the typology has nothing to say about him.

**C11. Statistical practice.**

- The supplement says "The script prints ten comparisons." The table shows eight rows.
- The chi-square of 25.8 on four degrees of freedom has "two cells... with expected counts below 5."
- Table 4, Panel B, puts an interval on a figure labeled "lower bound."
- The Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio has no interval.
- Counts and percentages would serve better than tests throughout this section. The narrative is the sampling unit. The unit of interest is a community's worship.

**C12. Position against earlier quantitative work.**

Escott (1979) coded the whole collection by hand. Fountain (2010) coded conversion in WPA and other narratives. The manuscript presents its coding as a new dataset. It should say what it adds to theirs and where its figures differ.

### Minor concerns

- The dialect spellings in the quotations were created by interviewers. Current practice says so when quoting (Stewart 2016).
- "Informants" is dated. "Narrators" or "interviewees" is now usual.
- The *Christian Recorder* is cited for "Issues of May 1866, January 1869, and March 1869." Three issues from four years need dates and pages, and a word on how they were chosen.
- "Pinn (1995) recovers a skeptical strand in narratives and spirituals." This is one sentence on a body of work that bears directly on G1. Engage it or cut it.
- Locators are given for four quotations only (S4).
- Kansas has an allocation of 0 in a proportional design. Say why.

### Recommendation

**Major revision.** The sampling and the evidence rule are good foundations. I cannot recommend acceptance until humans have validated the coding (C1), the edition problem is dealt with (C2), the Panel C association is taken out of the argument (C4), and the frame admits non-Christian and non-adopting responses (C5). If the human validation is not done, my recommendation is to reject.

## (c) Requiring a human

### c.1 A human-coded validation subsample

This is the first requirement of the editor's letter. Two people code each sample independently, from the same text the model saw or from the full text where stated, without sight of the model's codes. One should be the author. For the interviews, the other should know the history of slavery in the United States.

| Dataset | Units | How drawn | Variables | Text given to coders |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interviews | 120 narratives | The 40 already double-coded, plus 80 new: 20 coded white-supervised only, 15 Black-led only, 15 with secret worship, 10 with two or more modes, 20 "not stated." Random within each group, recorded seed. | The four worship flags; obedience preaching; attitude (negative, neutral, positive, none); faith before freedom and whose; timing of conversion; prayer for freedom; any postwar church and whether Black-run; hearsay only; the new variables in P2 (state of enslavement, conjure, Bible and literacy, who forbade worship) | Full narrative |
| Interviews excluded by the screen | 30 narratives | Random from the 725 | Any religious content; conjure; explicit irreligion | Full narrative |
| Wills | 200 entries | 50 per period; in 1349–60, 25 from 1349 and 25 from 1350–60. Random, recorded seed, not overlapping the 198 already used to test the rules. | All 14 bequest codes; burial place; holder of chantry property in five classes, with "no holder named" separate | Sharpe's entry |
| Gilds | 100 gilds | Stratified by county group (Norfolk, Lincolnshire, Cambridgeshire and Suffolk, London, other) and language | Date class and year; all purposes; founders; clergy role | Westlake's page image |

**What to report**

- Agreement between the two humans for each variable: percent, κ with an interval, the number of positive cases, and a prevalence-adjusted index.
- An adjudicated human code for each unit where the two differ, with the rule used.
- The model against the adjudicated human code, for each variable and each category: precision, recall, F1, and the confusion table, with intervals.
- Whether the model's errors run in one direction, and whether they differ by state, by interviewer, or by period.
- The headline proportions recomputed with a correction from the human sample. Egami et al. (2023) give one method; it must be read first.
- For the gilds, the error rate of the character recognition on 20 entries, counted by word.
- The time each coder took and the instructions each received.
- A repeat run of the model on the same 120 narratives with the same instructions, to show whether its codes are stable.

**Scale.** At about ten minutes a narrative, two minutes a will, and three minutes a gild, each coder needs about 25 hours for the interviews and about 12 hours for the rest.

### c.2 Checks against originals

- Compare 80 wills (20 per period) with the original rolls or with images of them. For each, list the pious bequests in the roll and mark which appear in Sharpe's entry. Report the omission rate by period and by bequest kind.
- Compare 40 English-language gild returns with the full texts in Smith (1870) and, for London, in Barron and Wright (1995), for purposes, founders, and clergy.
- Compare the four quoted interview passages with the supplement-series versions where they exist.
- Identify interviewers and their race from archival lists for the 206 narratives that describe worship.
