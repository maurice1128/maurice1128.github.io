# Who Holds the Remedy? Suffering, Lay Initiative, and the Church in Plague-Era England and the United States under Slavery

## Abstract

How do people respond toward faith and toward the church after collective suffering? This article induces a typology from two distant cases: plague-era England, mainly London, between 1320 and 1400, and enslaved and free Black Christians in the United States before and after 1865. It combines close reading of contemporary texts with three coded datasets: 1,979 wills enrolled in the London Court of Husting, the returns of 509 gilds in 1388–89, and a stratified random sample of 400 interviews with formerly enslaved people, 206 of which describe worship before 1865. The analysis is descriptive and makes no causal claim. Five regularities emerge. Demand for religious goods persisted or rose, and abandonment of faith is rare in the record. Responses toward the church varied with who held the remedy, meaning the practices a community's theology prescribes against suffering and death, and with who delivered care. Where the church was present and shared the remedy, devotion stayed inside it. Where it was absent or guarded the remedy, lay people took it into their own hands, inside the church or outside it. Where the church was allied with the oppressor, people separated God from that church and left for bodies they controlled. Authorities answered lay initiative by delegating and reclaiming, suppressing, or absorbing it. Heightened devotion faded with the danger, while organizations built by sufferers endured. Six shadow cases from the secondary literature mark the typology's reach and limits. Rival accounts, including deliverance and artifacts of the records, are confronted.

**Keywords:** collective suffering; lay religious initiative; Black Death; slave religion; typology; comparative-historical analysis

## 1 Introduction

On January 10, 1349, with plague in his diocese, Ralph of Shrewsbury, bishop of Bath and Wells, ordered his clergy to publish an emergency rule. People at the point of death who could find no priest should confess their sins to one another, "even to a layman, and if no layman is at hand, to a woman" (*etiam laico, et si desit vir laicus, mulieri*). The bishop presented the rule as apostolic teaching (*juxta doctrinam apostoli*), not as an innovation. He also required those who recovered to confess again to their own priest, "as they are bound" (Wilkins 1737, 2: 745–46). The striking thing in the mandate is its premise. No priests could be found, the bishop wrote, who were willing to take on the vacant cures and visit the sick, whether from devotion or for any stipend. Many, as he had heard, were therefore dying without the sacrament of penance.

Nearly five centuries later, in Edenton, North Carolina, enslaved Christians asked leave to meet again in "their little church in the woods." It "was built by the colored people," Harriet Jacobs recalled. After Nat Turner's revolt of 1831, "their request was denied, and the church was demolished" (Jacobs 1861: 103).

The two scenes share a structure and little else. In each, people under collective suffering looked to practices that their own theology prescribed against suffering and death. In each, someone other than the sufferers controlled who might perform those practices. This article asks what people did next. After collective suffering, how do people respond toward faith, and how do they respond toward the church?

The two objects must be kept apart. Faith here means devotion directed to God: prayer, penance, lament, and the search for meaning. A response toward the church is what people do with a particular body, its ministers, its rites, and its property. They may support it, supplement it, work around it, or leave it. The two can diverge. Bottan and Perez-Truglia (2015: 113–14) found that Catholic abuse scandals in the United States were followed by lasting falls in affiliation and attendance. They found no statistically significant fall in belief in God, although their estimates for belief are imprecise. That faith may persist while a church loses ground is therefore not a new observation, and I do not offer it as a finding. The open question is what sorts the responses.

The aim is inductive. I use historical evidence to derive a typology of responses and to state the regularities that the evidence supports, each with its scope. This is not a causal study. The design cannot show that suffering caused devotion, or that the conduct of a church caused people to stay or leave. It can show what kinds of response the record contains, under what recorded conditions each appears, and how church authorities answered.

The organizing concept is the remedy. By "remedy" I mean the practices that a community's theology prescribes against suffering and death: confession and absolution, burial and intercession for the dead, preaching and gathered worship. To "hold" the remedy is to control who may perform, attend, fund, and govern those practices. The term is analytic and is not taken from the sources.[^1] Who holds the remedy is coded from five indicators that can be read without knowing how people later responded: who ordains or appoints ministers, who owns property, who administers rites, who controls funds, and who delivers care.

Two cases are examined in depth. The first is plague-era England, mainly London, from 1320 to 1400. Its evidence is episcopal and papal letters, all 1,979 wills enrolled in the London Court of Husting in those years, and the returns that 509 gilds made to the Crown in 1388–89. Continental chronicles and a papal bull are used for comparison and are labeled as such. The second case is enslaved and free Black Christians in the United States. Its evidence is a planter's handbook, narratives and pamphlets by Black authors, and a coded random sample of 400 interviews with formerly enslaved people, 206 of which describe worship before 1865. Six further cases are treated at secondary depth, to mark where the typology reaches and where it stops.

Five regularities result, and section 6 states them in full. Demand for religious goods persisted or rose after collective suffering. Responses toward the church varied with who held the remedy and who delivered care. Church authorities answered lay initiative in three ways. Heightened devotion faded when danger passed, while organizations built by sufferers endured. Institutional self-correction may be a path to renewed trust, though the cases are few. Each is an observed regularity in two cases, not a law.

The article makes three contributions. It defines control of the remedy so that it can be coded apart from outcomes. It reports three datasets, coded under written codebooks and deposited with their scripts. And it offers a typology that covers the answers of authorities as well as the acts of lay people. Section 2 reviews theory and prior work. Section 3 sets out design, data, and methods. Sections 4 and 5 present the cases. Section 6 states the typology, section 7 confronts rival accounts, and section 8 concludes.

## 2 Theory and prior work

Three families of theory bear on the question, and each answers a different part of it.

Demand-side theories explain why suffering should raise religious demand. Weber (1978) held that religions of salvation must explain the distribution of fortune and suffering, and that social strata favor different explanations. Norris and Inglehart (2011) argue that religiosity persists where life is insecure and declines where survival is secure. Bentzen (2021) found that online searches for prayer rose sharply worldwide in March 2020. In her country panel the rise was no longer distinguishable from zero by about October. These theories predict a level of religious demand. They do not say which body receives it, or what people do when the body that claims to supply it fails them.

Supply-side and authority theories address the body. For Weber (1978), a church is an organization whose staff claims a monopoly over granting and withholding religious benefits. Bourdieu (1971) described the religious field as a struggle among specialists over the legitimate management of the goods of salvation, in which lay people are dispossessed. Finke and Stark (1992) argued that religious participation depends on the energy of competing suppliers. Chaves (1994) redefined secularization as the declining scope of religious authority, which allows belief and authority to move apart. Hirschman (1970) distinguished exit from voice and noted that exit requires an alternative. Martin (1978) and Casanova (1994) argued that the public fate of churches depends on how they stand toward states and elites. These theories say which institutions gain or lose. They rarely begin from suffering, and most treat "the church" as one actor.

Theories of power explain how position shapes what can be said and done. Scott (1990) distinguished the public transcript that subordinates perform from the hidden transcript they keep offstage. Genovese (1974) argued that the religion of the enslaved worked within a paternalist order and also supplied a means of self-defense. On either view, outward attendance is a poor measure of what people thought of the church they attended.

These literatures yield three expectations against which a description can be set. If demand alone mattered, devotion would rise and fall with danger, and the church that received it would be incidental. If supply alone mattered, lay action would appear wherever clergy failed, in whatever form came to hand. If position alone mattered, the powerless would keep a hidden religion and conform in public. None of the three says what authorities do when lay people act. None distinguishes lay action that the church houses from lay action that it does not.

Quantitative studies of shocks have measured institutions more often than responses. Belloc, Drago, and Galbiati (2016) found that earthquakes delayed the move to communal self-government in medieval Italian cities where the bishop was also the political ruler. They do not measure lay belief or trust. Dittmar and Meisenzahl (2020) found that plague outbreaks between 1500 and 1522 raised the probability that a German city adopted a Protestant church ordinance. They found no comparable effect of fourteenth-century plague: "Before religious competition led to the articulation of institutional alternatives, these shocks did not lead to institutional change" (Dittmar and Meisenzahl 2020: 983). The measured outcome is a law, not a change in devotion.

Historians of the two cases have reached more specific positions, which I report as the literature summarizes them. For the Black Death, older syntheses held that clerical deaths and failures damaged the standing of the church. Later studies doubt a general collapse. Cohn (1992) studied wills from six Tuscan and Umbrian cities and placed the main change in pious giving after the second plague of 1363, with gifts still directed to church institutions. Henderson (1994) set the charity of Florentine confraternities within a network that included the church. Duffy (1992) described English parish religion as vigorous, though for a period that begins around 1400. Robinson (2019) found from ordination lists that men ordained in the plague years came from much the same backgrounds as their predecessors. Platt (1996) linked demand for memorial masses to priests leaving parishes for chantry posts. Two readings therefore compete: devotion redistributed within the church, or trust in the church collapsed.

For slavery in the United States, Raboteau ([1978] 2004) described an "invisible institution" of worship beyond the masters' supervision, alongside attendance at churches that whites controlled. Frey and Wood (1998) dated mass conversion to the evangelical revivals of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Irons (2008) and Sparks (1994) showed that many Southern evangelical churches were biracial, so the masters' control of religion was never complete. Boles (2020) found northern congregations interracial but unequal, with Black members leaving over access and equality. Fountain (2010) argued that most enslaved people became Christians only with or after emancipation. Here too two readings compete: commitment followed control of worship, or commitment followed freedom.

Three things are missing from this body of work. First, control of the practices that answer suffering is seldom defined so that it can be coded apart from what people later did. If a church is called the people's own because they stayed in it, the description only restates the outcome. Second, the answers of authorities to lay initiative are seldom classified together with that initiative. Third, to my knowledge, these two well-documented cases have not been set side by side with evidence coded under one scheme. The concept used here stands closest to Weber's monopoly over religious benefits and Bourdieu's goods of salvation. It adds indicators that are independent of outcomes, a church disaggregated by level and by conduct, and a distinction between lay action inside the church and outside it.

Two clarifications guard against misuse. The concept describes control and passes no judgment on it, since every church has an order. And it does not assume that the practices work. It records what a community's own theology prescribed. Care, meaning nursing, relief, and attendance on the sick and dying, is recorded as a fifth indicator because it shows who was present. It is not itself the remedy.

The result is a typology of the kind that case-based research uses to develop theory (George and Bennett 2005). It is a structured description of kinds, built from cases, that later work can test. A typology states what varies and how it is observed. It does not estimate effects.

## 3 Design, cases, data, and methods

### 3.1 Logic of comparison and case selection

The design is a contrast of contexts with a modest search for common variation (Skocpol and Somers 1980). The two deep cases were chosen because they differ in almost every circumstance. In one, an epidemic struck a Christian majority whose church was governed by a clerical hierarchy. In the other, Christians inflicted a regime of bondage on people whose access to worship they controlled. The cases are five centuries apart. They share only the structure of interest: collective suffering, a Christian theology that prescribed practices against it, and records of who controlled those practices.

Two cases cannot establish covariation, and the hazards are well known (Lieberson 1991). Both cases were also chosen with their outcomes in view, which can manufacture patterns (Geddes 1990). I respond in three ways. Comparisons are made mainly within each case, across periods and across modes of worship. The indicators of who holds the remedy are coded from records that do not depend on the response. And six shadow cases, known only from secondary literature, are used to show where the types recur and where they fail. Both deep cases involve the Latin and Protestant traditions of one religion, so the typology claims nothing beyond Christianity.

London is the focus of the first case because the Husting calendar gives a continuous series across both plagues. The gild returns come mainly from eastern England, so the two English datasets describe different populations. The shadow cases were chosen from a wider review of the literature to span the conditions of interest. They are a church that was not allied with the sufferers' rulers (Ireland), lay bodies inside an established church (Brazil), a compliant establishment under a non-Christian ruler (Korea), churches allied with perpetrators (Rwanda), a living rival religion (the Andes), and a church's public self-correction (Taiwan). They illustrate, and they carry no evidential weight for the regularities.

### 3.2 Sources and datasets

*Texts.* For England I read the plague letters of the chapter of Canterbury, Ralph of Shrewsbury, and Archbishop Islip (Wilkins 1737), those of Archbishop Zouche of York with papal letters addressed to him (Raine 1873), and Robert of Avesbury's chronicle (Thompson 1889). For continental comparison I read the annals of Gilles li Muisis of Tournai (Lemaître 1906), the Paris chronicle attributed to Jean de Venette (Géraud 1843), and Clement VI's bulls as printed by Raynaldus (1880). For the United States I read Jones (1842), the Liberty County report of 1846, Walker (1830), Douglass (1845), Jacobs (1861), Allen ([1833] 1880), Jones and Allen (1794), and issues of the *Christian Recorder* for 1866–69. All were read in public digital copies. Translations from Latin are mine.

*London wills.* Sharpe (1889, 1890) calendared the wills enrolled in the Court of Husting. Every entry enrolled from 1320 to 1400 was coded, with no sampling (N = 1,979). Each entry is an English abstract, not the will. Fourteen kinds of pious bequest were coded as present or absent by a fixed set of rules. The wills were grouped by enrollment into four periods: 1320–48, 1349–60, 1361–75, and 1376–1400. A random tenth (198 wills), drawn before the rules were written, was coded a second time by a blind reading of each entry. Cohen's κ was 0.80 to 1.00 for the main categories (parish 0.86, chantry 0.99, fraternity 0.95, friars 0.93, monastic 0.80). A further variable, who was named to hold chantry or obit property, was not double-coded.

*Gild returns.* Westlake (1919: 137–238) calendared the certificates that English gilds returned to the Crown in 1388–89. All 509 gilds were coded for the form of the foundation date, stated purposes, founders, any clerical role in governance, and any mention of pestilence. A random tenth (51 gilds) was coded again from the page images. Agreement was complete for dates, pestilence, and clerical governance, and κ was 0.80 to 0.91 for funerals, masses, lights, and chaplains. It was weak for feasts (0.39). Forty-two returns were matched with those printed by Smith (1870), and foundation dates agreed in 37.

*Interviews with formerly enslaved people.* The Federal Writers' Project (1941) interviews were read in the Project Gutenberg transcription of the Library of Congress deposit. Of 2,143 narratives that could be segmented, 1,418 mention religion by a keyword screen (66.2 percent). From these, 400 were drawn at random, in proportion to each state's share. Each was coded for who controlled worship before 1865, the content of preaching, attitudes to white preachers, faith before freedom, the timing of conversion, and churches joined afterward. An independent blind second coding of 40 narratives reproduced the worship-control and obedience-preaching codes exactly (κ = 1.00). Agreement was lower for faith before freedom (κ = 0.83), attitude to white preachers (0.82), and postwar church attendance (0.64). In every disagreement the first coding was the more reticent, so its counts for those variables are lower bounds. The code for prayer for freedom could not be tested, because it did not occur in the 40.

### 3.3 Coding who holds the remedy

Table 1 lists the five indicators and the records that answer each. They concern control and care, not loyalty. A church is not coded as present because people stayed, nor as failing because they left. The indicators are coded for each body and each period, since one church can hold the remedy differently at different levels and times.

### 3.4 Statistics

Proportions are reported with Wilson 95 percent confidence intervals. Adjacent periods in the wills are compared with pooled two-proportion tests, and contrasts in the gild and interview data with Fisher's exact or chi-square tests. All figures are descriptive. Tests indicate whether a difference exceeds what chance would produce in records of this size. They do not identify causes, and no adjustment is made for multiple comparisons.

### 3.5 Use of artificial intelligence in data collection and analysis

A large language model performed the data collection, coding, and analysis ([TOOL, VERSION, DATES]). I state this plainly because every count below depends on it. Under my direction, the model retrieved and parsed the public editions, wrote the codebooks, drew the random samples with recorded seeds, applied the codes, and wrote and ran the analysis scripts. The codebooks were written before coding and amended as documented in each codebook and in the supplement.

The second codings differ in kind. For the wills, the first coding is a deterministic rule set and the second is the model's blind reading of each sampled entry. For the gilds, the first coding combines keyword rules with the model's reading, and the second is the same model's reading of the page images, made without sight of the first. For the interviews, a first recoding by the same coder measured only intra-coder stability and is not reported as reliability. The independent second coding was made by a separate session of the same model, given only the codebook and the locators. It was not made by a human.

Three safeguards apply. Every coded value is stored with the verbatim passage and the locator on which it rests. Scripts verified each interview passage against the source text. And rules, data, and scripts are deposited so that any figure can be recomputed ([REPOSITORY, anonymized for review]). The reliability statistics therefore measure the consistency of a documented procedure. They do not measure agreement between independent human judges, and two sessions of one model may share habits of reading.

### 3.6 Limits

Three limits apply throughout. The quantitative sources are a nineteenth-century calendar, a twentieth-century calendar, and typed interview transcripts, not the original rolls, certificates, or speech. The codes record whether a thing is mentioned, not its value or frequency. And no human coder has yet checked the codings. The supplement gives codebook summaries, full reliability tables, and robustness checks.

## 4 Plague-era England

### 4.1 Who held the remedy

Church authorities in England explained the plague as punishment for sin and ordered liturgical answers. On September 28, 1348, the prior and chapter of Canterbury wrote that God sometimes punishes in time so as not to condemn in eternity. They ordered prayers, masses, sermons, and processions, with indulgences (Wilkins 1737, 2: 738). Archbishop Zouche of York had ordered processions and litanies in July and named human sins as the cause (Raine 1873: 395–96). These answers were the clergy's to give. So was the sacrament of penance, on which a good death was held to depend.

On the five indicators, the position at the start of the period is clear. Bishops ordained. Priests heard confession, gave the Eucharist, anointed the dying, and buried the dead. I take the remedy, as defined above, to have been largely in clerical hands. The wills show one exception from the outset. Lay people already paid for intercession and often kept its endowment under family control.

### 4.2 Absence and conditional delegation

Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate of January 10, 1349, records what happened where priests could not be had. The contagion, he wrote, had left many parish churches without a priest. Because no priests could be found who were willing to take on those cures, perhaps because of the infection and the dread of contagion, many were dying without penance, "as we have heard." The dying might therefore confess to a layman or, failing one, to a woman. A deacon might give the Eucharist. Where no priest could anoint, faith in the sacrament must suffice, as in other cases. Those who recovered were to confess the same sins again to their own priest (Wilkins 1737, 2: 745–46).

The mandate cites apostolic teaching. It adds that confession to a lay person in such a case accords with the teaching of the Church and the canons. On its own account, it applies established doctrine on necessity and announces nothing new. The bishop explains why he must say so. The sick did not know what to do, and they believed that no confession could help them unless it was made to a priest who held the keys. The interest of the mandate here lies in two further features. A bishop records, on report, that priests were unwilling. And the delegation is bounded. It holds only at the point of death, and the priest's part returns with recovery. I call this answer "delegate and reclaim."

Other documents record the shortage. On October 12, 1349, Clement VI allowed the archbishop of York additional ordinations, because priests able to administer the sacraments "cannot be found" (*nequeunt inveniri*) on account of the mortality (Raine 1873: 401). On May 28, 1350, Archbishop Islip complained that surviving priests were leaving the cure of souls for privately paid annual masses and demanding "excessive stipends" (Wilkins 1737, 3: 1–2). On November 16, 1362, after the second plague, he issued a new constitution with the same opening words and sharper language (Wilkins 1737, 3: 50–51). These are the hierarchy's own records of a shortage of priests, and in two of them of priests' unwillingness. The York letter attributes the shortage to deaths, not to refusal. Burial, at least, was provided. Robert of Avesbury reports more than two hundred burials almost daily, from early February until after Easter 1349, in a newly made cemetery by Smithfield (Thompson 1889: 407).

### 4.3 The wills: devotion inside the church

The Husting rolls register the two plagues by volume alone. In 1349, 356 wills were enrolled, against a mean of 21.2 a year in 1340–47. In 1361, 130 were enrolled, against a mean of 19.3 a year in 1350–59. Read naively, the calendar shows every kind of pious bequest rising across the four periods. Much of that rise is an artifact. Before 1349 most enrolled wills dealt with land only. The testament of movables, where small pious gifts sat, was usually not enrolled (Sharpe 1889: xxv). Sharpe's summaries also lengthen, from a median of 74 words in 1320–48 to 154 in 1376–1400, and he left out bequests that he judged unimportant (Sharpe 1889: xlviii).

I therefore restrict the main comparison to wills whose summary states a burial place. That clause is a sign that the part of the will concerned with the soul was enrolled and abstracted. Among these wills (n = 83, 273, 332, and 252 in the four periods), the share with a gift to a parish church rises from 28.9 percent before the plague to 49.1 percent in 1349–60 and 69.9 percent in 1361–75. Gifts to friars rise from 6.0 to 9.5 to 22.6 percent, and gifts to fraternities from 2.4 to 5.5 to 12.0 percent. The steps after 1361 exceed chance (parish +20.8 points and friars +13.1, both p < .001; fraternities +6.6, p = .005). Chantries behave differently. They were already common before the plague, at 38.6 percent of these wills, and they did not move after either epidemic (41.0 and 41.3 percent). They rose only after 1376, to 50.8 percent. No class of church recipient declines in any period.

Who held the endowment of intercession shifted slowly. Among wills with a chantry or obit, property left only with a wife, heirs, or executors falls from 88.3 percent in 1320–48 to 64.1 percent in 1376–1400. Property placed with a parish body or a lay corporation rises from 4.3 percent (95% CI 2.2–8.2) to 29.4 percent (22.8–37.1). This variable rests on words that occur together in a sentence, and it was not double-coded. It is a rough indicator.

Three cautions limit what the wills show. The burial-clause subset reduces the recording artifact without removing it. When summaries of 120 words or fewer are taken alone, chantries are flat, while gifts to friars still rise, from 0.7 to 13.5 percent. The testators are Londoners with property, and 235 of the 1,979 are women. And the codes record presence, not sums or numbers of masses. Only one summary mentions plague. A testator enrolled in 1369 "makes his will seeing the peril of this world and especially of the existing plague" (Sharpe 1890: 125).

With those cautions, the wills fit devotion that stayed inside church institutions, and reached more of them, better than they fit withdrawal. They cannot establish either reading. Nor do they show the concentration of gifts that Cohn (1992) reported for central Italy after 1363. In wills stating a burial place, the mean number of bequest kinds rises from 1.49 in the first period to 3.41 in the last. Given the recording problems, this is weak evidence about London and no test of Cohn.

### 4.4 The gilds: lay custody inside the church

Table 3 about here

The gild returns show what lay people built. Westlake's calendar summarizes 509 gilds. Three-quarters come from Norfolk, Lincolnshire, Cambridgeshire, and Suffolk, and 41 from London. Of the 500 entries with any content, 80.2 percent mention lights, 58.0 percent masses or prayers for dead members, and 50.0 percent attendance at members' funerals or their burial. A chaplain or priest whom the gild maintained or sought appears in 37.6 percent, and relief of members in 35.4 percent. Some provision for dead members appears in 70.4 percent.

Founders are described for 104 gilds. They are lay parishioners or townspeople in 52 and craft groups in 42, together 90 percent. Six are mixed and four clerical. A cleric has a stated part in governing the gild in 11 of 509 entries (2.2 percent). The usual relation runs the other way. "The alderman collects the chaplain's salary," one return says, and another has a "chaplain elected by them" (Westlake 1919: certs. 180, 182). A third gild kept a chaplain "to assist the parson daily … whereby the parson's difficulties were much relieved" (cert. 382). In London, the wardens of St. Katherine's gild at Aldersgate called the Friars Minor to say a dirige and a trental of masses for a dead member, and paid them (Smith 1870: 8).

These bodies held part of the remedy. They organized funerals, bought intercession, and kept lights. They did so inside parish churches and with clergy whom they hired. Nothing in the returns shows suppression, and 73 gilds list the fabric or ornaments of a church among their purposes. I call this answer of the authorities "absorb." The returns were made to a Crown that was suspicious of gild property, so their piety may be overstated.

The foundation dates need care. Of the 286 gilds that state a year between 1300 and 1389, 235 give 1349 or later (82.2 percent; 95% CI 77.3–86.2). This cannot show that gilds multiplied after the plague. The inquiry recorded only gilds alive in 1388–89, and a gild that died earlier left no memorial, as Westlake (1919: 27) saw.[^2] Explicit mentions of pestilence occur in 8 entries (1.6 percent), and most record disruption. Only one presents the plague as the occasion of a foundation. At Lynn in 1349, three men saw the sacrament "carried through the parts of the town with only a single candle of poor wax burning in front of it," and a Corpus Christi gild followed (Westlake 1919: 199). A London gild at St. Bride's reported that its purposes "were suspended by the great plague, but renewed later by survivors and parishioners of other parishes" (Westlake 1919: 184). Both still existed in 1389. The returns document persistence more securely than they document a surge.

### 4.5 A continental comparison: lay custody outside the church

The English texts read here supply no documented case of English lay people taking the remedy outside the church. Avesbury records that about Michaelmas 1349 more than 120 flagellants, mostly from Zeeland and Holland, did their penance twice a day in London, in the sight of the people (Thompson 1889: 407–8). He does not say how Londoners responded. For the outside form I turn, as comparison only, to Tournai and to the annals of Gilles li Muisis, a Benedictine abbot wary of novelty.

Flagellant companies reached Tournai on August 15, 1349. Li Muisis wrote that a large part of the common people approved their rite "more than the divine office" and often mocked the clergy who thought otherwise (Lemaître 1906: 231, 239). By Advent the companies were carrying and burying the dead with cross and candles. The dying were often persuaded to leave them gifts, as was customary for curates, chaplains, hospitals, and the poor (240). The movement did not set out to leave the church. Its oath required a recruit to ask leave of his curate and to defend the rights of holy Church (243).

Clement VI condemned the companies on October 20, 1349.[^3] The bull charges that they despise the keys of the Church and carry the cross "without the license of a superior." It also charges that many of them, or their adherents, shed the blood of Jews and often of Christians, and seized the goods of clergy and laity (Raynaldus 1880, 25: 471). I do not assess how far those charges were fair. The same bull says that it does not forbid the faithful to do penance in their own homes, apart from such companies (472). It suppressed organized penance under lay government, not lay penance as such. At Tournai, proclamations by the town and the king came first. The bishop, chapter, and governors then arranged a sermon at which the pope's mandate was announced, though the bull was not shown. From that day the penitents ceased, "with great murmuring, unwillingly" (Lemaître 1906: 251–52).

Three further points from the continental texts qualify the picture. First, in these same years Christians massacred Jews across much of the Empire (Cohn 2007). Clement VI issued bulls in their defense on July 4 and September 26, 1348, and Raynaldus conceded that they failed everywhere outside the county of Avignon (Raynaldus 1880, 25: 453–55). The typology below concerns the responses of Christians toward their own church. It does not cover this violence, which Christians inflicted on others.

Second, clerical conduct varied. The Paris chronicler usually identified as Jean de Venette, a Carmelite, wrote that in many towns timid priests withdrew and left the ministry to bolder religious. He praised the sisters of the Hôtel-Dieu, who nursed the sick without fearing death (Géraud 1843, 2: 211–12). A friar had reasons to draw that contrast. He also wrote that no one died without confession and viaticum, which runs against Ralph's report. And he explained why the dying left so much to churches: they saw their own heirs die before them (213). Li Muisis recorded that many curates and chaplains who heard confessions and visited the sick died (Lemaître 1906: 258).

Third, a line in li Muisis points to fading. Pilgrimage to the relics of St. Sebastian surged in 1349. Then, "when the storm ceased, the pilgrimage and the devotion ceased as well" (Lemaître 1906: 258).

### 4.6 What the case shows

For plague-era England the record supports four statements. Demand for intercession and for the church's rites did not fall. In the wills it appears in more forms and more institutions after 1361. Where priests were absent, a bishop delegated confession to lay people on the terms of necessity and reclaimed it. Lay people took part of the remedy into their own hands inside the church, through fraternities that hired clergy, buried members, and kept lights, and through chantries placed with parish or lay bodies. The outside form, companies that governed their own penance and buried the dead, appears only in the comparison texts, where authorities suppressed it. The record does not show what lay people believed, whether they trusted the clergy less after 1349, or that the plague caused any of these arrangements.

## 5 Enslaved and free Black Christians in the United States

Two settings must be kept apart. In the plantation South, slaveholders controlled the worship of people they owned. In Philadelphia, free Black Methodists contested the control of white elders within a shared denomination. The first setting supplies the interviews and the planter's handbook. The second supplies the clearest record of leaving and building.

### 5.1 The plantation South: who held the remedy

Charles Colcock Jones, a Presbyterian minister and planter in Georgia, described the masters' power in a book meant to persuade other slaveholders. Masters, he wrote, may "forbid religious meetings, and religious instruction on our own plantations." They may "literally shut up the kingdom of heaven against men." Enslaved people were "in a state of almost absolute dependence upon their owners for the words of eternal life" (Jones 1842: 158). Jones also printed the terms on which his church offered instruction. In 1833 the Synod of South Carolina and Georgia resolved to "separate entirely the civil and religious condition of this people" and to "disclaim all interference with the former" (Jones 1842: 73–74).

On the five indicators, masters and white churches decided who might preach, where people might meet, and who owned any building. That control was never complete. Irons (2008) and Sparks (1994) describe biracial congregations in which Black members were numerous. Jones's own mission conceded in 1846 that Black lay leaders, the watchmen, could not be put down: "All attempts to suppress this class of influential men among their own colour have signally failed" (Association 1846: 13).

Harriet Jacobs described the power in use at Edenton after 1831. After the woods church was demolished, slaveholders decided "to give the slaves enough of religious instruction to keep them from murdering their masters" (Jacobs 1861: 105). Of an old man who wanted to read the Bible, she wrote that there were thousands like him, "but the law forbids it, and the churches withhold it" (113). Jacobs wrote for abolitionist readers, and her setting is a town.

### 5.2 The interviews: worship under and beyond supervision

Table 4 about here

The interview sample sets these texts against a wider body of testimony. Of the 400 narratives, 206 describe how worship was organized before 1865. White-supervised worship alone is reported in 110 of them (53.4 percent). Black-led meetings held with permission are the only mode in 26, secret worship in 17, and outright prohibition in 3. Another 50 report two or more modes. Some worship under Black control, permitted or secret, appears in 92 of the 206 (44.7 percent; 95% CI 38.0–51.5). "Sometimes at night us gather 'round the fireplace and pray and sing and cry," Susan Merritt of Texas recalled, "but us daren't 'low our white folks know it."

Preaching content is described in 37 narratives. In 25 of them (67.6 percent; CI 51.5–80.4) it is obedience alone. A Black man who preached, William Mathews said, was not allowed the gospel: "All he could preach 'bout was obey de massa, obey de overseer, obey dis, obey dat." Charlie Van Dyke remembered that "the overseer stood there to see the preacher talked as he wanted him to talk." Evaluations are mixed. Among 174 narratives that state an attitude to white preachers or the master's religion, 22 are negative (12.6 percent), at least 39 are positive, and at most 113 are neutral. The line between neutral and positive depended on the coder. Negative judgments cluster with obedience preaching. They occur in 14 of 23 such narratives and in none of the 9 that describe other preaching.

Faith before freedom was coded only when a narrative states that an identifiable enslaved person prayed, converted, or shouted in worship. At least 72 narratives state it and three deny it. The rest are silent, and silence is not unbelief. Among narratives that report Black control of worship, 44 of 92 state such faith (47.8 percent). Among the others, 21 of 114 do (18.4 percent). That is about four times the odds (odds ratio 4.06; CI 2.17–7.59), and the association holds within states (Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio 3.36). It weakens when only the informant's own faith or a relative's is counted (22.8 against 14.0 percent; odds ratio 1.81, CI 0.88–3.72). Much of it arises because one passage describes both a meeting and the people praying in it. This is association within testimony. It does not show that control of worship produced faith.

Prayer for freedom is reported in 13 narratives, 10 of them among those that report Black control. The reliability of that code is untested. Minnie Davis of Georgia remembered her mother "praying to herself: 'Oh, Lord, please send the Yankees on and let them set us free'" (all quotations from Federal Writers' Project 1941; locators are in the supplement).

The interviews need three cautions. The informants' median age was 83, so the typical informant was about eleven in 1865, and much of the testimony is childhood memory or family report. The interviews took place in the Jim Crow South, mostly with white interviewers, under a questionnaire that asked about plantation churches. And informants spoke differently to Black interviewers, as Blassingame (1975) warned. A Black interviewer is documented for 53 narratives. Among narratives that state an attitude, 7 of 21 given to Black interviewers are negative (33.3 percent), against 15 of 153 of the others (9.8 percent; Fisher p = .007). The comparison is fragile. Black interviewers are concentrated in two states, one narrative carries several of the differences, and none of the ten comparisons made is adjusted for multiple testing. Its direction suggests that criticism of white religion is understated in the collection.

### 5.3 Separating God from the masters' church

Black writers drew a line between the remedy and those who claimed to hold it. Frederick Douglass put it most sharply: "I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land" (Douglass 1845: 118). In his account the oppressor and the church officer are one person: "The man who robs me of my earnings at the end of each week meets me as a class-leader on Sunday morning, to show me the way of life" (119). David Walker stated a more radical conclusion and refused it. From the way Europeans had conducted religion, "one might believe it was a plan fabricated by themselves and the devils to oppress us. But hark! My master has taught me better than to believe it" (Walker 1830: 40). The master he means is Christ.

Neither Douglass nor Jacobs rejected white ministers as such. "We slaves loved Mr. Cookman," Douglass wrote of a Methodist preacher who, they believed, had helped persuade a rich slaveholder to free his slaves (Douglass 1845: 54). Jacobs described an Episcopal rector whose sermons enslaved people gladly attended: "it was the first time they had ever been addressed as human beings" (Jacobs 1861: 110). I read these two passages as attaching trust to conduct, not to office.

Loss of faith is rare in this record. Jacobs reports a mother, whose children had all been taken from her, crying in a class meeting, "God has hid himself from me" (Jacobs 1861: 108). Pinn (1995) recovers a skeptical strand in narratives and spirituals. The three denials in the interview sample concern not having religion in slavery, not rejecting it.

### 5.4 Free Black Philadelphia: leaving and building

Richard Allen, a Methodist preacher who had been enslaved, recalled the break at St. George's church in Philadelphia.[^4] During prayer a trustee took hold of Absalom Jones, "pulling him up off of his knees, and saying, 'You must get up—you must not kneel here.'" When the prayer ended, "we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church" (Allen [1833] 1880: 14–15). The group then worshipped apart and raised a subscription to build. The Methodist elder threatened to read them out of the Methodist meeting. They answered: "if you deny us your name, you cannot seal up the scriptures from us, and deny us a name in heaven" (16).

Allen remained a Methodist by conviction (18). The contest was over the pulpit and the property (20–21). In April 1816 Black Methodists formed the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church. They adopted a form of discipline to "preserve us from that spiritual despotism which we have so recently experienced" (24). On the indicators, ordination, property, rites, and funds passed from white elders to a body that Black Methodists governed.

The same community delivered care during the yellow fever of 1793, on terms that were not its own. Appeals in the city's newspapers had asked Black residents to nurse the sick, on the assumption, soon disproved, that they were immune. Jones, Allen, and the elders of the African church organized nursing and burial. Mathew Carey then accused Black nurses of extortion and plunder. The pamphlet of 1794 is their reply, and it should be read as a defense: "Thus were our services extorted at the peril of our lives, yet you accuse us of extorting a little money from you" (Jones and Allen 1794: 15). Burials among Black Philadelphians rose from 67 in 1792 to 305 in 1793 (15–16). The authors also print a verse on how quickly crisis devotion fades: "When the war is over, and all things righted, / God is forgotten, and the soldier slighted" (20).

### 5.5 After 1865

After emancipation, Black Christians in the South moved in large numbers into denominations that Black people governed (Montgomery 1993). In the interview sample at least 106 narratives mention a postwar church. Control could be judged for 46 of them, and 41 were Black-run. The AME weekly shows the indicators at work. In January 1869 it reported that congregations in Kentucky had stayed in the AME when the Southern Methodists tried to recover them. It also reported that AME societies in Virginia were worshipping in buildings deeded to the Southern church and held by white trustees. A letter of May 1866 records members at Nashville returning to the Southern church (*Christian Recorder* 1866–69). Hildebrand (1995) describes several Methodist bodies competing for freedpeople. The exodus was large, and it was not uniform.

Whether commitment itself began then is a separate matter. Only 28 of the 400 narratives date the informant's conversion, and 25 of those fall after 1865. Section 7 explains why this cannot settle the question.

### 5.6 What the case shows

The record supports four statements here too. Devotion persisted where the church was the masters', in permitted Black-led meetings and in secret ones. Where masters guarded the remedy, people worshipped beyond supervision. Where the church was identified with the oppressor, writers separated God from that church, and when they could, people left it for bodies they governed. Trust in individual white ministers followed conduct. The record does not show how many enslaved people were Christians, when most became so, or what moved any individual.

## 6 A typology of responses

Table 5 about here

### 6.1 Four types and a limit case

The two cases yield four types of lay response, sorted by a condition that the indicators record. The types describe acts, not persons. One community, and one person, can appear in several. Fifty interview narratives report more than one mode of worship.

*Devotion inside the church.* Where the church is present and shares the remedy, devotion stays within it and spreads across its institutions. London testators gave to parish churches, friars, and fraternities in rising shares after 1361. Venette's dying confessed and received the viaticum. Enslaved people in Edenton went gladly to hear a rector who addressed them as human beings.

*Lay custody inside the church.* Where clergy are scarce or stretched, and lay bodies are allowed, lay people take part of the remedy into their own hands within the church. The gilds buried members, kept lights, and hired chaplains. Testators placed chantry endowments with parish and lay bodies. Under slavery, the parallel is the permitted Black-led meeting and the watchman.

*Lay custody outside the church.* Where the church is absent or guards the remedy, and lay action has no license, people act beyond the church's supervision. The flagellant companies at Tournai governed their own penance and buried the dead. Enslaved people prayed in secret, in 28 of the 400 narratives.

*Separation.* Where the church is allied with the oppressor, people separate God from that church. When they can, they leave it for bodies they control. Douglass stated the separation. Allen's company enacted it in 1816, and freedpeople did so in large numbers after 1865.

The sorting condition is read from the indicators and not from the response. At Bath and Wells the record of absence is the bishop's own mandate. In London the record of sharing is the placing of chantry endowments and the hiring of clergy by gilds. In the South the record of guarding is Jones's statement of the masters' power, with 110 narratives of worship under white supervision alone. In Philadelphia the record is the dispute over pulpit and property. One church can stand in different conditions at different levels. A rector in Edenton shared what the town's slaveholders guarded.

*Rejection* is the limit case. Neither deep case documents a rejection of Christianity as such. Walker considered that conclusion and refused it. The type is reported only in the secondary literature.

### 6.2 Five regularities

Each statement below is an observed regularity in two cases, with the evidence and scope given. None is a law.

**G1.** After collective suffering, demand for religious goods persisted or rose, and abandonment of faith is rare in the record. In London wills that state a burial place, gifts to parish churches, friars, and fraternities all rose after 1361, and no class of church recipient fell. Under slavery, 44.7 percent of narratives that describe worship report some of it under Black control, and voices of lost faith are few. The scope is narrow. The wills are abstracts of the wills of propertied Londoners. The interviews are those that mention religion. Neither source was built to record unbelief.

**G2.** Responses toward the church varied with who held the remedy and who delivered care. The four types above state how. The interview data support this as an association only, and that association weakens under a stricter measure of faith.

**G3.** Church authorities answered lay initiative in three ways. They delegated the remedy and reclaimed it, as Ralph of Shrewsbury did. They suppressed lay custody, as Clement VI did in 1349, and as happened to the woods church at Edenton after 1831. Or they absorbed it, as parish churches absorbed the gilds. The evidence is one mandate, one bull and its enforcement, one demolished church, and 509 returns.

**G4.** Heightened devotion faded when the danger passed, while organizations built by sufferers endured. Li Muisis saw pilgrimage stop with the plague, and Jones and Allen printed a verse on the same pattern. Two gilds, one founded in the plague and one renewed after it, still reported in 1389. The church formed in 1816 was holding Southern congregations in 1869. The evidence for fading is thin, and it comes from Tournai and from one verse. Survivorship also applies. Enduring bodies are visible because they endured.

**G5.** Institutional self-correction may be a path to renewed trust. The evidence is slight. At Edenton an Episcopal rector addressed enslaved hearers as human beings, and people unused to attending church came. Islip twice named the failures of his clergy, but no lay response is recorded. A modern church's confession is discussed in section 7. The cases are too few for more than an observation, and I state it as a question for further work.

### 6.3 Shadow cases and hard cases

Six shadow cases, summarized in Table 5, were not studied from primary sources. Each coding rests on the secondary literature as cited, and the wording is that of report. Larkin (1972) reported a surge of Catholic practice in Ireland after the Famine, in a church that was not the church of the state or the landlords. Miller (1975) showed that part of it may reflect who died and who left. Russell-Wood (1974) described lay brotherhoods of enslaved and free Black people in colonial Brazil that managed burial and festivals within the Catholic Church. Kim (1989) studied the Shinto shrine issue in Korean Christianity under Japanese rule. Most Protestant bodies are reported to have complied, and resisters to have formed separate bodies after 1945. Longman (2010) argued that the Rwandan churches' alliance with the state gave them substantial responsibility for the genocide of 1994, and Kubai (2007) described the spread of new churches afterward. Stern (1982) described the Taki Onqoy movement in the Andes of the 1560s, which called for a return to the native deities. The sixth case, a church in Taiwan, is discussed in section 7.

The shadow cases suggest that the types recur beyond the two deep cases. They also mark limits. Separation is reported in Korea, where the oppressor was not Christian. A church's alliance with power may therefore matter more than the oppressor's creed. Rejection appears where a rival sacred system was alive. None of these codings has been tested.

The deep cases contain evidence that does not fit neatly, and it should be kept in view. Venette says that none died unconfessed, while Ralph says that many did. At least 39 of 174 narratives speak well of white preachers or of a master's religion. Members at Nashville went back to the Southern church. Two kinds of observation would count against the typology. One is a church that held the remedy, shared it, and delivered care, and yet lost its people. The other is lay bodies that formed outside a church before any recorded absence or guarding.

## 7 Discussion

### 7.1 Rival accounts

*Deliverance.* On Fountain's (2010) account, enslaved people committed themselves to Christianity mainly with or after emancipation. Freedom, not control of worship, was what they answered. The interview sample cannot test this. Dated conversions are rare (28 of 400), and 25 of them fall after 1865. But informants with a median age of 83 in 1937 were children in 1865, so late conversion is nearly built into the sample. Freedom also brought deliverance and control together. The texts support a narrower statement. People who already worshipped, some of them beyond supervision, moved into Black-run churches when they could. How many did so is not known, and the texts do not exclude Fountain's reading for the majority.

*Fading after danger.* If devotion only tracks danger, the rise under G1 is transient and no redistribution need be inferred. G4 accepts part of this account. In the London wills, gifts to church institutions did not fall back in 1376–1400. That series is confounded by changes in recording, so it cannot refute the account.

*Artifacts of record and composition.* Each dataset has one. The Husting calendar reflects what was enrolled and what an editor abstracted. The gild returns are a census of survivors, and a constant rate of dissolution could produce their dates. The interviews vary with the interviewer, and their informants were children under slavery. Composition matters as well. Those who made wills after 1349 had survived it. I have reported each artifact with the figure it affects. G1 rests on the absence of any recorded decline, which fuller recording could in principle mask. G2 and G3 rest on texts as well as counts.

*Provision and political alignment.* Two further accounts hold that people follow material relief or political allies. In 1793 the Black church in Philadelphia gave relief and did not receive it, which does not fit the first. The second has support. In March 1869 the AME weekly charged the Southern Methodists with treason against the government as well as the church (*Christian Recorder* 1866–69). In the typology, a church's alignment with a political order is one form of alliance with power. The two cannot be separated in these cases.

*Demand-side theories.* Existential-security and coping accounts fit G1. They are silent on G2 and G3, because they do not say where demand goes.

*Supply-side and authority theories.* These come closest. G2 can be restated as the failure of a monopoly over religious benefits. The typology adds two distinctions that those theories lack. It separates lay custody inside the church from lay custody outside it, and it classifies the answers of authorities. It also agrees with Hirschman (1970) and with Dittmar and Meisenzahl (2020) that exit requires an alternative. Separation appears where a body could be built, in free Philadelphia and after 1865. Custody outside the church, hidden or short-lived, appears where it could not.

### 7.2 A recent shadow case

One shadow case is recent, and its documents are public. The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan issued public statements under martial law on December 29, 1971, November 18, 1975, and August 16, 1977 (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1971, 1975, 1977). The last called for Taiwan to become "a new and independent country." On February 9, 1990, the church published an apology to the victims of the violence of February 28, 1947, and to their families. It confessed that the church as a whole, cowed by martial-law rule, had not given the families the public support and warmth they were due. It excepted a very few ministers and members. The same text demanded an apology and compensation from the government, so it was a political act as well as a confession (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1990). Rychetská (2022) reports that the church explained its human-rights activity in terms of Christian belief.

These documents show a church naming its own failure toward people who suffered. They do not show how the families received the apology or whether anyone's trust changed. I know of no study that measures either.[^5] The case is therefore an instance for G5 to be examined, not evidence for it.

### 7.3 Limits and further work

The results are descriptive. Two cases and six reports cannot support claims about causes or about frequency beyond the records coded. The sources are calendars and transcripts. The coding was done by a language model and has not been checked by human coders. Several monographs cited for context are known to this study through reviews and abstracts, and they are cited only for their general positions. The next steps follow. The original Husting rolls and gild certificates should be sampled against the calendars. The interviews should be recoded by human coders with interviewers identified from archival lists. Brazil's brotherhoods should be studied from primary records, since they would separate the control of the remedy from the power of the sufferers. And G5 needs cases in which trust is measured before and after a church corrects itself.

## 8 Conclusion

This article asked how people respond toward faith and toward the church after collective suffering. It answered with a typology induced from two distant cases. In plague-era England and under American slavery, the record shows religious demand that persisted or rose. What varied was the response toward the church, and it varied with who held the remedy and who delivered care. Where the church shared the remedy, devotion stayed inside it. Where the church was absent or guarded the remedy, lay people took it up themselves, inside the church when they were allowed and outside it when they were not. Where the church stood with the oppressor, people separated God from it and built their own bodies when they could. Authorities delegated and reclaimed, suppressed, or absorbed. Devotion in its heightened form faded, and the organizations remained.

These are regularities in two cases, stated with their evidence and their scope. They are not laws, and the design supports no causal claim. Their use is as a structured description that others can test, extend, or refute. The concept of holding the remedy is offered in the same spirit. It directs attention to five things that records often preserve and that can be coded apart from outcomes: who ordains, who owns, who administers, who funds, and who cares.
