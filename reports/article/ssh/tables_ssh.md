## Table 1. Indicators of who holds the remedy, and the records that answer them in the two deep cases

| Indicator | Plague-era England (comparison texts in italics) | Plantation South | Free Black Philadelphia and the postwar South |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Who ordains or appoints ministers | Bishops ordain. Clement VI permits York additional ordinations, October 12, 1349 (Raine 1873: 401). Gilds maintain or seek a chaplain of their own in 37.6% of 500 returns (Westlake 1919). | Masters may forbid meetings and instruction and choose the churches attended (Jones 1842: 158). Black watchmen persist (Association 1846: 13). Black-led permitted meetings are mentioned in 66 of 400 narratives. | White elders control the pulpit. The African Methodist Episcopal Church is formed in April 1816 with its own discipline (Allen [1833] 1880: 20–24). |
| 2. Who owns property | Chantry or obit property is left with wife, heirs, or executors only in 88.3% of chantry or obit wills in 1320–48 and 64.1% in 1376–1400. It is placed with a parish body or lay corporation in 4.3% and 29.4% (Sharpe 1889, 1890). | A church built by enslaved people in the woods at Edenton is demolished after 1831 (Jacobs 1861: 103). | AME societies in Virginia worship in buildings deeded to the Southern church and held by white trustees, January 1869 (*Christian Recorder* 1866–69). |
| 3. Who administers rites | Priests. Confession to lay people is allowed at the point of death and must be repeated to a priest on recovery (Wilkins 1737, 2: 745–46). Gild returns mention funerals in 50.0% and lights in 80.2%. *Flagellant companies bury the dead at Tournai (Lemaître 1906: 240).* | White-supervised worship only in 110 of 206 narratives that describe worship (53.4%). Some worship under Black control in 92 of 206 (44.7%). Secret worship is mentioned in 28 of 400. | The group that left St. George's worships apart (Allen [1833] 1880: 15–17). |
| 4. Who controls funds | Testators, heirs, and executors. Gild officers collect and pay chaplains' salaries (Westlake 1919: certs. 180, 213). Islip legislates on priests' stipends in 1350 and 1362 (Wilkins 1737, 3: 1–2, 50–51). | Not recorded in the sources coded. | The group raises its own subscription to build (Allen [1833] 1880: 15–17). |
| 5. Who delivers care | Priests are reported unwilling to visit the sick (Wilkins 1737, 2: 745). Relief of members appears in 35.4% of gild returns. *Sisters of the Hôtel-Dieu nurse the sick in Paris (Géraud 1843, 2: 212). Curates and chaplains die serving at Tournai (Lemaître 1906: 258).* | Not recorded in the sources coded. | Black Philadelphians nurse the sick and bury the dead in 1793 (Jones and Allen 1794: 3–16). |

*Notes:* The indicators are read from records of control and care. None is inferred from whether people stayed in a church or left it. "Not recorded" means that the sources read for this study do not answer the indicator. Percentages come from the coded datasets described in section 3 and in Tables 2 to 4.

*Sources:* As cited in each cell.

## Table 5. A typology of responses toward the church after collective suffering

| Type | Condition, read from the indicators | Lay response | Answer of authorities observed | Evidence in the deep cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. | 2.
