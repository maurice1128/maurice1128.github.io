# Who Holds the Remedy? Collective Suffering, Trust and the Responsibilities of the Church

## Abstract

When a community suffers together, what does a church owe the people whose trust it asks? This article argues that a church's authority is then tested by how it holds the remedy it proclaims: whether it stands with those who suffer, delivers care, shares or guards the means of grace, and corrects its own failures. Faith in God and trust in a church are treated as distinct. Three historical scenes are read closely from primary sources, as an interpretive lens and not as a test of causes: the plague of 1348–62, when a bishop let the dying confess to lay people and required survivors to confess again to a priest; enslaved Christians in North America, whose masters controlled access to the word; and the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, which in 1990 confessed its failure towards the victims of 1947. The scenes yield three questions: who holds the remedy, with whom the church stands, and whether it can correct itself. Scripture supplies the norm: lament, the critique of retribution, Ezekiel 34, Jeremiah 7, Amos 7 and Acts 6. The article ends with questions for churches in Taiwan today and takes no position on contested issues.

**Keywords:** collective suffering; trust in the church; means of grace; lament; ecclesial repentance; Taiwan

## 1 Introduction

On 10 January 1349, with plague in his diocese, Ralph of Shrewsbury, bishop of Bath and Wells, told his clergy to announce that the dying who could find no priest might confess their sins to a lay person, *et si desit vir laicus, mulieri* ('and if a man is lacking, to a woman').[^1] The church of his day taught that the remedy for the plague was penance, and that priests held it. The bishop now recorded that he could not deliver it. He had to decide what to do with a remedy that he held but could not bring to the people who needed it.

This article asks about that decision in a general form. When a community suffers together, what does a church owe the people whose trust it asks? The question is ethical before it is historical or sociological. It does not ask how churches can keep their members. It asks what makes a church's claim to be trusted a warranted claim, at the moment when that claim is most exposed.

Two things need to be kept apart from the start. *Faith* here means devotion directed to God: prayer, lament and the search for meaning. *Trust in the church* means confidence that a particular body, with its ministers and its rites, can be relied on to carry that devotion and to care for those who hold it. The two have different objects, and they can move apart. A study of Catholic abuse scandals in the United States found lasting falls in Catholic affiliation and attendance, and no statistically significant fall in belief in God, though its estimates for belief are imprecise.[^2] A recent study of Ukraine at war draws the same conceptual line between trust in God and trust in the church.[^3] The observation that faith can survive while trust in a church falls is therefore not new. It appears in student work on the Black Death, and historians have given reasons to doubt it for that case.[^4] I do not offer it as a finding.

My thesis is normative. When a community suffers together, a church's authority is tested by how it holds the remedy it proclaims: whether it stands with those who suffer, whether it delivers care, whether it shares or guards the means of grace, and whether it corrects its own failures. By 'remedy' I mean whatever a church announces as God's answer to suffering, together with the means by which it offers that answer: the word, the sacraments, prayer, burial, absolution and material care. The word is borrowed from one of the sources. The abbot Gilles li Muisis wrote that the clergy of Tournai took no care *de remedio apponendo* ('to apply a remedy').[^5] The verb 'holds' matters as well. A church does not make the remedy it proclaims. It receives it and holds it for others, as a steward holds what belongs to someone else (1 Cor. 4:1–2). A steward can be asked for an account, and collective suffering is one of the times when the account falls due.

The debts of this argument should be stated. Weber tied theodicy to social position: the fortunate want assurance that their fortune is deserved, while the disprivileged look for compensation and reversal.[^6] Martin and Casanova showed that the public fate of churches depends on how they stand towards states and elites.[^7] Among economists, Belloc, Drago and Galbiati found that earthquakes in medieval Italian cities delayed the move to communal self-government where the bishop was also the political ruler, and that such cities then built and decorated more religious buildings. They do not measure lay belief or trust.[^8] Among student theses, Zacharia compares the Black Death with the fire of London and the Lisbon earthquake, and Boone's title announces a study of redemptive suffering in the service of Black Philadelphians during the yellow fever of 1793.[^9] I add no new fact. I offer a way of putting an ethical question to cases like these, and a set of questions that a church can put to itself.

The method is to read three scenes closely, from primary sources, as an interpretive lens and not as a test of causes. The scenes are the plague of 1348–62 in Latin Christendom, the religion of enslaved Christians in North America, and the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan between 1947 and 1990. I chose them for three reasons. They differ in who inflicted the suffering: no human agent, fellow Christians, and a state. They differ in who controlled the church: a clerical hierarchy, slaveholders, and the sufferers' own assemblies. And in each scene people on the spot wrote about who held the remedy, in public texts that can be quoted and checked.

The limits follow from the method. Three scenes chosen by one reader cannot establish a rule, and I do not generalise from them. I make no claim that suffering caused any change in trust, or that the conduct of any church caused people to trust it. Statements of fact rest on quoted sources. Where I interpret, I say so and name a rival reading in the same paragraph. The sources are partial: clerical chroniclers, abolitionist narratives, a planter's handbook and a church's own statements. Three supplementary analyses are described in the appendix, and the argument does not depend on them.

Section 2 presents the scenes. Section 3 draws three questions from them, defined so that the answers do not depend on how things turned out. Section 4 turns to Scripture, because scenes cannot say what ought to be done. Section 5 puts the questions to the record of the church in Taiwan.

## 2 Three scenes

### 2.1 Plague, 1348–62: who may absolve and bury

When the plague approached England, the bishops explained it as a scourge for sin and prescribed penance. In 1348 the chapter of Canterbury wrote that God punishes in time so as not to condemn in eternity, and ordered masses, sermons and processions.[^10] On that account the remedy lay in confession and the sacraments, and those lay with priests.

Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate of 10 January 1349 records what happened when priests could not be had.[^11] The plague, he wrote, had left many parishes without a priest,

> *et cum non inveniantur presbyteri, qui velint devotionis zelo, aut pro stipendio aliquo curas praedictorum locorum subire, et infirmos visitare, eisque sacramenta ecclesiastica ministrare; forsitan propter infectionem, et contagionis horrorem, multi absque poenitentiae sacramento, ut accepimus, moriuntur.*

That is, no priests can be found who are willing, out of zeal or for any stipend, to take on the care of these places, visit the sick and administer the sacraments, perhaps for fear of infection, and many are dying without the sacrament of penance.[^12] The clergy were therefore to make it known that people at the point of death who could not have a priest should confess to one another, *etiam laico, et si desit vir laicus, mulieri* ('even to a layman, and if a man is lacking, to a woman'). A deacon might give the Eucharist. Where no priest could give extreme unction, faith must suffice (*debet ... fides sufficere*). The mandate then adds a condition. Those who confess to lay people and recover are to confess the same sins again *suo proprio sacerdoti* ('to their own priest').[^13]

The same shortage is recorded at the top of the hierarchy. On 12 October 1349 Clement VI allowed the archbishop of York additional ordinations, because priests able to administer the sacraments *nequeunt inveniri* ('cannot be found').[^14] In 1350 Archbishop Islip's mandate *Effrenata* complained that the priests who had survived were leaving the cure of souls for privately paid masses and demanding *stipendia ... excessiva* ('excessive stipends'); he reissued it in 1362, after the second plague.[^15] These are the hierarchy's own records of its clergy's failure. They are not the whole record. The Paris chronicler usually identified as Jean de Venette wrote that in many towns *sacerdotes timidi recedebant* ('timid priests withdrew'). He also wrote that the sisters of the Hôtel-Dieu, *mori non timentes* ('not fearing to die'), nursed the sick with all gentleness and humility, and that their number had to be renewed many times because of death.[^16] At Tournai, li Muisis recorded that many curates and chaplains who heard confessions and gave the sacraments died.[^17] At Avignon, according to the papal biography printed by Raynaldus, the pope appointed physicians to visit the poor and bought a field for a cemetery.[^18] Care was withheld and delivered in the same months, by different members of the same church.

Some lay people did not wait to be given the remedy. Companies of flagellants reached Tournai in August 1349. Li Muisis wrote that a large part of the common people approved of what they did *plus quam divinum officium* ('more than the divine office') and very often mocked the clergy who thought otherwise.[^19] By Advent the companies were carrying and burying the dead with cross and candles, and the dying were often persuaded to leave them gifts, as was customary for curates, chaplains, hospitals and the poor.[^20] The movement did not set out to leave the church. Its oath required a recruit to ask leave of his curate and to defend the rights of holy Church.[^21] Clement VI condemned it on 20 October 1349. He charged that the flagellants despised the keys of the Church and carried the cross *sine superioris licentia* ('without the licence of a superior'). The same letter states that it does not forbid the faithful to do penance in their own homes, apart from such companies and assemblies.[^22] At Tournai the companies were stopped in Lent 1350 by proclamation of the town and of the king, and they ceased, li Muisis says, *cum magno murmure inviti* ('with great murmuring, unwillingly').[^23]

A third party also acted. The bishop of Tournai had died, and in every parish the curates, parish clerks and gravediggers were ringing bells for the dead by day and night, for their own profit, until the whole town was afraid. Li Muisis, himself an abbot, explains what followed:

> *Gubernatores civitatis, videntes quod decanus, et capitulum, et clerus totus, de remedio apponendo non curabant, quia sua intererat et in facto lucrabantur, habito consilio, fecerunt ordinationes.*

The governors of the city, seeing that the dean, the chapter and the whole clergy took no care to apply a remedy, because it was in their interest and they were profiting by the matter, took counsel and made ordinances.[^24]

I read these texts as showing three routes by which the remedy passed for a time into lay hands. It was delegated by a bishop and then reclaimed. It was seized by a lay movement and then suppressed. And it was taken over by a town government when the clergy would not act. This is an interpretation, and its base is narrow: one diocesan mandate, one papal letter and one chronicle, all written by senior clergy. A rival reading is that very little passed anywhere, because most devotion still ran through the church. Venette says that the dying left many inheritances and goods to churches and religious houses.[^25] Cohn's study of wills in six Tuscan and Umbrian cities finds that the form of pious bequests changed after the second plague of 1363, and that the bequests still went to church institutions.[^26] Henderson places the charity of Florentine confraternities within a network of church, state, guild and hospital, and Duffy describes English parish religion on the eve of the Reformation as vigorous.[^27] Robinson's study of ordination lists finds that the men ordained in the plague years came from much the same backgrounds as their predecessors, which undercuts the old account of an inferior replacement clergy.[^28] To say that devotion was 'redistributed' and not lost would therefore be an interpretation too. Li Muisis offers a third reading in a single line. When the plague eased, the pilgrimages to the relics of St Sebastian stopped: *Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio* ('when the storm ceased, the pilgrimage and the devotion ceased as well').[^29] Devotion may simply have followed the danger.

Two supplementary analyses bear on these readings. The first compares where the religious bequests in London wills went before and after the plagues of 1348–49 and 1361: [[EVIDENCE:husting_wills]]. The second tabulates the foundation dates and stated purposes of English parish fraternities, as they reported them to the Crown in 1388–89: [[EVIDENCE:gild_foundations]].

*What this scene shows, and what it does not.* It shows a church that taught that its priests held the remedy, recorded in its own registers that it could not deliver it, lent it under a condition, and moved against those who took it unasked. It shows care given at the cost of life by some clergy and religious, and withheld by others. It does not show what lay people in general believed, whether they trusted the clergy less afterwards, or that any of these events caused a lasting change.

### 2.2 Enslaved Christians in North America: who controls the word

In the plague scene no human agent inflicted the suffering. In the second scene those who inflicted it were Christians, and they controlled the church. Charles Colcock Jones, a Presbyterian minister and planter in Georgia who promoted missions to enslaved people, described the masters' power in 1842, in a book written to persuade other slaveholders:

> we may, according to the power lodged in our hands, forbid religious meetings, and religious instruction on our own plantations; we may forbid our servants going to church at all, or only to such churches as we may select for them; we may literally shut up the kingdom of heaven against men ... It is not too much, therefore to say that the Negroes are in a state of almost absolute dependence upon their owners for the words of eternal life.[^30]

Jones also prints the terms on which his church offered instruction. In 1833 the Synod of South Carolina and Georgia resolved to 'separate entirely the civil and religious condition of this people' and to 'disclaim all interference with the former'.[^31]

People on the other side of that power described its use. Harriet Jacobs, writing of Edenton, North Carolina, after Nat Turner's revolt of 1831, says that the enslaved asked to meet again at 'their little church in the woods', which 'was built by the colored people'. Then: 'Their request was denied, and the church was demolished.'[^32] The slaveholders decided, she continues, 'to give the slaves enough of religious instruction to keep them from murdering their masters'. Of an old man who wanted to learn to read the Bible she writes that there were thousands like him, 'but the law forbids it, and the churches withhold it'.[^33]

Worship went on out of sight, in what Raboteau called the 'invisible institution'.[^34] Interviews with former slaves recorded in 1936–38 contain many memories of it. They need caution: most informants were children before 1865, the dialect on the page is the interviewers', and what informants said seems to have varied with who was asking.[^35] One informant in Tennessee, Andrew Moss, recalled his master's words, 'Youse my negro. I git ye to Heaven. Git up off'n your knees', and his mother going into her prayer ground, a muscadine bush, to 'pray for deliverance of de slaves'.[^36] A supplementary analysis codes a random sample of these interviews for who controlled worship before 1865: [[EVIDENCE:wpa_religion]]. From the masters' side, the report of Jones's mission in Liberty County conceded in 1846, of the Black lay leaders known as watchmen, that 'All attempts to suppress this class of influential men among their own colour have signally failed'.[^37]

Black Christian writers drew a line between the remedy and those who claimed to hold it. Frederick Douglass put it most sharply in the appendix to his *Narrative*: 'I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land.' He added that 'The man who robs me of my earnings at the end of each week meets me as a class-leader on Sunday morning, to show me the way of life'.[^38] David Walker stated a more radical conclusion and refused it. From the way Europeans had conducted religion, 'one might believe it was a plan fabricated by themselves and the devils to oppress us. But hark! My master has taught me better than to believe it'.[^39] Neither Douglass nor Jacobs rejected white ministers as such. Douglass wrote, 'We slaves loved Mr. Cookman', a Methodist preacher who, they believed, had helped to persuade a rich slaveholder to free his slaves. Jacobs describes an Episcopal rector whose sermons many enslaved people gladly went to hear: 'it was the first time they had ever been addressed as human beings'.[^40] In these texts trust attaches to conduct and not to office.

Free Black Methodists in Philadelphia made the separation in institutional form. Richard Allen recalled that at St George's church a trustee took hold of Absalom Jones during prayer, 'pulling him up off of his knees, and saying, "You must get up—you must not kneel here."' When the prayer was over, 'we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church.'[^41] Threatened with expulsion from the Methodist connection, they replied: 'if you deny us your name, you cannot seal up the scriptures from us, and deny us a name in heaven.'[^42] Allen remained a Methodist by conviction. What he contested was the white elders' control of the pulpit and the property, and in 1816 Black Methodists founded the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church, in the words Allen records, to 'preserve us from that spiritual despotism which we have so recently experienced'.[^43] The same community had already shown what it did with a remedy in its own hands. During the yellow fever of 1793, Jones and Allen organised Black Philadelphians to nurse the sick and bury the dead, 'sensible that it was our duty to do all the good we could to our suffering fellow mortals'.[^44]

After 1865 large numbers of Black Christians in the South moved into churches run by Black people, among them the AME and Black Baptist congregations.[^45] In January 1869 the AME's weekly reported that congregations in Kentucky had chosen to stay in the AME when the Southern Methodists tried to recover them, and that in Virginia many AME societies were worshipping in buildings deeded to the Southern church and held by white trustees. The same paper records members who went back to the Southern church.[^46] I read this movement as a sign that commitment followed control of worship: people joined, in numbers, the churches they could govern. That is an interpretation. Its strongest rival is Fountain's argument that Christian commitment among the enslaved came largely with or after emancipation, so that it was freedom, and not control of worship, that people answered.[^47] The two readings cannot be cleanly separated, because freedom brought deliverance and control at the same time. The texts read here more securely support a narrower statement: after the war, many people who had already been praying and worshipping moved into Black-run churches. They do not establish a mass first conversion, and they do not show what moved any individual.

*What this scene shows, and what it does not.* It shows, in the words of a slaveholding minister, that masters could open or shut access to the word. It shows, in the words of enslaved and free Black writers, that they distinguished the gospel from its custodians, kept worship going where they could control it, and built churches of their own when they were able. It does not show how many enslaved people were Christians, when most became so, or that suffering produced their faith. Each author quoted had a case to make, to abolitionist readers or, in Jones's case, to planters.

### 2.3 Taiwan, 1947–90: silence, statements and a confession

The third scene is recent, and in it a church speaks about itself. Its background is the violence that began on 28 February 1947, known in Taiwan as the 228 Incident, and the martial law that followed and lasted until 1987. The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) worshipped in Taiwanese, Hakka and Indigenous languages and was not a church of the ruling party-state. Rubinstein places it at the indigenous end of Taiwan's Protestant spectrum.[^48]

The documents read for this article include no public statement by the church about that violence before 1987, and the church's own later judgement, quoted below, is that it failed the victims' families. Its organised effort went into growth. The 'Double the Church' movement of 1955–65 aimed to double the number of congregations by the church's centenary in 1965.[^49]

Three statements followed. On 29 December 1971 the PCT declared that, because human rights are given by God, 'the people have the right to determine their own destiny'.[^50] On 18 November 1975 it issued 'Our Appeal'.[^51] On 16 August 1977 its 'Declaration on Human Rights' held that Taiwan's future should be decided by its seventeen million inhabitants and urged that Taiwan become 'a new and independent country'.[^52] Rychetská shows that from the late 1960s the church explained its human-rights activity in terms of Christian belief.[^53] Speaking about 1947 still carried a cost in the last year of martial law. By the church weekly's own later account, an issue that printed articles on 228 in February 1987 was confiscated by the Taiwan Garrison Command.[^54]

In 1989 the church issued a pastoral letter for the 228 anniversary.[^55] On 9 February 1990 it published an apology to the victims of 228 and their families. The apology confesses that, out of fear under martial law, '整體教會並未給予受難者家屬應有的聲援及溫暖' ('the church as a whole did not give the victims' families the support and warmth they were due'). It is addressed to the victims and their families, and it also implores God's mercy and forgiveness.[^56] A church here states that it failed to stand with its own people when they suffered, and it says so to them, forty-three years after the event.

What cannot be claimed needs to be stated as carefully as what can. It cannot be claimed that the PCT grew, or came to be trusted, because it stood with those who suffered. Its growth campaign began sixteen years before its first political statement and ended six years before it. I know of no study that compares its membership with that of other Protestant bodies across the martial-law period, and none that measures what the 1990 apology did to anyone's trust. The statements of the 1970s can be read as a church standing with a people under pressure. They can also be read as alignment with one part of a divided society. Chiu Kai-li argues that the church's theology of *chhut-thau-thiⁿ* ('rising out from under oppression'), which at first had political, cultural and spiritual dimensions, narrowed over thirty years into the single political goal of independence.[^57] Both readings are open on the evidence given here, and the scene does not decide between them. The chief witness, moreover, is the church itself.

*What this scene shows, and what it does not.* It shows a church that by its own account failed the families of those who suffered in 1947, that later spoke in public at some cost, and that then named its earlier failure before God and before the people concerned. It does not show that speaking or confessing earned trust, and it does not show how the victims' families received the apology.

## 3 The lens: three questions

The scenes differ in almost every circumstance. What they share is a structure: a church that proclaims a remedy for suffering, people who need it, and a question about whose hands it is in. From that structure I draw three questions to put to a church. They are framed so that they can be answered from records of what a church did and controlled, without knowing what later became of its members. The framing matters. If a church were called 'the sufferers' own' because people stayed in it, or 'failing' because they left, the lens would only redescribe outcomes.

**Who holds the remedy?** The first question asks who controls the means by which the church's answer to suffering reaches people. Four kinds of record answer it: who ordains or appoints ministers, who owns the buildings and endowments, who administers the sacraments and rites, and who controls the funds and profits by them. In the plague scene bishops ordained, priests administered, and at Tournai the clergy profited by the bells. In the second scene masters decided who might preach and whether anyone might meet, and after 1865 white trustees still held the deeds of buildings in which Black congregations worshipped. In the third the church was governed by its own assemblies. The question is not whether control is legitimate, since a church must have order. It is whether those who hold the remedy hold it for the people who need it, and what they do when they cannot or will not deliver it. Ralph lent it. The masters Jones describes could shut it up.

**With whom does the church stand?** The second question asks where a church is found when a community suffers. A fifth kind of record answers it: who visibly delivered care, to whom, and at what cost. The sisters of the Hôtel-Dieu stood with the sick, and the priests who withdrew did not, within one church. The class-leader who, in Douglass's account, took a man's earnings in the week and led his class on Sunday stood with the master. The apology of 1990 is a church's own answer to this question about its past.

**Can the church correct itself?** The third question asks what a church does when it is shown to have failed: whether it can say so, to whom it says so, and whether anything then changes in who holds the remedy. Islip named the failure of his clergy and answered it with a decree on stipends. The Methodist elders in Philadelphia answered a complaint with a threat of expulsion. The PCT confessed in public to the people it had failed. None of the three scenes offers a clear case in which a church shared control with those it had neglected. For that pattern the next section turns to Acts 6.

Five records, then, answer the three questions: who ordains, who owns, who administers, who funds, and who visibly cares. All five can be read before any outcome is known, and apart from it.

**Rival readings.** The same scenes can be read in other ways, and three should be named.

The first is deliverance. On this reading what binds people to a church is rescue and not the church's conduct. Enslaved people committed themselves to churches when they were freed, and would have done so whoever held the remedy. Fountain's work gives this reading its strongest form, and the second scene cannot refute it, because freedom and control of worship arrived together.[^58]

The second is fading after danger. Devotion rises with fear and falls with it, as li Muisis observed of the pilgrims. If so, much that looks like devotion moving from one body to another may be devotion that was always going to subside.

The third is political alignment. People may trust the churches that share their politics and distrust the others, so that what I have called standing with sufferers is, seen from elsewhere, taking a side. In 1869 the AME's weekly charged the Southern Methodists with treason against the government as well as against the church.[^59] The PCT's statements of the 1970s were political acts as well as pastoral ones.

I do not think these rivals can be eliminated on the evidence presented here, and the lens does not require it. They are claims about why people act. The lens makes no such claim. It asks what a church did with what it held, and that can be asked whatever moved the people it served. The rivals do mark the limit of the scenes as evidence. The scenes cannot tell us that a church which holds the remedy well will be trusted. Curates died at their work in Tournai, and the same chronicler reports that almost everyone there murmured against the whole clergy.[^60] Nor can the scenes tell us that a church which fails will be abandoned. Several matters remain open: whether control of the remedy matters apart from the delivery of care, whether confession changes anything for those who were failed, and how far any of this holds where states and medicine now supply much of what churches once did. These are empirical questions, and I leave them open. Whether a church ought to hold the remedy in a particular way is a question of another kind, and the scenes cannot answer it at all.

## 4 The norm: a biblical-theological reading

Scenes show what churches did. They do not show what a church owes. For that I turn to Scripture, read as a canon, and put to it the three questions of the lens. I do not claim that these texts caused anything in the scenes, although people in the scenes read them. Quotations follow the New Revised Standard Version.

**Lament as permitted speech.** Before Scripture explains suffering, it lets the sufferer speak. 'How long, O LORD?' (Ps. 13:1). 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?' (Ps. 22:1). Psalm 88 ends without any turn to praise: 'my companions are in darkness' (Ps. 88:18). Lamentations asks those who pass by, 'Is it nothing to you?' (Lam. 1:12). Westermann showed that lament is one of the two basic forms of prayer in the Psalms.[^61] Brueggemann argued that lament keeps the covenant two-sided and makes justice a proper question to put to God, and that worship which loses it comes to serve established power.[^62] O'Connor observes that God never speaks in Lamentations, and reads that silence as leaving room for the voices of survivors.[^63] Katongole's study of African Christian communities living with violence treats lament as a root of hope and of non-violent action.[^64] The bearing on the first question is direct. Complaint to God is part of the remedy, and it belongs to the one who suffers. A church that holds the remedy holds, among other things, the place where lament may be spoken. Jacobs describes a mother whose children had all been taken from her crying out in a Methodist class meeting, 'God has hid himself from me'. That is the speech of Psalm 88. The class-leader, Jacobs says, grew crimson with suppressed laughter.[^65]

**Retribution and its critique.** The bishops of 1348 read the plague as punishment, and they had Scripture for it. Deuteronomy 28 sets blessing and curse before Israel, and Amos asks, 'Does disaster befall a city, unless the LORD has done it?' (Amos 3:6). The canon also contains the critique. Job's friends defend retribution, and God says at the end that Job, not they, has 'spoken of me what is right' (Job 42:7). Jesus refuses the inference from suffering to sin: 'Neither this man nor his parents sinned' (John 9:3). Asked about Galileans killed by Pilate and eighteen people crushed by a tower, he denies that they were worse sinners than others, and then calls his hearers to repent (Luke 13:1–5). The critique limits retribution without removing accountability. Crenshaw treats punishment for sin as one biblical response to evil among several, none of them sufficient alone.[^66] This also bears on the remedy. A teaching that suffering is punishment makes those who administer penance the holders of the cure. The canon does not forbid that teaching. It forbids making it the only one, and it forbids presuming the guilt of those who suffer.

**The God who sees and knows.** 'I have observed the misery of my people ... I have heard their cry ... Indeed, I know their sufferings' (Exod. 3:7). The next verse adds, 'I have come down to deliver them' (Exod. 3:8). Fretheim reads such texts as speaking of a God who suffers because of the people, with them and for them.[^67] The second question of the lens has its ground here. If God is found beside those who cry out, then where a church stands is not a matter of strategy. It is a matter of whether the church is where its Lord is (cf. Matt. 25:31–46).

**Shepherds and the flock.** Ezekiel 34 is the closest biblical parallel to the scenes. The shepherds of Israel have fed themselves, and the charge against them is specific: 'You have not strengthened the weak, you have not healed the sick, you have not bound up the injured, you have not brought back the strayed, you have not sought the lost' (Ezek. 34:4). God does not respond by abandoning the flock. God takes over the work: 'I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep' (Ezek. 34:15), and the next verse names the neglected tasks as God's own (Ezek. 34:16).[^68] Mein adds a caution drawn from ancient animal husbandry. The shepherds are hired men who have misused an owner's property, so the chapter may say more about God's ownership than about God's compassion.[^69] For the present argument Mein's reading sharpens the point. The flock does not belong to the shepherds. They hold it for another, and they are removed for the way they held it. When li Muisis writes that the clergy of Tournai applied no remedy because they were profiting, his complaint has the shape of Ezekiel's.

**The temple and the king's sanctuary.** Jeremiah attacks confidence in the institution as such: 'Do not trust in these deceptive words: "This is the temple of the LORD"' (Jer. 7:4). Without justice for the alien, the orphan and the widow, the house becomes 'a den of robbers' (Jer. 7:11). At Bethel the priest Amaziah orders Amos to leave, because Bethel 'is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom' (Amos 7:13). Wazana reads the exchange as a dispute about belonging and exclusion, about who may speak in that place.[^70] These texts need restraint in use. Jeremiah and Amos address the state cults of ancient kingdoms, not voluntary churches. They warrant a question that any church can put to itself: has it become a king's sanctuary? They do not warrant one church's verdict on another.

**Self-correction and confession.** Acts 6:1–7 gives the third question its pattern. The Hellenists complained 'because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution' (Acts 6:1). The apostles did not deny it. The community chose seven men to oversee the distribution, all of them with Greek names, which suggests, though it does not prove, that the neglected group received a share of control. Luke then reports that 'the number of the disciples increased greatly' (Acts 6:7), but he does not present the correction as a means to that end. The Old Testament supplies a liturgy of corporate confession. The prayer of Nehemiah 9 calls God 'just in all that has come upon us' (Neh. 9:33), and Daniel prays in the first person plural, 'we have sinned and done wrong' (Dan. 9:5). The International Theological Commission notes that biblical confessions of the sins of ancestors are addressed to God, with one exception (Num. 21:7).[^71] Modern confessions, such as the Stuttgart declaration of October 1945 and the PCT's apology of 1990, are addressed also to those who were wronged.[^72] That goes beyond the usual biblical form. Acts 6 suggests why it is fitting: there the complaint came from people, and the answer was given to them.

**Counterweights.** The canon does not say that sufferers are right and institutions wrong. Ezekiel 18 rejects inherited guilt, 'it is only the person who sins that shall die' (Ezek. 18:4), so that each generation answers for itself, sufferers included.[^73] In Psalm 73 the psalmist's crisis turns when he goes 'into the sanctuary of God' (Ps. 73:17), so the assembly can be the place where faith recovers. Hebrews commands, 'Obey your leaders and submit to them', and adds that leaders 'will give an account' (Heb. 13:17). Acts sets the limit: 'We must obey God rather than any human authority' (Acts 5:29).

Traditions weigh these texts differently, and each sees something. Liberation and Black theologies begin from Exodus and the prophets and insist on God's partiality for the oppressed, which guards against spiritualising injustice.[^74] Catholic teaching holds to the continuity and holiness of the Church while confessing the sins of her members, which guards against fragmentation.[^75] Reformed theology makes every office answerable to the Word and sets limits to rulers.[^76] Evangelical readings give full weight to personal repentance, to God's sovereignty and to faithful endurance. A critic could fairly say that my reading leans towards the first of these. I accept the correction that the others offer. The first remedy for a failing church is repentance and reform, and although leaving may sometimes be right, it is not what these texts chiefly commend.

Read together, the texts say four things to a church that holds a remedy. The remedy and the flock belong to God (Ezek. 34). God is with those who cry out, and their complaint is prayer (Exod. 3:7; Ps. 88). Those who hold office will give an account (Heb. 13:17). And a church that has failed can say so and change (Acts 6; Neh. 9). This is the normative content of the three questions.

## 5 Reflections for the church in Taiwan

Taiwan serves as the worked example for two reasons. One of its churches has left an unusually explicit public record of where it stood and of where it failed to stand. And survey data exist on how far people in Taiwan trust religious bodies.

**What the record shows.** The first part of the record is the sequence described in section 2.3: three public statements between 1971 and 1977, a pastoral letter in 1989, and in 1990 an apology addressed to the victims of 1947 and their families and to God.

The second part is a survey series. The Taiwan Social Change Survey asked respondents how much they trusted religious groups and churches. The share answering that they trusted them completely or very much was 21.5 per cent in 2009, 13.3 per cent in 2014 and 10.7 per cent in 2018. The share expressing distrust was 16.0, 22.0 and 26.9 per cent in the same years.[^77] Three cautions govern any use of these figures. They come from published frequency tables and appear to be unweighted. The question covers religious groups of every tradition, and in these surveys Protestants were about 4 to 6 per cent of respondents, so the figures say nothing specific about Christian churches. And the series runs only from 2009 to 2018, because the 2023 survey did not repeat the question. The fall was already under way between 2009 and 2014. Its causes are unknown, and I offer no explanation of it.

A further item bears on public speech. In 2018, 67.6 per cent of respondents disapproved of religious leaders commenting publicly on whether the country is democratic; the figure was 62.3 per cent in 2014 and 60.6 per cent in 2023.[^78] The year 2018 was also the year of national referendums, among them questions on same-sex marriage, in which church-based groups campaigned.[^79] The coincidence in time is all that can be reported, and no causal link has been shown. On the question of marriage the PCT's own position is on record. Its pastoral letter of 10 June 2014 defines marriage as the voluntary union of one adult man and one adult woman, calls churches and members to accept people of same-sex orientation, and states that same-sex sexual acts are contrary to the Bible.[^80] I report these three points and take no view on them.

**Questions.** The lens and the norm together yield questions that a church in Taiwan might ask itself when it faces a matter that divides society or a disaster that strikes it. They are meant for churches of every tradition and political leaning, and they recommend no position on any contested issue.

*Is care delivered, and seen by those who need it?* Ezekiel's charge lists five tasks: strengthening the weak, healing the sick, binding up the injured, bringing back the strayed and seeking the lost. After an earthquake, a typhoon, an epidemic or a political injury, who among those affected could say that the church was present? This question comes first because it does not depend on agreement about anything else.

*Whose remedy is it?* Who decides who may receive what a church offers, whether word, sacrament, burial, counsel or relief, and on what conditions? When a church cannot reach people, does it lend what it holds, as Ralph of Shrewsbury did, or guard it? And when lay people or outsiders take up work that the church has left undone, does the church treat them as partners or as rivals?

*With whom does the church stand?* Amos 7 offers the test of the king's sanctuary. The 'king' may be a government, an opposition, a party, a majority or a donor. A church close to those who hold power can lose its freedom to speak, and so can a church that has bound itself to those who seek power. Chiu's criticism of the PCT's theology shows that the question can be put even to a church that has stood with people under pressure. It is easier to put to others than to oneself.

*What has not yet been confessed, and what has changed?* The apology of 1990 was addressed to the people who had been failed. Acts 6 goes one step further, since there the neglected group received a share in running the ministry that had neglected them. A church can ask what failures of its own it has not yet named, to whom it would have to name them, and whether anything in its ordering of authority, property or funds would change as a result.

*What community will carry faith to the next generation?* Faith and trust in a church are distinct, but faith is handed on in communities. A British study found that only about half of parental religiosity is passed on to children.[^81] Whether the same holds in Taiwan is not known. The question for a church is practical: if younger people cannot trust it, what gathered body will be there to receive and form their faith?

*Has the church listened before it speaks?* Scripture lets the sufferer speak first, and Acts 6 begins with a complaint that was heard. Before a church speaks in public on a contested matter, it can ask whether it has listened to the people whom the matter touches most closely, and whether they could recognise their own situation in what the church says. The survey figure for 2018 is not an argument for silence. The 1990 apology judged a silence, not a statement. The figure is a reason to ask what gives a church standing to speak, and the norm of section 4 locates that standing in presence with those who suffer.

None of these questions tells a church what to conclude, and churches that answer them honestly may still disagree. The questions concern how a church holds what it has been given, whatever position it takes.

## 6 Conclusion

I began with a bishop who held a remedy that he could not deliver and who lent it, for a time, to lay men and women. The argument since has been that his position is the position of every church when a community suffers together. A church proclaims a remedy that it did not make and does not own. Its claim to be trusted is tested by how it holds that remedy: whether it is present with those who suffer, whether care reaches them, whether it shares the means of grace or guards them, and whether it can name its own failures and change.

Three scenes gave this claim a shape. They did not prove it, and they were not asked to. They cannot show that suffering erodes trust, that standing with sufferers earns it, or that confession restores it. Rival readings of each scene remain open, and I have named them. The scenes do show that the questions are real ones. A bishop, a planter, a formerly enslaved bishop, an abbot and a church assembly each wrote, in their own words, about who held the remedy and for whom.

The norm comes from Scripture. The flock is God's and not the shepherds'. God is found beside those who cry out. Those who hold office will give an account. A church that has failed can confess and reform.

For churches in Taiwan, and for others, the result is a set of questions and not a programme. They can be asked by churches that disagree about much else. They ask a church to attend less to whether it is trusted and more to whether it is trustworthy with what it has been given.
