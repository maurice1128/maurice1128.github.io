# Who Holds the Remedy? Collective Suffering, Faith and Trust in the Church, with Reflections for the Church in Taiwan

Draft of 2026-11-07. Live doc: https://claude.ai/artifact/G4DJj3NGdHu6pyQmpvg3Bj

## Abstract

Collective suffering is often said to strengthen faith while eroding trust in the church. This article asks where devotion goes when Christians suffer together, and why. It offers a systematic narrative synthesis of historical and contemporary cases: the plague of 1347–51, enslaved and freed African Americans, Brazil, Ireland, Korea, Rwanda, the Andes and Taiwan. Church role is coded from records of ordination, property, sacraments, funds and care, independently of outcomes. Six outcomes emerge: faith and institution rising together; devotion redistributed within the church; migration to sufferer-controlled bodies; rejection of Christianity; functional displacement by state and medicine; and institutional self-correction. Suffering rarely drives Christians from God. It redistributes devotion toward whichever body holds the prescribed remedy and delivers care. The decisive conditions are the church's position relative to power and perpetrators, the theology of suffering and who controls its remedy, and the presence of an organised alternative. A biblical-theological reading, kept apart from the descriptive analysis, finds warrant for lament, for judging leaders by their care for the weak, and for institutional self-correction on the pattern of Acts 6. The article closes with neutral questions for churches in Taiwan, drawing on the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan's public statements and on survey evidence of trust between 2009 and 2018.

**Keywords:** collective suffering; trust in the church; theodicy; plague of 1347–51; African American religion; Presbyterian Church in Taiwan

## 1 Introduction

When a people suffers together, what happens to its faith and to its church? A familiar answer holds that faith survives while trust in the institution falls. After 1348, the story runs, Europeans kept praying but lost confidence in their clergy. Specialists dispute that story for the plague (Cohn 1992; Stearns 2009). The puzzle behind it is nonetheless real. In some settings suffering bound people more tightly to their church. In others it sent them to new bodies, and occasionally away from Christianity altogether.

This article separates two things the commonplace runs together. *Faith* here means devotion directed to God: prayer, intercession and the search for meaning. *Institutional trust* means confidence that a particular church, its clergy and its rites can carry that devotion. The two can move apart. In the United States, Catholic abuse scandals produced a long-lasting fall in Catholic affiliation and attendance. The same study found no statistically significant fall in belief in God or an afterlife, though its belief estimates are imprecise (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015). A recent study of Ukraine at war likewise separates trust in God from trust in the church, and reports trust shifting toward the army and volunteers (Ishchuk 2026, abstract).

The argument of this article is simple to state. Collective suffering rarely drives Christians away from God. It redistributes devotion toward whichever body holds the prescribed remedy and delivers care. Where that body is the sufferers' own, faith and institution rise together. Where the establishment fails, or sides with those who inflict the suffering, devotion moves to lay or sufferer-controlled bodies. Rejection of Christianity follows only where a rival sacred system is still alive.

None of the parts of this argument is wholly new, and the debts should be stated plainly. Weber tied theodicy to social stratum: privileged groups seek assurance that their fortune is deserved, while the disprivileged turn to salvation and reversal (Weber 1946b). Martin (1978) and Casanova (1994) showed that the fate of religion depends on church–state configuration. Where a monopoly church is allied with elites, anticlericalism follows. Where religion carries national identity against an outside oppressor, as in Poland and Ireland, it strengthens. Hirschman (1970) showed that members leave a failing organisation only when an alternative exists.

Economists have since shown that shocks have conditional religious effects. Belloc, Drago and Galbiati (2016) found that earthquakes in north-central Italian cities between 1000 and 1300 delayed the move to communal self-government where the bishop also held political power. In the published version, those episcopal-see cities also built and decorated more religious buildings after quakes. Dittmar and Meisenzahl (2020) found that plague outbreaks between 1500 and 1522 predict German cities' adoption of Protestant church ordinances, that is, once an organised alternative existed. They report no comparable institutional effect for the Black Death (Dittmar and Meisenzahl 2020, 988 n. 63).

Closer still, several student projects compare the Black Death with other catastrophes. Zacharia (2018) sets it beside the 1666 London fire and the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, and argues that the plague exposed the Church's institutional shortcomings. Carmody (2012) compares it with cholera in nineteenth-century London. Boone (2013) reads a theodicy of redemptive suffering in the Black community's service during the 1793 Philadelphia yellow fever. The claim that faith survived while trust in the church fell is therefore a commonplace, not a finding.

What this article adds is narrower. First, it names a mechanism for the conditionality that economists have measured: control of the prescribed remedy, as distinct from the provision of care. Second, it distinguishes devotion *redistributed within* a church from devotion that *exits* to sufferer-controlled bodies. Third, it codes the church's role from records of control and care, independently of outcomes, so that the explanation does not restate what it explains. To our knowledge, no study has combined these elements across historical and contemporary cases. That claim is provisional until database searches with institutional access are complete *[verify: JSTOR, ProQuest and ATLA searches]*.

The method is a systematic narrative synthesis. Cases were drawn from a structured review of scholarship in English, Chinese and other languages, covering theory, theologies of suffering, plague, slave religion, comparative and global cases, and contemporary surveys. A case entered when a Christian population, or one living under Christian institutions, suffered collectively and when sources recorded both devotion and the channel it took. Cases with contrasting outcomes were added deliberately, to avoid selecting on the outcome (Geddes 1990; Seawright and Gerring 2008).

For the two best-documented cases, primary sources were read in full. These were fifteen plague-era texts and twelve texts by or about enslaved and freed African Americans, plus a coded sample of 51 narratives from the Federal Writers' Project interviews of the 1930s, covering 14 states (Federal Writers' Project 1941). Each case was coded on seven conditions and on five indicators of church role. Within cases, sequences were checked with hoop tests: devotion should shift to lay bodies only after a recorded failure of the establishment (Collier 2011; Mahoney 2012). Rival explanations were then set against the same evidence.

Section 2 presents the six outcomes and their strongest cases. Section 3 explains the patterns, sets out how church role was coded and weighs rival explanations. Section 4 turns, in an explicitly normative register, to a biblical-theological reading. Section 5 offers reflections for the church in Taiwan, and section 6 states the limits of the argument.

## 2 Six outcomes of collective suffering

Across the cases, collective suffering almost always raised the demand for intercession, meaning and community. Where that demand went varied a great deal. Six outcomes recur, summarised in table 1, and a seventh minor branch, loss of faith, is noted at the end of this section. Most outcome evidence measures belonging and practice more than belief, so the faith column records the direction the sources report, not a measured level.

### 2.1 Outcome A: faith and institution rise together

Where the church belongs to the sufferers, and the perpetrator stands outside it, faith and institution tend to rise together. Ireland is the classic case. Larkin (1972) argued that a "devotional revolution" between 1850 and 1875 made Irish Catholics a uniformly practising people. Irish Catholics were subordinate to a Protestant state and landed elite, while their church was their own. Two qualifications apply. Miller (1975) showed that pre-Famine attendance was high in the anglophone east and low in the Irish-speaking west. Because the Famine killed or removed the western poor disproportionately, part of the rise may be a composition effect. Larkin himself later traced its beginnings to parish missions of 1825–46 (Larkin 2014).

The early church supplies an older instance. During the Cyprianic plague of the third century, Christians were a minority without state power. Stark (1996) argues that their nursing of the sick raised survival and drew converts, although the size of that effect is contested. The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan under martial law, discussed in section 5, also fits this outcome. Its gain was loyalty within its own constituency more than numbers, since its 1955–65 growth campaign predates its political turn.

The Jews of 1348–51 offer a contrast outside Christianity. They were a tolerated minority, and the violence came from Christian neighbours. Clement VI's bulls of 4 July and 26 September 1348 rejected the poisoning charge. Raynaldus's *Annales*, however, record that the accusation could not be quelled anywhere except in the county around Avignon (Raynaldus 1880, 25:455). The sufferers' devotion stayed within their own communal institutions, which the synthesis codes as outcome A outside Christianity. The case tests whether the mechanism reaches beyond Christianity; it says nothing directly about Christian outcomes.

### 2.2 Outcome B: devotion redistributed within the church

For most Christians in plague-era Europe, devotion rose but flowed through lay channels inside the church. The hierarchy recorded its own failure in its own registers. On 10 January 1349, Ralph of Shrewsbury, bishop of Bath and Wells, wrote that priests could not be found to serve the dying:

> "cum non inveniantur presbyteri, qui velint devotionis zelo, aut pro stipendio aliquo curas … subire … confiteantur … sua peccata etiam laico, et si desit vir laicus, mulieri" (Wilkins 1737, 2:745)

In draft translation: since no priests can be found willing to take on the cure, from zeal or for any stipend, the dying should confess their sins even to a layman, and if no layman is present, to a woman *[verify translation]*. The same mandate required survivors to confess again to their own priest (Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46). The bishop delegated the remedy and then reclaimed it. In 1350 Archbishop Islip complained that surviving priests left the cure of souls for better-paid chantry masses (Wilkins 1737, 3:1).

Lay initiative grew, but inside the church rather than against it. Henderson (1994) places Florentine confraternal charity within a network of church, state, guild and hospital. Cohn's (1992) study of testaments from six Tuscan and Umbrian cities finds the decisive change after the second plague of 1363, not 1348. Testators then concentrated bequests into chapels, perpetual masses and named artworks, given to church institutions. The change was in the form of piety, not necessarily its quantity.

Brazil shows the same outcome under slavery. Catholicism was established, and religious orders were major slaveholders; the Jesuits had the most extensive operations (Schwartz 1982). Yet enslaved and freed Africans held their own chartered lay brotherhoods, the *irmandades*. These handled burial and festivals, elected their own officers and sometimes aided manumission (Russell-Wood 1974). In the 1836 Cemiterada, Salvador's population rioted to defend church burial against a secularising reform (Reis 1991). That is the opposite of institutional distrust. Brazil matters because the enslaved there were as powerless as in the US South, yet they controlled their own church bodies. The case therefore separates church role from power position.

### 2.3 Outcome C: migration to sufferer-controlled bodies

Migration occurs when the establishment fails the sufferers or sides with those who harm them, and an alternative body exists or can be built. The flagellants of 1349 show a brief version. At Tournai, lay penitent companies took over the clergy's funeral role and its income:

> "societates penitentium cum cruce et cereis corpora levant, portant et sepeliunt; et sepe inducti sunt morientes ut talibus donent, sicut solet fieri curatis" (li Muisis, in Lemaître 1906, 240)

That is, the penitent companies lifted, carried and buried the dead with cross and candles, and the dying were often induced to leave them gifts, as was customary for curates *[verify translation]*. Li Muisis adds that much of the crowd valued the penitents' rite above the divine office and often derided the clergy who opposed it (Lemaître 1906, 239). The movement's own oath asked the curate's licence and swore to defend the rights of the Church (Lemaître 1906, 243). It did not set out to leave the church, and it was suppressed within about a year (section 3).

The clearest case of migration is the religion of enslaved African Americans. Slaveholders' control of the means of grace was explicit. The planter-missionary Charles Colcock Jones conceded as much:

> "It is not too much, therefore to say that the Negroes are in a state of almost absolute dependence upon their owners for the words of eternal life." (Jones 1842, 158)

The interviews of former slaves show both control and its evasion. In a coded sample of 51 Federal Writers' Project narratives, 31 report white-controlled or supervised worship. Twenty-six report Black-led worship before 1865, 12 of them secret (Federal Writers' Project 1941; author's coding). In the 42 systematically sampled narratives the figures are 26 and 19. This is the "invisible institution" that Raboteau ([1978] 2004) described.

Black authors consistently separated God from the institution that claimed to mediate him. Frederick Douglass put it most sharply:

> "I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land." (Douglass 1845, appendix)

Free Black Methodists in Philadelphia made the same separation in institutional form. After trustees of St George's church pulled Absalom Jones off his knees at prayer, the Black members "went out of the church in a body" (Allen [1833] 1880, 14–15). When threatened with expulsion from the Methodist connection, they replied:

> "and if you deny us your name, you cannot seal up the scriptures from us, and deny us a name in heaven." (Allen [1833] 1880, 16–17)

In 1816 they founded the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church, in Allen's words to escape "spiritual despotism" (Allen [1833] 1880, 24).

After 1865, freedpeople moved in large numbers into the AME and Black Baptist churches. Fountain (2010) argues that fewer than 40 percent of datable conversions occurred before freedom, so that formal commitment followed emancipation. The full texts qualify this. Only 7 of the 51 coded narratives date a conversion, and 5 of those fall after 1865. Yet half the sample reports Black-led worship before 1865. In 1869 the AME weekly reported that whole Kentucky congregations remained "a unit" in the AME when the Southern Methodists tried to win them back (*Christian Recorder*, 16 Jan. 1869). The evidence therefore supports a postwar move of people already Christian into Black-run churches more securely than a postwar first conversion. Some freedpeople did stay with, or return to, the Southern Methodist church (*Christian Recorder* 1866–69).

Twentieth-century cases repeat the pattern under very different conditions. In colonial Korea most Protestant bodies accepted Shinto shrine rites, while a minority refused and went to prison. After 1945 the resisters would not rejoin a church that had complied, and they formed separate bodies (Kim Sung-Gun 1989; Kim Sŭng-t'ae 1991; Yi Chin-gu 2025). An outside perpetrator therefore does not guarantee outcome A: a compliant establishment lost its resisters.

Rwanda is the starkest modern case. Longman (2010) argues that the churches consistently allied themselves with the state and played ethnic politics, giving them substantial responsibility for the 1994 genocide. Tutsi victims belonged to the same churches whose hierarchies were aligned with Hutu Power. Devotion did not collapse; it moved. Pentecostal communities multiplied after 1994, and Kubai (2007a) links their spread to needs for healing, a rebuilt social fabric and a post-ethnic identity. She also finds Catholicism still dominant, so the shift is partial. In West Africa, the Aladura churches arose among Yoruba Anglicans during the 1918–19 influenza and the crises of the 1920s, when mission churches would not practise prayer-healing (Turner 1967; Peel 1968).

### 2.4 Outcome D: rejection of Christianity

Outright rejection is rare, and it requires a living rival. The Taki Onqoy movement in the Andes (c. 1564–80) is the clearest instance. Its preachers taught that the *huacas*, the Andean sacred powers, were punishing the people with disease for accepting the Christian god, and they called for a return to *huaca* worship (Stern 1982b; Millones 1990). The friars read the same epidemics as punishment for idolatry. One suffering supported opposite theodicies, and Christianity was the conquerors' religion. In Brazil, the 1835 Malê uprising was organised by African Muslims outside Catholicism (Reis 1986).

The option of rejection was visible even where it was not taken. David Walker stated, and then refused, the inference that Christianity was a weapon of the oppressor:

> "one might believe it was a plan fabricated by themselves and the devils to oppress us. But hark! My master has taught me better than to believe it" (Walker 1830, art. III, 40)

### 2.5 Outcome E: functional displacement

From the eighteenth century, states and medicine competed with churches for the demand that suffering creates. After the Lisbon earthquake of 1755, Pombal's state directed reconstruction. The Jesuit Gabriel Malagrida, who blamed the disaster on sin, was later executed by the Inquisition for heresy, not for that sermon (Molesky 2015). Neiman (2002) treats Lisbon as the moment natural and moral evil began to separate in European thought. Rosenberg (1962) traces American responses to cholera from 1832, when it was widely read as divine judgment on sin, to 1866, when it was a sanitary problem for boards of health.

COVID-19 shows the same pattern in a secure society. Google searches for prayer rose worldwide in early 2020, beginning before churches closed, and faded in most regions by about October (Bentzen 2021). In 2025, 69 percent of Americans said the pandemic had not affected their religious life (Pew Research Center 2025b). Under displacement, churches keep consolation while state and medicine take over explanation and protection.

### 2.6 Outcome F: institutional self-correction

The last outcome is the rarest. A church admits that it has failed a group of sufferers and shares control of the failing ministry with them. The canonical pattern is Acts 6:1–7. The Hellenists complained that their widows were neglected in the daily distribution; the apostles did not deny it; and seven men with Greek names were chosen to oversee the distribution. Luke then reports that the number of disciples "increased greatly" (Acts 6:7). The text is a canonical narrative, not a historical case, and it is coded here for its pattern. The PCT's 1990 apology to the families of 228 victims is a partial modern instance: it confesses failure, but its effect on trust has not been studied (section 5).

### 2.7 A minor branch: loss of faith

Some sufferers read their suffering as unredeemable and moved toward humanism or a transformed theism. Black theologians later gave this branch its sharpest form (Jones [1973] 1998; Pinn 1995). Before 1877 it is thinly documented. One bereaved mother in Harriet Jacobs's narrative prays, "God has hid himself from me" (Jacobs 1861, ch. XIII). The sources say little about how common such loss was, and the article does not claim to measure it.
