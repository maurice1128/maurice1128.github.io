**How the journal's articles combine history, Scripture and ethics.**

- *A thesis argued against a named position.* The typical abstract says "I argue that…" and names whom it argues with. Okaya, for example, takes on philosophical accounts of political apology that require an authorised speaker.
- *One or two theological interlocutors, read in depth.* Aquinas, Augustine, Barth, Bonhoeffer and Oliver O'Donovan recur. Whyte's article on ecclesial repentance works through Bonhoeffer; Okaya's works through Moberly, Brueggemann and Bonhoeffer.
- *Scripture as one sustained reading.* Scripture-led articles take a single passage or book and tie it to a single question: Romans 13 read with Proverbs (Rhodes 2025), Romans 14–15 on disagreement (Errington 2025), Jeremiah 14 on confession (Okaya 2026), the Deuteronomic code as a genre (Cash 2023). Catenae of texts are rare.
- *History in the service of a present argument.* Historical articles reconstruct one debate or one figure and then draw the normative point (Hengstmengel 2026 on Dutch Reformed usury; Berkman 2024 on MacKinnon and Anscombe; Guth 2023, a "critical genealogy" of Calvin on marriage).
- *Contextual pieces from outside the North Atlantic are welcome* (Hong Kong, Indonesia, Aotearoa, China), provided the theological argument is explicit.
- *Counts and statistical appendices are almost absent.* The one article with modelling (Kivimäki 2025) is an outlier.

**What this means for the manuscript.** The subject is squarely in scope, and the journal has just published on ecclesial apology and on abuse and repentance. The form is less typical: three comparative historical scenes, a survey of biblical texts, a list of questions and a quantitative appendix, with no ethicist as interlocutor.

## 2. Reviewer 1 (Christian ethics: moral theology, ecclesiology, political theology)

### Summary of the argument as I understand it

The author asks what a church owes the people whose trust it asks when a community suffers together. Faith in God is distinguished from trust in a church. The thesis is that a church's authority is "tested by how it holds the remedy it proclaims", where "remedy" means God's answer to suffering together with the means by which a church offers it, and "holds" is meant in the sense of stewardship (1 Cor. 4:1–2). Three historical scenes are read as a "lens": the plague of 1348–62, the religion of enslaved Christians in North America, and the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan from 1947 to 1990. From them the author draws three questions (who holds the remedy, with whom the church stands, whether it can correct itself). Scripture is then asked to supply the norm, and the result is a set of six questions for churches in Taiwan. The author takes no position on any contested issue.

### Main strengths

1. **A real and timely question.** What makes a church's claim to be trusted a warranted claim is a question for ecclesial ethics, and this journal's readers are already discussing it (Whyte 2024; Okaya 2026; do Vale 2026).
2. **The opening image.** Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate is a memorable case, and the question of what to do with a remedy one holds but cannot deliver is a good one.
3. **Primary sources from more than one tradition and language.** Latin registers and chronicles, slave narratives, and a church document quoted in Chinese are unusual in this journal and welcome.
4. **Intellectual honesty.** The author names rival readings, admits the partiality of the sources and refuses causal claims.
5. **The stewardship idea has promise.** "A church does not make the remedy it proclaims. It receives it and holds it for others" (§1 ¶4) could carry an article. The use of Mein on Ezekiel 34 to make the same point (§4, "Shepherds and the flock") is the best exegetical moment in the paper.
6. **Clear, plain prose.**

### Major concerns

**1. There is not yet an ethical argument.** The manuscript says "The question is ethical before it is historical or sociological" (§1 ¶2) and "My thesis is normative" (§1 ¶4). What it delivers is description, a list of questions, and a summary of biblical themes.

- The author says so: "I add no new fact. I offer a way of putting an ethical question to cases like these, and a set of questions that a church can put to itself" (§1 ¶5), and "the result is a set of questions and not a programme" (§6 ¶4).
- The normative content is stated in four sentences at the end of §4: "The remedy and the flock belong to God… God is with those who cry out… Those who hold office will give an account… a church that has failed can say so and change." No Christian tradition denies any of these. They do not decide a single case in the paper.
- The thesis itself says authority is "tested by" four things. It never says what passing or failing looks like, or what follows from failing.

The test of an ethical argument is whether it rules something out. I would ask the author to pass judgment on the paper's own cases:

- Was Ralph right to require those who recovered to confess again to a priest?
- Was Clement VI right to suppress the flagellant companies while leaving penance at home untouched?
- Was the 1833 Synod wrong to "separate entirely the civil and religious condition of this people" (§2.2 ¶2), and why, on the author's criteria?
- What exactly did the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan owe in 1947 that it did not give?

At present the lens observes these cases and judges none of them.

**2. "Who holds the remedy" is not yet a defined concept, and parts of the apparatus are circular or inconsistent.**

- *The definition is too elastic.* "By 'remedy' I mean whatever a church announces as God's answer to suffering, together with the means by which it offers that answer: the word, the sacraments, prayer, burial, absolution and material care" (§1 ¶4). In the scenes the term then covers sacramental absolution, death bells, literacy, church buildings, nursing, lament and a human-rights declaration. A concept that covers all of these explains none of them.
- *The concept does two jobs.* As the first of three questions it is descriptive: who ordains, owns, administers and funds (§3 ¶2). As the thesis it is the umbrella under which all three questions fall: "how it holds the remedy it proclaims: whether it stands with those who suffer, whether it delivers care, whether it shares or guards the means of grace, and whether it corrects its own failures" (§1 ¶4). So "holding the remedy well" is defined by the criteria it is meant to ground.
- *The normative step is asserted, not argued.* "The question is not whether control is legitimate, since a church must have order. It is whether those who hold the remedy hold it for the people who need it" (§3 ¶2). What counts as holding it "for" them is the whole question. "For the people" cannot be read off the four records.
- *The lists do not map onto one another.* The thesis has four tests; §3 has three questions; §3 ¶5 has five records; §4 ends with four statements; §5 has six questions. "Five records, then, answer the three questions" (§3 ¶5) is not accurate on the paper's own account: four records answer the first question, one answers the second, and none answers the third.
- *The non-circularity rule is broken by the author.* §3 ¶1 rightly says that calling a church "the sufferers' own" because people stayed in it would only redescribe outcomes. But §1 ¶6 classifies the Taiwanese case as controlled by "the sufferers' own assemblies". The 1990 apology, as the author reads it, says that this church failed the sufferers. Which record shows that its assemblies were theirs?
- *Authority, trust and trustworthiness slide into one another.* The thesis speaks of "a church's authority" (§1 ¶4), the introduction of "a warranted claim" to be trusted (§1 ¶2), the conclusion of "its claim to be trusted" and finally of being "trustworthy" (§6). These are three different things. The closing contrast between being trusted and being trustworthy is closely associated with Onora O'Neill's work, and it is used without acknowledgement or analysis.
- *The Donatist question is not faced.* §2.2 concludes that "trust attaches to conduct and not to office". If authority is tested by conduct, does a failing church lose authority? Are its sacraments, its preaching, its absolution affected? The tradition has an answer (the minister's unworthiness does not void the gift), and this journal has just published on the point (do Vale 2026). The author needs to say whether the "test" concerns the validity of what is held, the warrant for trusting the holder, or the holder's accountability before God. I think it must be the second and third, but the manuscript does not say.
- *Is "remedy" the right theological category?* Penance was preached as a remedy for sin, not for plague. Calling the gospel a "remedy for suffering" leans towards a therapeutic account that Hauerwas, among others, has criticised: on his view the church does not have an answer to suffering so much as a community able to stay present to it. The author may reject that view, but it should be met. There is also a better pedigree for the word than a chronicler's phrase: the old language of Christ the physician and of the sacraments as medicine.

**3. The ethics literature a reader of this journal expects is missing.** The stated "debts" (§1 ¶5) are Weber, Martin, Casanova, an economics paper and two undergraduate theses, the latter cited from a repository abstract and a title. No ethicist appears until §4, where Katongole is given one sentence and Cone, the International Theological Commission and Calvin one footnote each. The following engagements are absent:

- *Ecclesial repentance and institutional sin.* Jeremy Bergen's *Ecclesial Repentance* is the standard study of church apologies of the kind the author treats. Bonhoeffer's confession of the church's guilt in the *Ethics* is the classic text; Jennifer McBride builds a theology of public witness on it; Whyte applied it to abuse in this journal. "Ecclesial repentance" is one of the manuscript's keywords and none of this is cited. The debate about structural or social sin (for example Daniel Finn's "What Is a Sinful Social Structure?") is also relevant to the question whether an institution, as distinct from its members, can sin and repent. The manuscript cites the ITC's *Memory and Reconciliation* only for a point about the addressee of biblical confessions and does not discuss its main claim, that the Church is holy while her members sin, which is the principal rival to the author's framing.
- *Who is the subject that repents?* The author gives Ezekiel 18 one sentence (§4, "Counterweights") and then praises an apology made forty-three years after the event, largely by people who were not the actors of 1947. This journal has just hosted a sharp exchange on whether present bodies owe anything for the wrongs of predecessors (Biggar 2025; Banner 2025; Reddie, Banner and Brock 2026). A reader will expect the author to say how a church is one moral subject across generations, and why Ezekiel 18 does not block that.
- *Ecclesial ethics and church authority.* Hauerwas on the church as a community of character; O'Donovan on authority and judgment, and on the church's authority as derived and answerable; Bretherton on listening, hospitality and the church's relation to power; Healy's criticism of idealised ecclesiologies and his call to attend to the concrete, sinful church; Radner on the church's divisions and violence. Any two of these would give the paper a conversation partner. The "king's sanctuary" test (§5) is a claim in political theology made without reference to any political theologian.
- *Katongole.* *Born from Lament* is cited for one point. His work on Rwanda (*Mirror to the Church*) and *The Sacrifice of Africa* concern churches that failed their people during collective violence, which is the paper's subject.
- *Lament.* Westermann, Brueggemann and O'Connor are a sound biblical base. The ethical and pastoral literature is missing: Billman and Migliore, Swinton's "practical theodicy", Rah's *Prophetic Lament*, Eklund on the laments of Jesus, and Shin in this journal. Jesson (2024, also here) on being with others who suffer bears directly on "with whom does the church stand".
- *Trust.* Baier's "Trust and Antitrust", O'Neill's *A Question of Trust* and Hawley's *How to Be Trustworthy* supply the distinctions the paper needs: trust and reliance, trust and trustworthiness, competence and commitment. The paper's first sub-criterion (care delivered) is about competence; its others are about commitment. The philosophy of trust would let the author say so precisely.
- *Abuse and institutional trust.* The introduction grounds the faith/trust distinction in a study of Catholic abuse scandals (§1 ¶3) and never returns to abuse. Yet abuse is the contemporary case in which a church itself inflicts the suffering, and it is where the theological literature on institutional trust now is (Keenan; Brock on Vanier; Panchuk on religious trauma; Whyte; do Vale). Either take the case up or do not open with it.
- *The classic Christian text on the opening question.* Luther's 1527 letter on whether one may flee a deadly plague argues that those who hold a ministry must stay. It is the obvious historical interlocutor for a paper that begins with priests who would not visit the dying, and it is not mentioned.
- *Winner* (*The Dangers of Christian Practice*) argues that Christian practices carry characteristic deformations, with slaveholders' prayer as a case. This is close to the author's second scene.

I do not ask for all of these. I ask for a real conversation with a few of them in place of the sociological paragraph.

**4. Scripture is surveyed, not argued from.**

- *The texts are selected by the lens.* "For that I turn to Scripture, read as a canon, and put to it the three questions of the lens" (§4 ¶1). The questions were derived from the scenes; Scripture is then asked those questions; it answers them. The author must show why these texts, and not others, govern the matter.
- *The texts most directly about custody of the remedy are absent.* The keys and binding and loosing (Matt. 16:19; 18:18; John 20:22–23) are where the tradition has located the question "who may absolve". James 5:14–16 ("confess your sins to one another") is the apostolic warrant for the very practice in the opening scene. The hireling who flees (John 10:11–13) and the charge to elders not to serve "for sordid gain" (1 Pet. 5:1–4) fit the plague scene exactly. And the load-bearing text of the whole paper, 1 Cor. 4:1–2 ("stewards of God's mysteries"), appears once, in a parenthesis (§1 ¶4), and is never exegeted.
- *A missed connection.* Charles Colcock Jones's sentence "we may literally shut up the kingdom of heaven against men" (§2.2 ¶1) quotes Jesus' woe against the scribes and Pharisees (Matt. 23:13, Authorised Version). A slaveholding minister describes his own class in the words of a dominical curse. The author does not notice it. This is where scene and norm could meet.
- *Acts 6 carries more than it can bear.* It supplies "the third question its pattern" (§4, "Self-correction and confession") on an inference from names: "all of them with Greek names, which suggests, though it does not prove, that the neglected group received a share of control". A reader could as easily say that the apostles kept the word and prayer for themselves (Acts 6:2–4) and delegated the tables, which is hierarchy guarding the remedy and handing over the logistics. The author needs to meet that reading.
- *One paragraph per theme.* Lament, retribution, Exodus 3, Ezekiel 34, Jeremiah 7 with Amos 7, Acts 6 with Nehemiah 9 and Daniel 9, and a paragraph of counterweights make seven movements in 1,400 words. Hebrews 13:17 is quoted for accountability, and its command to obey is then balanced by Acts 5:29 in a single sentence. This reads as proof-texting made fair by listing texts on the other side.
- *No hermeneutic is stated.* "Read as a canon" is all we are told. How does a prophetic oracle against Israel's rulers become a norm for a voluntary church? The author raises the difficulty for Jeremiah and Amos ("These texts need restraint in use") and resolves it by saying that the texts "warrant a question" but not "one church's verdict on another". That is a significant hermeneutical claim with no argument behind it.
- *The four traditions paragraph* ("Traditions weigh these texts differently, and each sees something") gives each tradition one sentence and ends "I accept the correction that the others offer". It is courteous, and it does no work.

I would prefer two or three texts read properly (1 Cor. 4 with Ezekiel 34 on stewardship; James 5 and the keys on who may absolve; Acts 6) to the present survey.

**5. The relation between scenes and norm is unclear, and the paper generalises after saying it will not.**

- §1 ¶7: "Three scenes chosen by one reader cannot establish a rule, and I do not generalise from them." §6 ¶1: "his position is the position of every church when a community suffers together." The second sentence is a generalisation, and it is the paper's main claim.
- If the scenes prove nothing and the norm comes from Scripture, what are the scenes for? The author says they give the claim "a shape" (§6 ¶2). I think the honest description is that they are paradigm cases that display distinctions, in the way cases function in casuistry. If so, say so, and choose them for the distinctions they display.
- Roughly a tenth of the text is spent saying what the scenes do not show (three "What this scene shows, and what it does not" paragraphs; the paragraph "What cannot be claimed" in §2.3; the rival readings in §3). Almost all of it concerns causal claims about what people later did. A normative argument does not need to refute causal claims it does not make. One sentence would do.

**6. "Takes no position on contested issues" is in tension with writing ethics, and the neutrality is not as clean as claimed.**

- A paper may decline to decide the first-order question (independence, marriage) and still be ethics, provided its second-order criteria have bite. Here they do not yet (concern 1).
- The neutrality is partly rhetorical. In §5 the author places three things side by side: a survey figure on disapproval of religious leaders' public comment in 2018, the 2018 referendums "in which church-based groups campaigned", and one church's pastoral letter on marriage. Then: "The coincidence in time is all that can be reported… I report these three points and take no view on them." Placing them together makes a suggestion that the disclaimer withdraws. Either argue the point or remove the juxtaposition.
- The sixth question ("whether it has listened to the people whom the matter touches most closely") is not neutral in the marriage debate, because who is "most closely touched" is one of the things in dispute.
- The criteria themselves take sides, and should. "God is found beside those who cry out" (§4, "The God who sees and knows") is the preferential option in other words. The author concedes "my reading leans towards the first of these" traditions. Own it and defend it, and then say what it does and does not imply for the two Taiwanese controversies.

**7. Scope and definitions.**

- *"Collective suffering" is not defined.* An epidemic lasting months, a system of bondage lasting centuries and a massacre followed by forty years of martial law are different kinds of thing. What makes suffering collective, and why does the collective case raise a different question from the suffering of one parishioner?
- *Who are the sufferers?* Members only? In the second scene perpetrators and sufferers belong to the same churches. In the first, the largest group of people killed by Christians in 1348–49, the Jewish communities, does not appear at all (Reviewer 2 will say more). Does a church owe the remedy to those outside it?
- *The introduction's evidence does not match the scenes.* The faith/trust distinction is supported from abuse scandals and from wartime Ukraine (§1 ¶3), neither of which is a scene.

**8. Originality.** The descriptive observation is conceded not to be new (§1 ¶3). The scenes are familiar and add "no new fact". The questions for churches are sensible and general. The possible contribution is conceptual: custody of the means of grace as the place where a church's trustworthiness is tested. That contribution has to be made against existing work on ecclesial repentance (Bergen, the ITC, Whyte, Okaya), and it is not.

### Minor concerns

1. Abstract: 209 words; it lists biblical references in place of stating the argument ("Scripture supplies the norm: lament, the critique of retribution, Ezekiel 34, Jeremiah 7, Amos 7 and Acts 6").
2. "Means of grace" is a confessional term with a precise sense in Lutheran, Reformed and Wesleyan theology. Burial and bell-ringing are not means of grace in that sense. Define the usage or change the term.
3. §1 ¶5: citing undergraduate theses from an abstract and a title ("Boone's title announces a study…") weakens the paper. Cut.
4. §5, fifth question: a British study of religious transmission is used for a question about Taiwan, followed by "Whether the same holds in Taiwan is not known". Cut or replace.
5. The appendix describes three quantitative analyses on which "the argument of the article does not depend". Then they do not belong in this article.
6. §4, "Lament as permitted speech": the Jacobs example (the mother in the class meeting) is powerful and should be developed; at present it is two sentences at the end of a paragraph of citations.
7. §4: "Modern confessions… are addressed also to those who were wronged. That goes beyond the usual biblical form." Matthew 5:23–24 and Luke 19:8 are the obvious New Testament texts on being reconciled to the person wronged, and neither is mentioned.
8. The conclusion's list of authors ("A bishop, a planter, a formerly enslaved bishop, an abbot and a church assembly") omits the women (Jacobs; the sisters of the Hôtel-Dieu) who are among the paper's best witnesses.
9. The three scenes are narrated in very different ways (close Latin quotation; a mosaic of American voices; a list of dates for Taiwan). The unevenness shows.

### Recommendation: major revision

I considered recommending rejection, because in its present form the manuscript is a well-documented prolegomenon to an argument and not the argument. I recommend major revision because the question is right for this journal, the primary-source work is unusual, and the stewardship idea could carry a real thesis. The next version must (a) define its central concept and say what follows when a church fails the test, (b) pass judgment on its own cases, (c) argue from two or three biblical texts in place of a survey, and (d) engage the literature on ecclesial repentance, church authority and trust. If it comes back as the same paper with more footnotes, I would recommend rejection.

## 3. Reviewer 2 (church history: late medieval and American religious history; Taiwan's churches)

### Summary of the argument as I understand it

The author uses three historical episodes to frame a question for churches: when people suffer together, who controls the church's "remedy", whom does the church stand with, and can it correct itself? The episodes are the Black Death (mainly Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate of January 1349, Archbishop Islip's *Effrenata*, Gilles li Muisis on Tournai and Clement VI's condemnation of the flagellants), slavery and the Black churches in the United States (C. C. Jones, Jacobs, Douglass, Walker, Allen, the *Christian Recorder*), and the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) from the violence of 1947 to its apology of 1990. Biblical norms follow, and then questions for churches in Taiwan today, with survey figures on trust in religious bodies.

### Main strengths

1. **Primary sources are quoted in the original, with locators,** and the author is frank that the witnesses are senior clergy, abolitionist authors, a planter and a church speaking about itself.
2. **The historiographical cautions on the plague are right.** The use of Cohn, Henderson, Duffy and Robinson against the old "collapse of trust" story is accurate, and the author avoids the textbook errors.
3. **The treatment of the ex-slave interviews is properly cautious,** and Fountain's argument about the timing of conversion is represented fairly.
4. **The PCT apology is quoted in Chinese and described accurately as addressed both to the families and to God.** Few Anglophone readers know the text.
5. **The author sees that care was "withheld and delivered in the same months, by different members of the same church"** (§2.1). That is the right instinct for all three scenes.

### Major concerns

**1. The plague scene misdescribes its central document.** The article opens: "The church of his day taught that the remedy for the plague was penance, and that priests held it. The bishop now recorded that he could not deliver it. He had to decide what to do with a remedy that he held but could not bring" (§1 ¶1). §2.1 then speaks of a remedy "delegated by a bishop and then reclaimed", and §3 says "Ralph lent it."

- Confession to a lay person in necessity was established teaching long before 1349. It rests on a pseudo-Augustinian text taken up by Gratian and Peter Lombard (*Sentences* IV, d. 17) and is discussed by Aquinas (*Summa theologiae*, Supplement, q. 8, a. 2). The same teaching held that such a confession is not sacramental absolution and that a penitent who survives must go to a priest. Teetaert's *La confession aux laïques dans l'Église latine* (1926) is the standard study. I give these references from memory and the author should check them.
- The mandate itself says so. In the passage the author summarises, the dying are to confess to one another *juxta doctrinam apostoli*, "according to the teaching of the apostle" (James 5:16). The manuscript's paraphrase omits those words.
- The phrase *suo proprio sacerdoti* echoes the Fourth Lateran Council's canon on annual confession (*Omnis utriusque sexus*, c. 21), which the manuscript never mentions.

So Ralph did not "decide" to "lend" anything. He told his clergy to publish what the schools already taught, and the "condition" was part of the doctrine. That weakens the claim that the remedy "passed for a time into lay hands". It also makes the scene more interesting ethically: the doctrine of necessity already said that the remedy was not finally the priests' to withhold. The notable thing in the letter is the bishop's admission that priests could not be found "out of zeal or for any stipend".

**2. The key word is taken from a passage where it means something else.** "The word is borrowed from one of the sources. The abbot Gilles li Muisis wrote that the clergy of Tournai took no care *de remedio apponendo*" (§1 ¶4). On the manuscript's own account (§2.1), the clergy and gravediggers were tolling bells day and night for profit "until the whole town was afraid", and the governors then made ordinances. The *remedium* in that sentence is a remedy for an abuse of bell-ringing, not "God's answer to suffering". What the town "took over" was public order at funerals. If the ordinances went further, into moral legislation meant to turn away God's anger (as plague ordinances often did), the author should say so and quote them, because that would support the reading. As written, the title's word rests on a pun, and the "third route" (§2.1, "I read these texts as showing three routes") does not show the remedy passing into lay hands.

**3. The flagellants and the missing Jews.**

- The author summarises Clement VI's charges as contempt for the keys and carrying the cross "without the licence of a superior" (§2.1). As I recall the bull, it also charges the companies and their hangers-on with shedding the blood of Jews "whom Christian piety receives and sustains", and of Christians, and with seizing property. Historians dispute how far that charge was fair (Kieckhefer 1974 is the place to start), but a summary that leaves it out makes the pope's act look like a defence of clerical monopoly and nothing else.
- The bull is generally cited as *Inter sollicitudines*. The author's note gives a different incipit for the copy printed in Raynaldus. Explain the relation.
- Much more seriously, an article on "collective suffering" during the plague, which asks "with whom does the church stand", never mentions the massacres of Jews in 1348–51 or Clement VI's two bulls of 1348 in their defence. That is the plague-era case in which the head of the Latin church stood, in words at least, with a suffering community that was not its own, and in which many Christians, including clergy and town councils, did the opposite. Its absence also makes "no human agent" (§1 ¶6) a misleading description of whose suffering the plague scene contains.

**4. The second scene runs together two different situations.** Its heading is "Enslaved Christians in North America", and it opens: "those who inflicted it were Christians, and they controlled the church" (§2.2 ¶1).

- The Philadelphia material concerns free Black Methodists in a Northern city (St George's, the founding of the AME Church in 1816, the yellow fever of 1793). Their dispute with white Methodist elders was about seating, pulpit and property, in a denomination whose early Discipline condemned slaveholding. The plantation mission in Liberty County, Georgia, is a different world.
- Recent work shows that most Southern evangelical churches before 1865 were biracial, with Black majorities in many, and that Black members shaped them (Irons on Virginia; Sparks on Mississippi). "They controlled the church" is true of plantation missions. It is too simple for the churches as a whole, and a simpler picture makes a cleaner contrast than the evidence allows.
- The 1794 *Narrative* of Jones and Allen is used to show "what it did with a remedy in its own hands" (§2.2). It is an apologetic pamphlet, written to answer Mathew Carey's published accusation that Black nurses had profiteered. Black Philadelphians had been asked to serve on the false medical belief that they were immune. Neither fact is mentioned. Newman's biography of Allen and Nash's *Forging Freedom* give the context.
- "None of the three scenes offers a clear case in which a church shared control with those it had neglected" (§3). The second scene contains a contested one. In 1870 the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, set apart its remaining Black members as the Colored Methodist Episcopal Church and, as I recall, transferred property to it. AME writers of the time treated the new body as the creature of the old masters. Was that sharing the remedy, or shedding responsibility for it? The case would test the lens better than Acts 6 does. Hildebrand's *The Times Were Strange and Stirring* and Dvorak's *An African-American Exodus* are the studies I would begin with.
- The paragraph on the post-1865 exodus argues about why people joined Black-run churches ("commitment followed control of worship"; Fountain as rival). That is a causal question which the article says it is not asking.

**5. The scenes are convenient.** The author gives three reasons for the selection (§1 ¶6) and admits that "Three scenes chosen by one reader cannot establish a rule". But the selection does more than fail to prove a rule. It builds the conclusion into the cases.

- The stated contrasts are neater than the history. *Who inflicted the suffering: "no human agent, fellow Christians, and a state."* In 1348–51 human agents killed Jews in great numbers. In Taiwan after 1947 the head of the party-state was a baptised Methodist and several Mandarin-speaking Protestant churches stood close to the regime, so "fellow Christians" belongs to the third scene as well.
- *Who controlled the church: "a clerical hierarchy, slaveholders, and the sufferers' own assemblies."* The third term assumes what the 1990 apology denies.
- There is no hard case for the thesis. A church that kept tight hold of the remedy and served sufferers well, or a sufferers' own church that failed them, would test the lens. The second scene has material for the latter (the AME's own disputes about governance; the CME) and the author passes over it.
- The plague scene uses England for the hierarchy, Tournai for the laity and Avignon for the pope. Each place supplies the element the argument needs.
- Three scenes in 3,000 words means that none is treated at the depth a historian would accept. The Taiwanese scene, on which the article's application depends, is the thinnest (about 600 words).

I would advise either two scenes done properly, with Taiwan as the destination, or an explicit statement that the scenes are illustrations chosen to display distinctions, with the hard cases included.

**6. The Taiwan material is too thin for the weight it carries.** Taiwan is in the abstract and the keywords and is called "the worked example" (§5 ¶1). It receives about 600 words in §2.3 and about 980 in §5, a third of which are caveats about survey data.

- *Whose silence in 1947?* "The documents read for this article include no public statement by the church about that violence before 1987" (§2.3 ¶2). That is an argument from the author's reading list. As I understand it, the church was at that date two synods, north and south, founded by different missions, and its General Assembly was constituted only in 1951; the author should check this. If so, who is the "it" that was silent? That question is the ethical question of corporate identity over time, and the article should ask it.
- *Its own dead.* Presbyterians were prominent among those killed in 1947; the cases usually named include Lin Mosei (林茂生) in Taipei and the physician Chang Chi-lang (張七郎) and his sons in Hualien. The author should verify these against the standard accounts. "it failed to stand with its own people" (§2.3 ¶4) is then literal, and the scene should show it.
- *"Its organised effort went into growth"* (§2.3 ¶2). This implies that the Double the Church movement was a substitute for solidarity. No evidence is given. The church's growth in those years also owed much to Indigenous conversions, which belong to a different story.
- *"Our Appeal" is named and not described* (§2.3 ¶3). It is, as I recall, "Our Appeal Concerning the Bible, the Church and the Nation", and it followed the authorities' confiscation of Bibles in Taiwanese (romanised) and in an Indigenous language earlier in 1975. A state seizing vernacular Bibles is the Taiwanese case of "who controls the word". It is the one event that would tie the third scene to the second and to the paper's first question, and it is passed over in a clause.
- *The cost of speaking.* The only cost recorded is the confiscation of one issue of the church weekly in 1987, on the weekly's own later account. The arrest and imprisonment of the church's General Secretary, Kao Chun-ming, after the Kaohsiung Incident of December 1979 is the best-known instance and is absent.
- *What happened after 1990?* The paper's third question asks "whether anything then changes in who holds the remedy". For the PCT the author does not say. Did the church change anything in its practice, its liturgy (memorial services), its governance or its use of resources? How did victims' families respond, at the time or since? The President's public apology came in 1995, so the church spoke five years before the state. Okaya's recent article in this journal is about exactly that situation.
- *Context of the 1971 statement.* It was issued two months after the Republic of China lost its United Nations seat and shortly before President Nixon's visit to Beijing. Its plea for self-determination addressed the fear of being disposed of by others. Without this the reader cannot judge whether it was "a church standing with a people under pressure" or "alignment with one part of a divided society" (§2.3 ¶5).

**7. Fairness and balance on Taiwan.**

- *"The church in Taiwan" is one church.* §5 is headed "Reflections for the church in Taiwan" and promises questions "for churches of every tradition and political leaning". All the evidence concerns the PCT. The Catholic Church, the Mandarin-speaking denominations that arrived after 1949, and the independent Chinese churches are not mentioned, although their different relations to the party-state are the natural comparison for "with whom does the church stand".
- *The only failure named is a failure to oppose the government early enough.* A reader who is not a Presbyterian, or not sympathetic to the 1977 call for "a new and independent country", will not find the frame neutral. The only critical voice (Chiu Kai-li) appears to criticise the PCT's political theology from within the same tradition. Is there a view from outside it, and what did other churches say about the 1977 Declaration at the time?
- *Whose suffering?* The apology is addressed to "the victims of 228 and their families". The first days of the 1947 violence also included attacks on mainlanders by crowds, and the political victims of the following decades included many mainlanders. A neutral account should say which sufferers the church's later statements had in view and which they did not.
- *Indigenous members.* A large share of PCT congregations today are Indigenous. The article's "sufferers' own assemblies" and its questions never mention them, though the Bibles seized in 1975 included an Indigenous-language edition and the church's disaster ministry has largely been among them.
- *The "king's sanctuary" question* (§5): "so can a church that has bound itself to those who seek power" will be read in Taiwan as aimed at the PCT's relation to one political camp. That may be fair. If it is meant, argue it with evidence; if it is not, rephrase.
- *The 2018 paragraph* suggests a link between church campaigning in the referendums and public disapproval and then says "no causal link has been shown". That is unfair to the churches that campaigned. The same paragraph is also thin on the PCT, whose 2014 pastoral letter did not, as I understand it, settle the church's own internal disagreement.

**8. The survey figures cannot bear on the argument.** The author concedes that the trust question covers "religious groups of every tradition", that Protestants were 4 to 6 per cent of respondents, that the figures "say nothing specific about Christian churches", and that the tables "appear to be unweighted" (§5 ¶3). Most religious affiliation in Taiwan is Buddhist, Daoist and folk-religious, and public controversies about large Buddhist and temple organisations in those years are at least as likely to lie behind such a trend as anything churches did. The series should be cut or reduced to a footnote. The item on religious leaders "commenting publicly on whether the country is democratic" needs its exact wording in Chinese and English before any use is made of it.

**9. Disaster is promised and not delivered.** The first Taiwanese question refers to "an earthquake, a typhoon, an epidemic or a political injury" (§5). No instance is examined. The 1999 earthquake, Typhoon Morakot in 2009 and the COVID-19 restrictions on worship each have a documentary record of church action, and the first two would allow the author to test "Is care delivered, and seen by those who need it?" against evidence. In Taiwan a Buddhist relief organisation is the most visible religious responder to disasters, which is relevant to "treat them as partners or as rivals".

### Minor concerns

1. Cite the standard English sourcebook for the plague documents (Horrox, *The Black Death*, 1994) alongside Wilkins, so that readers can check the translations; and Putnam's old but still basic article on *Effrenata* and priests' wages (*American Historical Review*, 1915).
2. On clergy and parishes after the plague, Dohar's study of Hereford and Harper-Bill's essay on the English Church are the obvious supports. Ian Forrest's *Trustworthy Men* (2018) is a study of how the medieval church depended on, and ranked, lay trust; a paper with "trust" in its title should know it.
3. Jean de Venette was a Carmelite and li Muisis a Benedictine abbot. Both had reasons to contrast the regular clergy favourably with secular priests. The note that the continuator "was a Carmelite friar" should be turned into a sentence of source criticism in the text.
4. "The plague of 1348–62" is an odd label for two English epidemics; Cohn's Italian evidence concerns 1363.
5. Douglass's 1845 *Narrative* and Jacobs's 1861 *Incidents* are paginated in their first editions. Give pages.
6. "a formerly enslaved bishop" (§6) for Richard Allen is correct, but the scene does not tell the reader that Allen had been enslaved, or when he became a bishop.
7. §2.3 ¶1: "martial law that followed and lasted until 1987" compresses the chronology. Give the dates.
8. §2.3 ¶3 calls 1971, 1975 and 1977 "three statements" and in §2.3 ¶5 "its first political statement". Were they understood as political or as confessional acts by the church itself? The 1985 Confession of Faith is relevant.
9. Romanisation is inconsistent (Hanyu Pinyin for a book title, Taiwanese romanisation for *chhut-thau-thiⁿ*, Wade–Giles for names). State a policy.
10. Montgomery (1993) is the only study cited for the post-1865 churches. Add Walker's *A Rock in a Weary Land* or Dickerson's history of the AME Church if the paragraph is kept.
11. Rubinstein (1991) is thirty-five years old. On the PCT and democratisation there is more recent work, including Kuo Cheng-tian's *Religion and Democracy in Taiwan* and Amae Yoshihisa's chapter on the PCT's role in transitional justice.

### Recommendation: major revision

The author reads sources carefully and is honest about their limits, and I would like to see the Taiwanese material reach an international readership. But the opening case is misdescribed, the governing metaphor rests on a word that means something else in its source, the largest plague-era instance of Christians inflicting suffering is missing, the American scene merges two settings, and the Taiwanese scene is a chronology of five documents. On fairness: the article is careful in tone, but its evidence comes from one church and its only named failure is that church's early silence, so it is not yet the even-handed set of questions "for churches of every tradition and political leaning" that it says it is. I would be glad to read a revised version.

## 4. Editor's decision letter

Dear author,

Thank you for submitting "Who Holds the Remedy? Collective Suffering, Trust and the Responsibilities of the Church" to *Studies in Christian Ethics*. I have now received two reports, from a Christian ethicist and from a church historian who knows Taiwan's churches. Both recommend **major revision**, and that is my decision.

**Scope.** The manuscript is within scope. The trustworthiness of churches, ecclesial repentance and the church's conduct under collective suffering are theological questions in Christian ethics, and the journal has recently published on ecclesial repentance after abuse (Whyte, 37.2), on ecclesial apology (Okaya, online first) and on whether God speaks through an abusive pastor (do Vale, online first). You cite none of these.

**Desk decision.** I sent the manuscript to review, but I came close to returning it. Three things nearly decided the matter: the paper describes its own contribution as "a set of questions and not a programme"; its stated intellectual debts are sociological and economic; and no Christian ethicist is engaged as an interlocutor. What decided me in its favour was the question itself, the primary-source work, and the Taiwanese material. In its present form the article is not publishable in this journal.

**Required revisions, in order of priority.**

1. **Make the ethical argument.** State a normative thesis that can be wrong, and defend it. Say what it means for a church's authority to be "tested", and what follows from failing (Reviewer 1, concerns 1 and 2). Pass judgment on your own cases: Ralph's requirement of a second confession, Clement VI's suppression of the flagellants, the 1833 Synod's separation of "civil and religious condition", the PCT's silence.
2. **Define the central concept and remove the circularity.** Give "remedy" and "holding" a definition that does not expand to cover every case. Distinguish authority, trust and trustworthiness. Address the Donatist question. Make the tests, questions and records consistent with one another (Reviewer 1, concern 2).
3. **Engage the literature.** Replace the paragraph on Weber, Martin, Casanova and the student theses with a real conversation with a small number of works on ecclesial repentance (Bergen; Bonhoeffer; the ITC's main argument; Whyte; Okaya), church authority and ecclesial ethics (two of Hauerwas, O'Donovan, Bretherton, Healy), and trust (O'Neill; Baier or Hawley). Say how a church is one moral subject across generations, with reference to Ezekiel 18 and to the recent exchange on inherited responsibility in this journal (Reviewer 1, concern 3).
4. **Argue from Scripture.** Reduce the survey to two or three texts read in depth, include the texts on stewardship, the keys and mutual confession, meet the counter-reading of Acts 6, and state your hermeneutic (Reviewer 1, concern 4).
5. **Correct the history.** Present Ralph's mandate as an application of the established doctrine of necessity; deal with the meaning of *remedium* in li Muisis; give Clement VI's charges in full; bring in the persecution of the Jews; separate the plantation South from free Black Philadelphia; give the context of the 1794 *Narrative*; consider the Colored Methodist Episcopal Church as a test case (Reviewer 2, concerns 1 to 4).
6. **Justify or change the selection of scenes.** Either reduce to two scenes with Taiwan as the destination, or present the scenes openly as illustrative cases and include at least one that is hard for your thesis (Reviewer 2, concern 5; Reviewer 1, concern 5).
7. **Thicken Taiwan, and be even-handed.** As it stands, the Taiwanese material does not justify its place in the abstract and keywords. Describe "Our Appeal" and the seizure of Bibles; the church's own dead in 1947; the institutional identity of the church in 1947; the imprisonment of its General Secretary; what followed the 1990 apology; other churches' relations to the party-state; Indigenous members; and one disaster case. Remove the juxtaposition in the paragraph on 2018 or argue it. Head §5 "churches", not "the church", or confine your claims to the PCT (Reviewer 2, concerns 6, 7 and 9).
8. **Remove what does not serve the argument.** The survey series (Reviewer 2, concern 8), the British transmission study, the quantitative appendix and the supplementary analyses belong in a different article or in an online supplement. Compress the repeated statements of what the scenes do not show; a normative argument does not need to refute causal claims it does not make.
9. **Presentation.** Abstract of 150 to 200 words that states the thesis. Page references for all quotations. Consistent romanisation. The revised article, with notes, should not be longer than the present one, so additions must be paid for by the cuts in point 8.

Please return the revision with a point-by-point response. I will send it to the same reviewers. Reviewer 1 has said that a second version which adds references without adding an argument would be recommended for rejection, and I would follow that advice.

With thanks for sending your work to the journal,

The Editor
