## Article 4 — Believing without Trusting? Christian Identification and Trust in Religious Organizations in Taiwan, 2004–2023

### At a glance

### Research question and thesis

The series model predicts that when a church is seen to fail sufferers, or to side with one camp, faith can persist while trust in the institution falls. Section 16 found this pattern in the United States, Germany and England. It found a version of it in Taiwan's published TSCS tables. The Protestant share was 4.0% in 2009, 5.5% in 2018 and 5.6% in 2023, with Catholics at 1.0–1.5%. Over the same years, "complete or very much" trust in "religious groups and churches" (宗教團體和教會) fell from 21.5% (2009) to 13.3% (2014) and 10.7% (2018). Distrust rose from 16.0% to 26.9% (Fu et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2024). These figures appear unweighted. The trust item covers all religions, and the 2023 wave did not repeat it. The article asks whether the divergence survives proper weighting, whether it is specific to religion, and what predicts it.

The falsifiable claim has four parts:
- **(H1) Divergence.** Weighted trust in religious organizations declines from 2009 to 2018 while weighted Christian identification does not.
- **(H2) Religion-specific decline.** The decline is larger than any decline in trust in the other institutions asked about in the same battery.
- **(H3) Perceived politicization.** Respondents who disapprove of religious leaders speaking publicly on politics show more distrust. The association is strongest in 2018, the year of the referendums on marriage.
- **(H4) Cohort and transmission.** Distrust is higher in later-born cohorts. Among respondents raised in Christian homes, current Christian identification depends on childhood participation in a gathered community, not on parental identification alone. This is the model's "intergenerational transmission requires community" element.

The claim is refuted if any of the following holds:
- Weighted estimates show no significant trust decline, or a matching fall in Christian identification.
- Trust in religious organizations falls no faster than trust in the other institutions in the battery, which would make it a general trust story.
- Disapproval of political speech does not predict distrust net of controls, or its association with distrust does not change across waves.
- Cohort gradients are flat.
- Among those raised Christian, childhood participation does not moderate retention.

The article measures perceptions of religious political involvement; it does not evaluate the positions that churches took. H3 is therefore symmetric. It asks whether objection to religious bodies speaking on politics *as such* predicts distrust. The test does not ask which side respondents favoured.

### Design and evidence

**Case selection.** Taiwan is a strong test because the model's two outcomes can come apart there:
- Christians are a stable minority of about 5.5–7% (Protestant plus Catholic) in a plural religious market, so identification does not move mechanically with public trust.
- The country has a recent episode of visible religious political mobilization from the early 2010s through 2018 (Huang 2017; Ho 2019).
- It also has a historical contrast. The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) took human-rights and disaster-ministry stances (Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1971, 1977, 1990, 1999, 2009). Other Christians formed a party-state-aligned genealogy (Tseng 2017).
- Korea, with a larger Christian share and a sharper trust collapse, serves as a descriptive foil (Christian Ethics Movement of Korea 2026).

**Within-case comparisons.**
- Across waves.
- Across groups: Christians against non-Christians, and religious against non-religious respondents.
- Across birth cohorts.
- Against other institutions in the same trust battery (difference in trends).

**Construct problem.** "Religious groups and churches" is not "churches." Most Taiwanese religious organizations are not Christian, and the prominent disaster responder is Buddhist Tzu Chi (Huang 2009 *[verify 921 coverage]*). The article therefore treats the item as religion-wide institutional trust. Christians' own trust is analysed separately as an insider view. Church-specific wording is used only where the ISSP 2018 questionnaire supplies it *[verify]*.

1. **Introduction (~700 words).** Purpose: state the belief-versus-trust puzzle, the Taiwan figures and the four hypotheses.
   - Cite: Davie 1990; Gallup n.d.-a; fowid 2025a; Fu et al. 2019.
   - Read: Fu et al. 2019, appendix 4, p. 222.
2. **Theory and hypotheses (~1,300).** Purpose: set out believing without belonging as a stage, politicization backlash, scandal spillover, care versus advocacy, and transmission through community. Derive H1–H4 and the symmetric wording of H3.
   - Cite: Voas and Crockett 2005; Voas and Chaves 2016; Hout and Fischer 2002, 2014; Djupe, Neiheisel, and Conger 2018; Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; Hungerman 2013; Frick, Moser, and Simmons 2021; Pew Research Center 2021b; Bentzen 2019, 2021 (the devotion side).

   - Cite: Huang 2017 (Huang Ke-hsien, "'Culture Wars' in a Globalized East: How Taiwanese Conservative Christianity Turned Public in Same-Sex-Marriage Controversy and Secularist Backlashes"); Ho 2019; Tseng 2017; Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 1971, 1977, 1990, 2014; Yi and Su 2021.
   - Read: the PCT documents in full, in Chinese.
4. **Data and measures (~1,100).** Purpose: a wave-by-item availability table, weights, sample sizes, harmonization and the construct problem.
   - Cite: Fu et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2024.
   - Read: the SRDA codebooks and questionnaires for all five waves, the sampling and weighting chapters of each report, and the ISSP 2018 Taiwan documentation.
5. **Methods (~800).** Purpose: survey-weighted estimation; pooled ordered-logit or linear-probability models with wave fixed effects; wave × predictor interactions; decomposition of change into composition (cohort, religion) and within-group change.

6. **Results (~1,900).** Purpose: (a) weighted trends against the published unweighted figures; (b) the religion-specific versus general trust decline; (c) predictors of distrust, H3; (d) cohort patterns; (e) the transmission test among those raised Christian, if parental-religion and childhood-attendance items exist *[verify]*; (f) optional ISSP/WVS placement of Taiwan.
   - Cite: Pew Research Center 2025a, which reports retention by upbringing as a comparator.
7. **Qualitative extension (optional, ~700).** Purpose: interviews on reasons for reduced involvement, chiefly scandal and care, which the survey cannot measure.
   - Cite: Pound and Ryan 2024 *[verify]* as a comparator on "intentional distancing."
   - If word limits bind, move this section to a separate Chinese-language article.
8. **Discussion (~900).** Purpose: compare Taiwan with the US, Germany and Korea. Assess what supports or qualifies the series model, especially Branch B's "legitimacy rather than numbers" and the transmission element.
   - Cite: Christian Ethics Movement of Korea 2026; Frick and Simmons 2017; PRRI 2024; Taylor 2007.
9. **Conclusion (~300).** Purpose: findings, limits and the data needed next, such as reinstating the trust item in the 2028 module.

### Anticipated reviewer objections

| Objection | Response | Supporting citations |
|---|---|---|
| The item measures trust in all religious organizations, not churches. | The article accepts the point and reframes the outcome as religion-wide trust. It analyses Christians' own trust separately, uses church-specific ISSP wording if available, and uses interviews for church-specific reasons. | Fu et al. 2019; Huang 2009 |
| This is just a general fall in institutional trust. | Difference-in-trends against the other institutions in the same battery; H2 is falsifiable. | Gallup n.d.-a |
| The trust series has only three waves and stops in 2018. | The article states this plainly. It extends the series to 2023 with the political-speech items and makes no trust claim after 2018. | Wu et al. 2024 |
| The 2018 referendums did not cause the decline, which began in 2009–2014. | The article agrees and makes no causal claim. Mobilization began in the early 2010s, so the 2009–2014 fall is not evidence against a politicization pathway, but it is not evidence for one either. | Huang 2017; Ho 2019 |
| The Christian subsample (roughly 80–100 Protestants per wave, unweighted) is too small. | Pool waves, report confidence intervals and treat Christian-only results as descriptive. | Fu et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2024 |
| Age, period and cohort are confounded. | Cohort tables, alternative constraints and an explicit statement of the identification limits. | Voas and Crockett 2005; Molteni and Biolcati 2023 |
| The author takes sides on marriage or national identity. | Hypotheses concern perceptions of involvement, not positions. H3 is symmetric. The PCT is shown as not the opposite pole on marriage. Pre-register the hypotheses before analysis. | Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 2014; Huang 2017 |
| Where is "collective suffering"? | The article tests the model's institutional branch and transmission element, where the proximate driver today is institutional conduct and politics, not external suffering. | Section 16; Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; Pew Research Center 2025b |

### Risks and fallbacks

| Risk | Fallback |
|---|---|
| SRDA access is delayed. | Start with the ISSP 2018 file, if Taiwan's TSCS 2018 data are deposited there *[verify]*, and the published reports. |
| The trust item is absent in 2004 or worded differently across waves. | Restrict the trust series to 2009–2018 and use the political-speech series (2014–2023) for later years. |
| There are no care or scandal items. | Drop those predictors from the survey models and address them only in the interviews. State the limit. |
| The Christian subsample is too small. | Pool waves; report Christian-only estimates descriptively; rest the main inference on the full-sample models. |

| Ethics review or recruitment stalls. | Submit the quantitative article alone and move the interviews to a later Chinese-language paper. |
| The topic is politically sensitive. | Use neutral item wording in the text, symmetric hypotheses and pre-registration; describe positions only as respondents perceive them. |
