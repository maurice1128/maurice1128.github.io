This is the fastest article in the series. It needs no archive work, only close reading of published scholarship the research notes have already mapped. It is also the best seminary writing sample, because it shows the author reading theology critically next to history and social science.

### Research question and thesis

The essay asks why scholarship on Christianity under catastrophe gives contradictory verdicts. For the Black Death, a popular commonplace says "faith in God survived, trust in the Church fell." Specialists find mostly the reverse: demand for intercession rose and ran through the church (Cohn 1992; Henderson 1994; Duffy 1992). For slavery, historians find intensified faith alongside rejection of the slaveholders' churches (Raboteau 2004; Gerbner 2018). Economists find disasters raise religiosity (Bentzen 2019), but scandals cut participation without measurably cutting belief (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015). Theologians predict that retributive frames bind the church's credibility to outcomes, while solidarity frames sustain faith through defeat (Brueggemann 1986; Moltmann 1974; Cone 1975).

The thesis is that these verdicts conflict because each literature measures a different construct, usually without saying so. Historians infer "faith" from expressions that also signal institutional dependence, such as bequests for masses. Quantitative work measures aggregate religiosity or participation. Theology predicts both but tests neither. Read together, they show one pattern: devotion rises, and trust is redistributed according to the church's role (own community, failing establishment, or ally of perpetrators). The essay ends by stating this as a conditional model and a research agenda, not a proven result.

The claim is falsifiable at two levels. At the level of the literature, it is refuted if a body of work on *collective suffering* already measures devotion and institutional trust separately across cases. The scandal literature comes close (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; Frick, Moser and Simmons 2021), but it studies misconduct, not catastrophe, and the essay must say so. At the level of the pattern, it is refuted if the coded cases show institutional trust tracking the severity of suffering rather than the church's role. It is also refuted if devotion falls where the church was the sufferers' own (for example, the early church in Stark 1996, or the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan).

### Design and evidence

**Genre and selection.** This is a state-of-the-field essay, not a systematic review, but it uses a stated protocol. The corpus is the works already assembled in sections 3–5, 7–11 and 16 of the literature review. They are grouped into three literatures: historical (Black Death, Atlantic slavery, global and Sinophone cases), social-scientific (disaster religiosity, historical political economy, scandal and disaffiliation studies) and theological (theodicy, contextual and anti-theodicy). Inclusion requires that a work make a claim about religious response to *collective* suffering. Works on individual illness or bereavement are excluded.

**Coding.** Each work is coded on four fields: what it treats as "faith"; what it treats as "trust"; which institution it means (establishment, sufferer-controlled body, or "the church" undivided); and whether it observes, infers or predicts the outcome. The full coding goes in a supplementary table if the venue allows one *[verify]*.

**How sources measure the two constructs.** The essay states that neither construct is directly observable in historical sources (section 11.7). It then shows each literature's proxies:

| Literature | Devotion proxy | Institutional-trust proxy | Typical conflation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black Death history | Bequests, confraternities, cults (Cohn 1992; Henderson 1994) | Clerical supply, complaint, suppression of lay movements (Robinson 2019; Kieckhefer 1974) | A bequest for masses is both devotion and dependence |
| Slave-religion history | Conversion narratives, spirituals, hush harbors (Raboteau 2004; Fountain 2010) | Separations, exclusion from biracial churches (Boles 2020; Montgomery 1993) | Christianization rates read as faith, though masters controlled access (Gerbner 2018) |
| Quantitative social science | Survey religiosity; prayer searches (Bentzen 2019, 2021) | Participation, giving, exits (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; Frick and Simmons 2017) | "Religiosity" aggregates both |
| Contemporary surveys | Belief in God (Pew Research Center 2024) | Confidence in organized religion (Gallup n.d.-a) | Usually separated, but not tied to collective suffering |
| Theology | Predicted from the theological frame | Predicted from the church's role | Normative claims, untested |

**Primary texts.** The essay is not archival. It quotes only a few short passages that show the split, such as Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate of 10 January 1349 (full Latin in Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46; Holmes 1896 only calendars it as no. 2130), li Muisis's report that the Tournai crowd valued the flagellants' rite "plus quam divinum officium" (Lemaître 1906, 239), and Douglass's 1845 appendix.

### Section-by-section outline

Total about 8,000 words, excluding bibliography.

1. **Introduction: three verdicts on one question (~650).** Opens with the Black Death commonplace and the specialists' correction. States the thesis and the scope (Christianity; collective, not individual, suffering). Sources: Stearns 2009; Cohn 1992; Duffy 1992; Douglass 1845. *Read:* Douglass 1845 appendix.
2. **Two constructs, many proxies (~700).** Defines devotion and institutional trust. Argues that trust must be measured per institution and that "redistribution" differs from "exit." Includes the proxy table above. Sources: Arnold 2005; Gorski 2000; Davie 1994; section 11.7. *Read:* Arnold 2005, introduction and conclusion.
3. **The historians' pattern (~1,900).**
   - 3a, Black Death (~800): delayed devotional intensification, with the 1363 turning point (Cohn 1992 *[verify thesis; fact-check found no confirmation]*). Testamentary piety (Chiffoleau 1980). Absorption or suppression of lay penance: the Bianchi (Bornstein 1993) versus the flagellants (Kieckhefer 1974; Graus 1987). Clerical replacement (Robinson 2019, in *Archives* 54/2). The Meiss debate as a warning that expression is not feeling (Steinhoff 2006). Delumeau's clerically shaped "pastoral of fear" (Delumeau [1983] 1990). Corrected wording: code the plague as "faith up, trust redistributed," not "trust down." Note the deliverance rival in li Muisis's "Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio" (Lemaître 1906, 258): some devotional peaks track the acute threat.
   - 3b, Atlantic slavery (~750): the invisible institution (Raboteau 2004); masters against baptism (Gerbner 2018); exclusion preceding separation (Boles 2020); late conversion (Fountain 2010: fewer than 40% of datable conversions before freedom, a contested finding). Brazil's irmandades separate church role from power position (Russell-Wood 1974; Reis 2003).
   - 3c, beyond the two classic cases (~350): Rwanda (Longman 2010); the Korean shrine-worship schism (Yi Chin-gu 2025); Taiwan (Tseng 2017; Madsen 2012).
   - What historians see: church role and timing. What they miss: a common measure, and comparison across cases.
   - *Read:* the Ralph of Shrewsbury mandate in Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46 (Holmes 1896, no. 2130, only summarises it), or Horrox 1994.
4. **The social scientists' pattern (~1,600).** Covers four strands. (a) Disaster religiosity (Bentzen 2019, 2021; Ager and Ciccone 2018). (b) Historical political economy: Dittmar and Meisenzahl 2020 (each extra outbreak raised the probability of a Protestant ordinance by about 11–14 percentage points, 14 in the baseline); Belloc, Drago and Galbiati 2016, worded as "earthquakes delayed communal self-government only where bishops held political power, which the authors attribute to enhanced religious-political authority"; Jedwab, Johnson and Koyama 2019, noting that persecution rose in aggregate even though higher-mortality cities persecuted less, and that the liturgical-calendar result in the published article (pp. 347, 382–83) has an agricultural-calendar alternative; Chaney 2013. (c) Scandals and exit (Bottan and Perez-Truglia 2015; Hungerman 2013; Frick, Moser and Simmons 2021; Frick and Simmons 2017 is a short time-series letter, not a quasi-experiment). (d) Believing without belonging and partisan sorting (Davie 1994; Voas and Crockett 2005; Voas and Chaves 2016; Hout and Fischer 2002, 2014). They see conditionality and, in the scandal work, a measurable split. They miss theology and structural oppression as a "shock." *Read:* the lead articles, tables included.
5. **The theologians' pattern (~1,400).** Covers retributive versus solidarity frames, and who controls the remedy each prescribes (Brueggemann 1986; Moltmann 1974; Sölle 1975; Cone 1975, 2011; Sobrino 2008; Kitamori 1965; Park 1993). It then takes anti-theodicy as a prediction about institutions (Surin 1986; Tilley 1991; Swinton 2007), and the loss-of-faith branch (Jones [1973] 1998; Pinn 1995; Williams 1993). The Taiwanese homeland and *chhut-thau-thiⁿ* theologies follow, with Chiu's (2021) critique of their narrowing. What theologians see: interpretation and its institutional stakes. What they miss: tests of their own predictions, and the fact that theology is partly a clerical product (Delumeau). *Read:* Cyprian, *De mortalitate*; Luther 1968 (the 1527 letter); PCT 1977.
6. **Convergence: one pattern, three partial views (~800).** The synthesis table (literature · unit · faith measure · trust measure · what it sees · what it misses). It argues three points. Devotion rises in nearly every literature. Trust follows the church's role and its capacity to deliver care. "Erosion" is usually redistribution to sufferer-controlled bodies, often inside the church (confraternities, irmandades, hush harbors).
7. **An agenda: a conditional model (~700).** States the five conditions: power position; theology and who controls its remedy; church role and capacity to care; duration and recurrence; and intergenerational transmission through community. States the three outcomes and three rival explanations to test: deliverance (Fountain 2010), provision, or "flour Christians" (Madsen 2012), and political alignment (Hout and Fischer 2014; Djupe, Neiheisel and Conger 2018). Proposes designs: paired indicators, nested comparisons, Brazil as a variable-separating case, and Taiwan's churches as a near-natural experiment (sections 7, 11.10).
8. **Conclusion (~250).** Restates the claim about the literatures, not about history itself.
