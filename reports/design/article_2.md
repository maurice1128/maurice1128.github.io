### Research question and thesis

The working model holds that suffering raises demand for meaning, intercession and community. This article asks what decides *where* that demand goes. The cases share a scriptural repertoire, which *histoire croisée* lets us treat as a common variable (Werner and Zimmermann 2006), but they end differently. After 1348 most Christians channelled more devotion through the church: chapels, perpetual masses, saints and confraternities (Cohn 1992; Henderson 1994; Chiffoleau 1980). After 1865 freedpeople left white-controlled churches in large numbers (Dvorak 1991; Montgomery 1993; Stowell 1998).

The thesis has three parts. (1) A punishment theology strengthens the institution that controls the penitential remedy. It erodes trust only where that institution visibly fails to supply the remedy: the absent deathbed priest (Ralph of Shrewsbury's mandate of 10 January 1349; Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46, calendared in Holmes 1896 as no. 2130), or the suppressed lay penitents (Kieckhefer 1974). (2) Where the establishment is allied with the perpetrators, devotion moves to sufferer-controlled bodies whenever these can exist: the hush harbour, the Black-led congregation, the AME (Raboteau [1978] 2004; Boles 2020; Newman 2008). (3) Formal affiliation peaks when sufferers *gain control* of an institution, not when suffering peaks. This reframes Fountain's (2010) finding as evidence for control rather than for deliverance as such. The primary texts read so far support a postwar move of people already Christian into Black-run churches more securely than a postwar first conversion. The WPA narratives rarely date conversion (44 of 51 coded narratives give no date), so they cannot settle Fountain's quantitative claim.

**Three routes of lay control.** On the plague side, remedies passed to lay hands in three ways, which the article codes separately. (a) *Delegated and reclaimed:* Ralph let the dying confess "etiam laico, et si desit vir laicus, mulieri," but survivors had to confess again to their own priest (Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46). (b) *Seized and suppressed:* at Tournai the flagellant companies carried and buried the dead and took the bequests that went with the task ("societates penitentium cum cruce et cereis corpora levant, portant et sepeliunt"; Lemaître 1906, 240) until crown, town governors and papal bull stopped them. (c) *Taken over by town authorities:* with the see vacant, Tournai's governors issued burial and bell-ringing ordinances because the dean, chapter and clergy were profiting from the deaths (Lemaître 1906, 255). Route (a) left the institution in control; (b) and (c) are where trust in the clergy visibly broke.

**Falsification.** The claim fails if any of the following holds. (a) Devotion shifts to lay-controlled bodies *before* any documented establishment failure or complicity (a hoop test). (b) After 1865, growth in Christian affiliation is spread evenly across white-led bodies (MECS, northern MEC, later the CME) and Black-led ones (AME, Black Baptist), which would favour deliverance. (c) In Brazil, where the enslaved were powerless but held their own brotherhoods, devotion nonetheless left the church, which would make power position, not church role, decisive. (d) Cohn's post-1363 shift appears evenly across places regardless of local clerical performance, which would favour recurrence alone. (e) Muslim societies, which had no sacramental priesthood, produced flagellant-style lay penitential movements against religious authorities. That would show sacramental monopoly is not the mechanism.

### Design and evidence

**Case logic.** The cross-case pair is most-different: era, continent, economy, denomination, and natural versus moral evil (Seawright and Gerring 2008). Since a most-different pair with a shared outcome invites Lieberson's (1991) critique and selection on the dependent variable (Geddes 1990), causal weight rests on four within-case contrasts that hold era and region constant (Mahoney 2012; Beach and Pedersen 2019).

1. **Christians versus Jews, 1348–51.** Same shock. For Jews the Christian establishment was persecutor or unreliable protector (Cohn 2007; Barzilay 2022; Finley and Koyama 2018). The model predicts consolidation inside Jewish communal institutions (Einbinder 2018; Foa 2000). Jewish position is coded directly, not as race (Heng 2018).
2. **Lay confraternity versus parish, and absorbed versus condemned penance.** Bequest destinations and post-1349 guild foundations; Italian *disciplinati* and the 1399 Bianchi, which clergy and communes absorbed (Meersseman 1977; Bornstein 1993 *[verify argument]*), against the German flagellants of 1349, whom the papacy condemned (Kieckhefer 1974; Graus 1987).
3. **Black-led versus white-led or biracial congregations.** Membership, discipline and exits in shared churches (Irons 2008; Sparks 1994; Sensbach 1998; Boles 2020) against Black-led congregations and the AME.
4. **Before versus after emancipation.** Phase-indexed: before 1760, 1760–1830, 1830–65, 1865–77 (Frey and Wood 1998; Fountain 2010; Kreuzer 2023). The plague side is phased as 1347–52, 1361–63 and the later recurrences to about 1400.

**Shadow cases.** *Brazil:* the established church was a slaveholder (Schwartz 1982), yet the enslaved and freed held chartered irmandades (Russell-Wood 1974; Soares [2000] 2011; Kiddy 2005; Reginaldo 2011). The prediction is devotion and trust in their *own* church bodies rising together; the 1836 Cemiterada fits (Reis [1991] 2003). The rejection branch appears where alternatives existed (Reis [1986] 1993; Sweet 2003). *Muslim plague responses:* the same shock, a theology of martyrdom and mercy, and no sacramental priesthood monopolising the remedy (Dols 1977; Ibn al-Wardī, trans. Dols 1974; Ibn Ḥajar 1993; Stearns 2011). Authority was contested among jurist, physician and ruler (Būjarra 2011), not bypassed by lay penitents. That there was no flagellant-style movement is *[verify against Dols 1974; 1977]*.

The plague sources already show the deliverance and recurrence rivals at work. Li Muisis wrote of the St Sebastian pilgrimages that "Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio" (Lemaître 1906, 258): devotion tracked the acute threat. Short-term peaks therefore cannot count for control; only the destination of devotion across waves can.

- The two-track pattern recurs in Black-authored texts: faith in God kept, institutions "in union with slaveholders" rejected (Douglass 1845, Appendix; Walker 1830, 40; Allen [1833] 1880, 16–17). The dominant side states its control of the remedy outright (C. C. Jones 1842, 158).
