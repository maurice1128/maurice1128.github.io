Mu-Hua (Maurice) Wang

I work on physical assistive robotics for long-term care: robots that carry out contact-rich care tasks on a bedridden human body, starting with diaper changing. That task demands three things at once — perceiving a body that will not cooperate, knowing enough about that person to move them without harm, and handling deformable material safely against skin.

My work so far covers the first two and has begun the third. I built and clinically validated an RGB multi-view markerless motion capture system at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, and took it into hospitals and day-care centres. I have studied, in simulation, what a robot must know about a person before it moves their limb, and how it should read a force sensor at the moment of first contact.

Since September 2026 I have been a student research assistant in Prof. Yi-Ting Chen’s lab at NYCU, working on robot cup-feeding for people with dysphagia. With Prof. Jing-Chen Hong I am designing piezoelectric contact sensing. I hold a B.S. in Biomedical Engineering from NYCU and an M.S. place there on approved leave.

Note on how the projects below are written. Each carries a Scope line stating where the result stops. Every number traces to a stored run artifact, and non-significant cells are reported as not detected at that power rather than as absent.

— Care robots —

Gentle by knowing: person knowledge protects restricted joints better than force thresholds in simulated robotic arm lifting
Mu-Hua Wang
Under review at Frontiers in Robotics and AI (Brief Research Report), manuscript 2019057 · peer review completed, both reviewers endorsed publication

A simulated robot lifts the forearm of persons with restricted shoulders or elbows (MuJoCo/MyoSuite). When it supports the limb from outside, the loaded joint is not on the axis of its force sensor: the tightest force-magnitude guard lowered mean joint load by 29–36 % but moved harmful outcomes by only 0–7 points. With a complete care record, a policy learned offline reduced harmful outcomes from 110 to 25 of 300 new simulated persons, mainly by declining 91 of them, with 174 vs. 177 safe completions. The reduction replicated on 300 further persons (−0.27 [−0.32, −0.21]).

Scope: simulation only, one body structure shared by training and test; safe-completion non-inferiority not established; the reduction shrank by almost half with half of the restrictions unrecorded; harm is a mechanical-exposure proxy, not injury.

Do Not Stop at First Contact: Deliberate Overshoot Makes a Care Robot's Pain Risk Robust to Its Noise Model
Mu-Hua Wang
Submitted to IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters (manuscript 26-6751), October 2026

A care robot that stops a limb at the first sensed resistance keeps one force reading, at the sensor's detection floor, so its stated pain risk depends on a noise level it assumes but cannot check. Pushing 2.4° past first contact takes readings above the floor that measure both the limb and the noise. On a MuJoCo leg, as the assumed noise ranges from the true noise to seven times it, realised pain at a stated 5 % stays at 5.0–6.3 % with the overshoot and rises from 6.7 to 17.1 % when the robot stops at contact. Predictions were pre-registered.

Scope: simulation only, 200 simulated persons per experiment; the overshoot hurts more persons while probing (2.5–7 % against 0.5–3.5 %) but fewer overall, and the between-person spread of pain thresholds must come from human studies.

Test the Swallow Before the Robot Pours: A Virtual Patient, Phantom Throat and Rim-Holding Cup Controller for Robot Cup-Feeding in Dysphagia
Mu-Hua Wang · advisor: Yi-Ting Chen
Platform in progress, no manuscript · 2026

A GPU particle model swallows 5 mL of water through a throat taken from a real CT scan. Its first version left 2.13 mL in the throat; after the motion errors were fixed, 0.12 mL remains, inside the healthy reference of 0.4 mL, while 0.0125 mL still reaches the airway — 87 of those 88 particles turned out to be numerical artefacts. A cup controller that follows the head and holds the liquid at the rim cut liquid sway at the lip from 2.04 to 0.87 mm with a slosh-frequency notch. A phantom throat is designed from the same lumen.

Scope: simulation and design only: no robot trial, no participant, no printed part; one CT anatomy; the zero-aspiration healthy test still fails.

Can Two Robot Hands Put an Adult Diaper on a Bedridden Body Without Pushing Harder Than 40 N?
Mu-Hua Wang
Work in progress, 2026 · no manuscript

A contact-accurate simulation in which a deformable older-adult body, a mattress, a tape-type adult brief and two force-gated hands can never pass through each other. In one reference run the hands fasten all four tabs at a peak force of 28.5 N, and a second run reproduces all 7 stages bit for bit; two fit measures still fail. The failure that matters most is visible in slow motion: the soiled brief, squeezed under the body, springs back 123 mm when the hand lets go. The page lets you scrub through the run with every hand force in sync.

Scope: simulation only; one body and one brief; the valid result is one run; not yet validated against a physical diaper or a person.

Eleven Piezo Channels Inside a Table-Tennis Handle: an In-Racket Sensing Board
Mu-Hua Wang · advisor: Jing-Chen Hong
Hardware design, not yet fabricated, 2026

A 6-layer, 18 × 75 mm board designed to sit inside a racket handle and record 11 piezoelectric film channels, with two synchronised ADCs, an IMU, Wi-Fi and charging (235 parts). Before ordering, a board-level simulation found a reference error 50× the ADC noise floor, which was fixed; an independent checker that rebuilds connectivity from the factory files found 0 opens and 0 shorts and caught 8 of 8 deliberately damaged copies. The page lets you look through the six copper layers and click the blade to see which film strips would answer.

Scope: design, simulation and file checks only; no board built, no hit recorded; charging with Wi-Fi on overheats (about 80–100 °C, simulated) and 32 kS/s per channel needs a driver not yet written.

— Motion capture —

A markerless motion capture system built and taken into clinics, and what its error costs the decisions made from it.

RGB Multi-View 3D Markerless Motion Capture (NTKCAP)
NYCU · research assistant Oct 2023 – Sep 2024, team lead Oct 2024 – Sep 2025 · advisor Prof. Hui-Ting Shih

Real-time whole-body pose sensing for clinical use — the perception layer a robot working over a bed has to have. I wrote the pipeline, ran the validation experiments, and collected the clinical data myself.

  • GPU acceleration and CPU parallelism across capture, inference, triangulation and inverse kinematics; DLT triangulation with occlusion handling; and ROWV, an outlier-rejection algorithm for small-sample settings, faster and more accurate than KNN and RANSAC on this problem.
  • VICON comparison over 21 participants, plus 104 walking trials from 11 participants on a nine-camera ring.
  • Shipped the product, not only the algorithm: clinician-facing PyQt6 GUI, SQL and cloud pipeline with ICD-10 coding, Unreal Engine rehabilitation game.
  • Field deployment: 2 signed hospital MOUs (Taipei Veterans General; Taipei City Hospital), 200+ stroke and knee-pain patients in Vietnam, 40+ stroke patients with exoskeleton at Kaohsiung Chang Gung, 20+ elderly at a Kaohsiung daycare centre, amputee feedback trials with NYCU Physical Therapy. Led a 5-person team; 4 conference presentations, 6 media features.

How Much Joint-Angle Error Can the Identification of a Weak Muscle Group Tolerate? A Predictive-Simulation Study
Mu-Hua Wang, Hui-Ting Shih
Submitted to Journal of Biomechanics, November 2026

In forward-dynamic gait simulation, one sagittal muscle group at a time was weakened by 20, 40 or 60 % and joint-angle error of 0–12° was added before identifying which group was weak. Rank-1 identification fell from 0.845 at 0° to 0.549 at 12°, above twice the 0.227 baseline. Mild weakness was identified at only 0.610 even at 2°. A gain from adding a force plate was not detected at 0–2°; at 8° it was +0.159.

Scope: simulation only, one planar model in five strength-rescaled variants, 66 conditions; the severity difference and the force-plate gain at 0–2° are inconclusive at this power.

Online Fusion of Model-Based and Learned Prediction for Humanoid Walking Policies over Delayed Links, Without Retraining
Mu-Hua Wang
Submitted to IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters (26-7088), November 2026

A walking policy that runs on a remote computer steers a body it sees late, and the humanoid falls: pretrained policies start to fall at round trips of 41–46 ms. With the policy frozen, a physics-model predictor and learned forward models predict the present state, and each is weighted by errors checked one delay later. In simulation on three humanoids the round trip tolerated at 90% survival rises to 147–180 ms. Command plans of 200 ms in each packet raise mean survival on a recorded Wi-Fi link from 48–80% to 93–100%.

Scope: simulation only, flat ground, three pretrained policies from one public repository, no hardware; on G1 a retrained student holds 90% survival to 170 ms against 147 ms, and a body the predictor was not fitted on costs 58–97 ms.

Ataxia-Like Foot Placement in Learned Humanoids: Sensor Noise and Gait Clocks as Causes
Mu-Hua Wang
Manuscript prepared for IEEE RA-L (not yet submitted), 2026

Scored Unitree's learned walking policies for G1, H1 and H1-2 with the clinical gait measures used for cerebellar ataxia, normalised for body size. At their training sensor noise they scatter their feet sideways 2.1–2.9× as much as healthy young adults (in leg lengths); with clean sensors they are more regular than people. Self-distillation on noisy readings (DAgger) cuts G1's lateral step deviation from 3.91× to 1.05–1.20×, and a gait clock fitted to people leaves no measure above the healthy range on four of five retrained G1 seeds.

Scope: simulation only (MuJoCo, Isaac Gym); the noise is the training noise, not a measured real-robot noise; retraining on G1 only; a fitted state observer did not help, and two registered predictions fell outside their intervals.

— Learning motor skills —

Learned In-Betweeners Make Simulated Dexterous Hands Learn Faster
Mu-Hua Wang
Submitted to IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters (manuscript 26-6874)

Both layers of a modular motion model tested on simulated dexterous hands, one change at a time, with the main comparisons registered before the runs. As the action space of a PPO policy, keyframes joined by a learned in-betweener lead a continuous latent at the same decision rate on held-out skill sequences (0.59 against 0.22 at 1.5 M frames, 17 of 20 seeds) and reach the training plateau sooner; at 5 M frames the data do not separate them. At the skill level, choosing the best policy for each stage completes 97 of 400 test episodes against 61 for the best single policy; separately trained skills complete 45.

Scope: simulation only, one task per layer; the early lead is significant on the cube only; the selection pipeline ran once; the registered prediction that separately trained skills win failed.

Withdrawn Teachers and Learned Body Models in Muscle-Driven Motor Learning
Mu-Hua Wang
Under review at IEEE Transactions on Cognitive and Developmental Systems (TCDS-2026-0625)

One soft actor-critic learner on six muscle-driven bodies, tested with controls that change one thing at a time. On a muscle-driven finger, a learner whose competent teacher is withdrawn ends 43.25 mm ahead of one never guided, and the share of the teacher's benefit it keeps rises from 24% to 101% with the time it was taught. On planar arms, a random body model becomes increasingly harmful as joints are added (trained minus random: −357.20 mm on four joints), and the trained model avoids that harm.

Scope: simulation only, single reach task, one teacher and one random model per body; on the elbow no teacher benefit survives withdrawal, and the trained model's advantage over matched model-free learning is not shown to grow with joints.

Labels that cover only part of a recording can hide compositional difficulty in hand-motion data
Mu-Hua Wang
Under review at Machine Learning (Springer), submitted November 2026

Held-out-combination tests ask whether a motion model handles action–object combinations it never saw. On recorded motion a correct label may describe only part of its recording, so holding the label out leaves the motion in training. Joining OakInk-Image clips and changing only label coverage moves the penalty from +17.03 % (full) to +3.87 % (half), with every estimate calibrated against a no-effect control over 40 random splits. On TACO the drop repeats with 256 recordings per model (+11.14 % → +3.69 %).

Scope: offline datasets and one model class, no robot experiment; on TACO the first test with 64 recordings per model was not significant (p = 0.095), and under the Nadeau–Bengio correction no full-versus-half comparison is significant.
