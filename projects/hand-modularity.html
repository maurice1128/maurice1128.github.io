Modularity Makes Dexterous Hands Learn Faster, Not Better

Modular motion models promise a hand that learns faster and reaches further. Both promises were registered before the runs. Only the first survives.

Mu-Hua Wang · National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University

[Paper (PDF)] [All projects]

Not posted to any preprint server. The hosted PDF is the anonymised submission build, so it carries no author block.

Abstract

Modular motion models such as MotionBricks build motion from reusable parts, and the idea is attractive for dexterous hands: a hand that learns in modules should learn faster and reach further. We test both promises, with every prediction registered before the runs.

Used as the action space of a dexterous policy, a MotionBricks-style keyframe interface learns held-out skill sequences far better early in training (0.59 against 0.22 success) but a continuous latent catches up by the end (0.49 against 0.44). Separately learned skills do no better than one flat policy trained on the same demonstrations (45 against 61 of 400 test episodes), and a hand-off defined too loosely let one skill fling the ball out of the next one's reach. The one gain, choosing the best policy for each stage (97 against 61), needs no separate training.

Modularity makes dexterous hands learn faster, not better, and four conditions explain when it would pay. To choose which motion data to test on, we also built an instrument for whether hand-motion priors generalize to unseen combinations: over nine axes of three datasets, difficulty appears only where the two factors interact, a statistic computed on raw poses in minutes.

What went wrong at the seam

A hand-off defined too loosely let one skill fling the ball out of the next one's reach. The failure is at the join between modules, which is exactly the place a modular design is supposed to buy you something.

Choosing what to test on

Picking the dataset is not neutral: a benchmark whose labels carry no compositional difficulty cannot answer the question. So the paper also builds an instrument for it — over nine axes of three datasets, difficulty appears only where the two factors interact, from a statistic computed on raw poses in minutes, before anything is trained.

That instrument, and what it says about how a hand-motion dataset has to be labelled before it can test compositional generalisation at all, is developed in a separate paper.

Scope

  • One prior architecture family (MotionBricks-style keyframes) against one continuous latent, on one hand.
  • The advantage that survives — per-stage policy selection — is not an argument for modular training, since it needs none.
  • This page states the paper's abstract-level results only. The full conditions under which modularity would pay are in the manuscript.
