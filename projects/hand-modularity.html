Dexterous manipulation · simulation · registered comparisons · submitted to IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters (manuscript 26-6874)

Learned in-betweeners make simulated dexterous hands learn faster

Modular motion models such as MotionBricks build motion from reusable parts, but earlier work changed the prior, the decision rate, the data and the training procedure at once. We tested each of MotionBricks’ two layers on simulated dexterous hands, changing one thing at a time and registering the main comparisons before running them. Keyframes joined by a learned in-betweener lead a continuous latent that decides at the same rate (0.59 against 0.22 on held-out skill sequences at 1.5 M frames) and reach the training plateau sooner; at 5 M frames the data do not separate the two. At the skill level, choosing the best policy for each stage completes more episodes (97 against 61 of 400); training the skills separately does not (45).

Mu-Hua Wang · National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University · sole author

A learned in-betweener learns faster, and the lead is early

Each hand-motion prior became the action space of a PPO policy on a simulated Shadow Hand holding a 5 cm, 0.20 kg cube under a 1.5 g lateral acceleration, every prior deciding once every 16 frames. Policies trained on two-skill sequences and were scored on three-skill sequences they never saw. At 1.5 M frames the keyframe interface succeeded on 0.59 of held-out sequences against the continuous latent’s 0.22 (20 seeds; difference +0.38, 95 % CI 0.23 to 0.52; higher in 17 of 20 seeds; registered). Over the first 2.5 M frames its mean training success was 0.39, against 0.25 for plain PPO and 0.27 for the latent (10 seeds each; higher in every seed pair, exact two-sided Mann–Whitney p = 1.1 × 10−5 for both, post hoc). Because the latent also decides once every 16 frames, the difference is not the decision rate.

The gain belongs to the learned in-betweener, and a loose success rule lets a skill cheat

Motion level · poses joined by a learned in-betweener

Faster

The same target poses joined by straight lines never learned to release (0 of 10 seeds), so on this task the learned in-betweener, not the keyframes alone, carries the gain. Not every modular action space helps: a bank of 16 fixed primitives is the weakest learned prior (0.08).

Skill level · units that act, chained

Selection, not separate training

Adroit relocate, extended into grasp the ball, then carry it to a target in the air once or twice, with behaviour cloning from the 25 human demonstrations of D4RL; PPO from scratch never completed a carry. A stage counts as done only after its goal has held for 25 consecutive steps: with a one-step criterion, a carry skill learned to fling the ball through the target (video).

Selecting the best policy for each stage helps; training skills separately does not

A registered test on fresh seeds, with 20 trainings per side and 400 test episodes never used for any choice. A is the best of 20 flat policies, used for every stage. B uses the same 20 flat policies, the best grasper for the grasp and the best carrier for every carry. C trains 10 grasp skills and 10 carry skills separately and chains the best of each. A against B changes only the selection; B against C changes only the training.

Episodes that completed every stage, of the 400 test episodes. Fisher’s exact test against A: B, p = 0.002 (two stages) and p < 10−5 (three stages); C, p = 0.12 and p = 0.23. The selection pipeline ran once.

Our registered prediction, that separately trained skills beat the best flat policy, failed (45 against 61, p = 0.12), and separate training lost to per-stage selection (45 against 97, p < 10−5). Per stage, C lost at the grasp (0.57 against A’s 0.77). B won through the carry: its carrier completed 0.34 of carries after a grasp, against A’s 0.20.

In this run, the gain comes from per-stage selection, not from training the skills separately.

When separately trained modules would pay: four hypotheses, and neither task meets the last

A chain of modules succeeds with probability ∏ P(module i | end states of module i−1), so the cost of separate modules sits at the hand-offs. We formulated four conditions during the study and offer them as hypotheses, not results. MotionBricks, in animation, plausibly meets all four.

Where this stops

Scope: everything is in simulation, with one task per layer. The early lead is significant on the cube only (three other objects, p = 0.067), and at 5 M frames the data do not separate the keyframe interface from the continuous latent. All interfaces share one set of PPO settings. The skill-level selection pipeline ran once, with behaviour cloning from 25 demonstrations, and the registered prediction that separately trained skills beat the best flat policy failed. The comparison with plain PPO changes the decision rate as well as the prior. Stronger baselines (learned skill priors, action chunking adapted to RL, terminal-state regularization), interface-specific tuning and repeated selection pipelines are the next steps.
