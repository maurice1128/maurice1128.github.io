How much of an observed methadone dose patients drink signals treatment interruption and morphine-positive tests

Where every methadone dose is drunk at the window under observation, the prescription is only a ceiling, and how much of it a patient drinks is a daily record of the patient's own behaviour. In 9,695 randomly scheduled urine tests from 552 patients, drinking less of the prescription preceded interruption of treatment, and a rise in the amount drunk before a test accompanied a morphine-positive result. These are associations in one programme, not causes and not a prediction tool.

Mu-Hua Wang · National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University · first author, with Tso-Jeng Wang (corresponding author)

Patients who drink less of their prescription interrupt treatment more often

The programme records the prescribed dose and the dose actually drunk on every day of attendance; the prescription let patients drink as little as 1 mg in 99.7% of daily records, so the amount drunk is the patient's own choice. Treatment interruption is an absence of at least 30 days beginning 8–90 days after a test: 251 of 8,990 tests with complete follow-up (2.8%) were followed by one, in 144 distinct absences, and 55.6% of these absences ended with a return to treatment.

The interruption association holds when each absence is counted once (1.30), for interruptions that begin 91–180 days after the test (1.23), and, weaker but present, after excluding tests in which 20 mg or less was drunk that week (1.15). Adding the share drunk to attendance and test history raised the cross-validated AUC for interruption from 0.622 to 0.726.

For the concurrent urine result, a fall only had lower odds of a positive test than stable intake (0.52, 95% CI 0.43–0.63). The rise before the test held when the direction was read only from the week before the test (1.86) and when it was read on the share drunk, which removes prescription changes (1.62); heterogeneity across years was not detected (P = 0.36).

A record the programme already keeps carries both signals, without hand-set boundaries

The daily amount is noisy and its size depends on the prescription, so the record is read in two ways that do not. The share drunk is the dose drunk divided by the dose prescribed, averaged over the attended days of the week before the test (the dosage gap of the study protocol). The direction comes from the signs of the highest three-day slopes of the dose drunk before and after the test: stable, a rise before the test, a rise only after it, or a fall only. This rule summarises a 17-group classification I built in MATLAB for this programme and agrees with it for 99.4% of classified tests, without the classification's hand-set boundaries.

The prescription is a ceiling; what a patient drinks below it is behaviour. Drinking less preceded interruption, and a rise before a test accompanied a positive result, so a negative urine test in a patient who drinks little is not a sign that treatment is going well.

Where this stops

Scope: one methadone programme in Taiwan, 2018–2022, and only patients on its list of those retained for at least two years, a selection related to the outcome; observational and exploratory, not pre-registered; associations, not causes.

  • Part of the interruption signal is a taper towards zero: excluding weeks with 20 mg or less drunk weakens the odds ratio from 1.27 to 1.15.
  • A fall only goes with fewer positive tests mainly through the days after the test: read only from the week before, its odds ratio is 0.79 (0.66–0.95).
  • A rise only after the test follows the result rather than preceding it: read on the share drunk, its odds ratio is 1.01 (0.85–1.20), consistent with prescribers raising the dose after a positive test.
  • The association of a fall only with interruption is inconclusive within patients, 1.58 (1.00–2.51), and the share drunk was associated with a positive test in some years only (heterogeneity P = 0.002).
  • The records do not say why a dose was not drunk in full; the morphine assay also detects codeine and prescribed morphine; age and sex were not available for the pooled models.
