Gentle by knowing: person knowledge protects restricted joints better than force thresholds in simulated robotic arm lifting

A robot cannot feel its way to gentleness. When it supports a multi-joint limb from outside, the joint it loads is not on the axis of its force sensor. In a musculoskeletal simulation of a robot lifting the forearm of persons with restricted shoulders or elbows, the tightest force-magnitude guard lowered mean joint load by 29–36 % but moved harmful outcomes by only 0–7 points. With a complete care record, a policy learned offline reduced harmful outcomes from 110 to 25 of 300 new simulated persons, mainly by declining 91 of them. Safe completions were 174 vs. 177, and non-inferiority was not established. Everything is simulated, with one body structure.

Knowing which joint is restricted, and declining, protected joints that force guards could not

Many care recipients cannot move a shoulder or elbow freely, and a robot lifting their forearm can press a restricted joint into the end of its range. Contact-force limits do not sense that joint. A harmful outcome here is a robot-caused end-range joint load of at least 0.5 N·m, summed over shoulder elevation and elbow flexion: a mechanical-exposure proxy, not injury. A safe completion is a completed lift below it. Controllers ranged from a fixed rule that knows nothing, through a restriction flag and joint ranges with 20° error, to an oracle that knows every outcome, and all but the fixed rule and the force guards could decline.

  • Force-magnitude guards lowered the load but did not keep it under 0.5 N·m. The tightest guard left 36–48 % of persons above the threshold and lost 38–51 % of safe completions.
  • Most of the benefit came from deciding whom not to lift (91 of 300 declined), but, post hoc, the learned policy also harmed fewer of the persons it did lift: 53 vs. 95 of 423.
  • Which joint, not how many degrees (post hoc). With one learner at every level of knowledge, per-joint flags gave almost all of the benefit.
  • Gaps in the record stay unprotected. Persons whose restriction was not recorded were harmed exactly as often as under the fixed rule (50 vs. 50 of 85).

Force guards trip late because the joint load hides under the limb's weight

Over 600 persons and eight support sites (post hoc), 0.5 N·m corresponds to 0.8–4.8 N at the contact with the full lever arm; along the lift direction it is 1.6–6.8 N at the elbow and a median of 8 N at the shoulder (5th to 95th percentile 5–109 N). Most of these are small against a forearm-and-hand weight of about 17 N and guard thresholds of 25–50 N. Each guard prevented only 0–13 % of the fixed rule's harmful outcomes, and every harmful lift of the fixed rule stayed below the ISO/TS 15066 quasi-static force limit for the lower arm (160 N). Force limits remain necessary for contact safety; they are not sufficient for joints.

Slide how cautious the robot is: naming the restricted joint gives almost all of the benefit

The controllers above change their decision rule as well as their knowledge. Here one learner with one rule sees six levels of knowledge about each person. It lifts only if its predicted chance of a safe completion reaches τ, and declines otherwise. Raise τ and every level trades safe completions for fewer harmful outcomes; what differs is how good a trade each level can get.

The reduction replicated, and shrank by almost half when the record had gaps

On 300 further new persons the reduction against the fixed rule replicated (−0.27 [−0.32, −0.21]). It held in a shifted population with more and tighter restrictions (38 vs. 170 harmful of 250) and under graded end-range resistance. Safe completions relative to the fixed rule were −0.01 [−0.06, +0.04] in the replication, +0.02 [−0.03, +0.07] in the shifted population and +0.06 [0.00, +0.12] under 20° graded resistance. With half of the restrictions unrecorded, the reduction shrank from −0.27 to −0.15 (64 vs. 108 harmful), and declining everyone flagged harmed fewer persons than the learned policy (50 vs. 64) at 29 fewer safe completions.

Where this stops

Scope: simulation only (MuJoCo/MyoSuite), no robot or human data; one task with single-point support and a passive simulated person; training and test share one body structure and joint-limit geometry, so transfer to other bodies is untested; the complete care record is an idealisation, and harm is a mechanical-exposure proxy, not injury.

  • Not established: safe-completion non-inferiority of the learned policy against the fixed rule (−0.01 [−0.05, +0.03]), and against the body model in the replication. At the secondary 2.5 N·m threshold the learned policy completed 4–8 points fewer lifts than the fixed rule.
  • Failed predictions: identifying the body by two gentle probe lifts was supported in a first set of 40 persons but not in its replication of 120, and probing harmed more persons than the same rule without it (29 vs. 21 of 120). Choosing the support site without declining was not shown to reduce harm.
  • Force guards: only force-magnitude guards with thresholds set across persons were tested. A guard that estimates joint torque from the force vector with a limb model is the most informative next comparison.
  • Physics perturbations (threefold reflex gain, wider individual variation) kept the joint-limit geometry, and the fixed rule's harm rate barely moved (36 % and 40 %), so they test muscle dynamics and body variation, not body structure.
  • Protocol: hypotheses were written in a locally kept, dated protocol before each round's data and tested on simulated persons never used before. It was not externally registered, and rounds 17–26 are not corrected for multiplicity. Every hypothesis, including the failures, is in Supplementary Table S1. As a check against a straw-man baseline, the best of all 48 fixed strategies chosen with hindsight harmed 203 of 600 persons; the learned policy harmed 53.
