Labels that cover only part of a recording can hide compositional difficulty in hand-motion data

To test whether a motion model can handle combinations it never saw, you hold out some label combinations from training. But on recorded motion, a correct label often describes only part of its recording, so the held-out motion can stay in training under another label. When OakInk-Image clips are joined in pairs and only the label's coverage changes, the measured penalty drops from +17.03 % to +3.87 %. On TACO it drops from +11.14 % to +3.69 % with 256 recordings per model; a smaller first test with 64 was not significant.

Holding out a label does not hold out its motion

The usual test holds out label combinations, say an action with a tool, while every action and every tool still appears in training. It then compares a model that never saw the held-out combinations with one that also saw other recordings of them; the share of test error this exposure removes is the penalty. The split is made on labels, but the model learns from motion. If a recording labelled “mug, drink” also contains the hand passing the mug, holding out “mug, pass” leaves that passing motion in training, and every label-level leak check still passes.

The last number is the one that surprised me. In the half-coverage version, test slices taken from the first clip still show the held-out combination, and the label still describes them, yet they lose most of their penalty too. So what changed is the training data, not what is scored.

Every estimate is read against a known zero, never against zero

The model is a variational autoencoder of hand motion, and the penalty uses its reconstruction error. Two models trained on different data always differ a little, so a positive penalty alone proves nothing. Each result is repeated over 40 random splits and read against references whose answer is known in advance:

The first TACO test did not work. With 64 recordings per model the drop was +6.44 % → +3.67 %, p = 0.095: not significant, inconclusive at this power. I then declared a second test with 256 recordings, before training it, on the same splits; that one showed the drop (p = 4.1 × 10−6). Both are in the paper, not pooled.

Where this stops

Scope: offline datasets and one model class (reconstruction error of a variational autoencoder), with no robot experiment. The main evidence is OakInk-Image; on TACO the drop holds with 256 recordings per model across splits, but was not significant with 64 (p = 0.095), and with held-out cells treated as a sample its p-value is 0.029 to 0.074 depending on the model. Under the more conservative Nadeau–Bengio correction no full-versus-half-coverage comparison is significant (p = 0.14 and 0.25). Part of OakInk-Image's penalty comes from wrist translation; the leak into the unexposed model and the dilution of the exposed model's held-out recordings change together and are not separated.
