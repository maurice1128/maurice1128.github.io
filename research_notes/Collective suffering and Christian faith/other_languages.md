# Scholarship and Primary Sources in Further Languages

Collective suffering and Christian (and Abrahamic) faith: Black Death, slave religion and comparative cases.

> **Scope.** This file covers Japanese, Korean, Portuguese (beyond Reis and Soares), Spanish, Czech, Polish, Scandinavian, Arabic, Hebrew and Yiddish, Greek, Russian and Ukrainian, and French work on Rwanda. It does not repeat the English, German, French, Italian, Catalan, Dutch, Latin or Chinese material in the other notes files.
>

> - **NDL**: National Diet Library Search OpenSearch API (Japanese books and articles).

> - **Sudoc**: French union catalogue SRU, UNIMARC records with PPN numbers.

## 1. Japanese

### Takeaway
Japanese work covers four of the review's cases.
- **Black Death.** The core is 石坂尚武's source edition and his books, which read plague religiosity as "suffering and mentality" (苦難と心性). One book explicitly links plague to the Reformation.
- **Kakure Kirishitan.** There is a live dispute. 宮崎賢太郎 holds that the hidden Christians became Japanese folk religion; 中園成生 traces their origins and offers a counter-reading.
- **Nagasaki.** A critical literature on Nagai's *hansai* (burnt offering) theodicy runs from 高橋眞司 to 篠崎美生子 (2013).
- **Theology of suffering.** Japanese Christianity produced its own suffering theology (北森嘉蔵, 1946). It also received Black theology early: Cone appeared in Japanese in 1973, and Kuribayashi adapted it to Buraku liberation (1991).

## 2. Korean

### Takeaway
Korean sources are the strongest addition in this file.
- **Minjung and han theology.** The originals by 서남동 and 안병무 are verified. Minjung theology is the clearest case of suffering interpreted as God's presence among the oppressed, built by theologians who were themselves imprisoned.
- **Two church-history literatures map onto the model.** The first is the shrine-worship (신사참배) controversy: the mainline church complied under Japanese rule, the resisters later split off (이진구 2025), and the case is "faith up, establishment trust down, faith migrates." The second is the Korean War, where suffering coincided with church growth and "this-worldly blessing" faith (김흥수 1999).
- **Black Death.** Korean scholarship now has its own clerical-mortality and flagellant studies (박흥식, 이필은, 이상동).

### Minjung and han theology

1. **Sŏ Nam-dong 서남동 [Suh Nam-dong]. 1983.** *Minjung sinhak ŭi t'amgu* 民衆神學의 探究 [Explorations in Minjung Theology]. Seoul: Han'gilsa 한길사. Reissued Seoul: Tongyŏn 동연, 2018.
   - Translation: key essays are in English in CTC-CCA 1981/1983 (below). A Japanese edition was not found.
   - Contribution: the foundational statement. ## 4. Spanish (Spain and Latin America)

### Takeaway
- **Cofradías.** Spanish material supplies the European template for Black brotherhoods: the Seville *negros* brotherhood, founded c. 1393 by archbishop's licence according to the literature, unverified here. Mexico, Panama and Cuba have their own literatures.
- **Cuba.** Ortiz and Cabrera document Afro-Cuban *cabildos* and Regla de Ocha and Palo practice. This is the "re-coding" branch: devotion migrated into African-controlled bodies that kept Catholic forms.
- **Taki Onqoy.** The 1560s Andean movement rejected Christianity outright amid epidemic and conquest. It is the model's rejection branch, and the scholarship now debates how far the colonial sources invented it.
- **Black Death in Iberia.** The key revisionist work is Castán Lanaspa (2020). Spanish Jewish-plague scholarship is represented by Cantera Montenegro (2022).

## 5. Czech

### Takeaway
Two Czech originals were verified. The first is Graus's 1949 Marxist study of the pre-Hussite urban poor, the root of the "crisis" reading of Hussite origins. The second is Šmahel's four-volume *Husitská revoluce* (1993), which has a German translation. Mengel (2011) remains the English anchor arguing that Bohemia's Black Death was milder than assumed. ## 7. Scandinavian (Norwegian, Swedish, Danish)

### Takeaway
- **Mortality.** Scandinavian historiography offers very high Norwegian mortality estimates (Benedictow) and the Nordic Deserted Farm Project (Sandnes 1978), which measured farm desertion. Desertion matters for the church because tithes and land rents collapsed.
- **Church-specific studies.** None were retrieved. Clerical mortality, the archbishopric of Nidaros and Birgitta of Sweden's plague revelations remain leads.
- **Sweden.** Myrdal's two books bracket a shift from a Marxist causal reading (1978) to a long-run "plague waves" model (2003).

## 8. Arabic (with English anchors)

### Takeaway
Muslim plague writing from 1348 onward framed plague as martyrdom (*shahāda*) for believers and mercy rather than punishment. It debated contagion against predestination and ruled on flight. Dols (1977) and Stearns (2011) remain the anchors.

For the model, the important point is that the theological interpretation differed from Christian "punishment for sin" readings while the epidemic was the same. That gives a rare test of the interpretation variable with the shock held constant.

Modern Arabic scholarship found: Būjarra (2011) on the Maghreb, where the jurist, the doctor and the ruler competed for authority over plague. This is an institutional-trust question in Islamic form.

## 9. Hebrew and Yiddish

### Takeaway
The Jewish 1348–49 record is a case where a minority suffered both plague and Christian massacre. The commemorations (the Nuremberg Memorbuch martyrology, Joseph ha-Kohen's *ʿEmeq ha-bakha*) frame the dead as martyrs for the sanctification of the Name (*qiddush ha-Shem*). Communal faith was reinforced and the persecuting Christian establishment was further delegitimized. Yuval's Hebrew book, verified with its English and German translations, is the major interpretive work on Jewish–Christian mutual images, including blood libel and messianic vengeance. In Holocaust-era Hebrew, Shapira's ghetto sermons (critical edition 2017) and Teichtal's turn to Zionism show faith intensified, and in Teichtal's case trust in his own anti-Zionist rabbinic establishment revised, under extreme suffering.
