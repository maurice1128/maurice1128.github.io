## 1. German-language scholarship (Graus, Bergdolt, Haverkamp, Borst, Bulst, Jankrift, Meier, flagellants, Leppin)

### Takeaway
The core German works all exist and are verified. Graus 1987 (*Pest – Geissler – Judenmorde*), Bergdolt 1994, Haverkamp ed. 1981 (with his essay on the 1348–50 persecutions), Bulst 1979 (*Saeculum*), Borst 1981 (*HZ*), Meier ed. 2005, Jankrift 2020 and Hübner 1931 (*Geisslerlieder*) are the standard German references. None of the major German monographs on 1348–50 has been translated into English. Graus exists in Czech (2020) and Bergdolt in Italian (2020) only. Volker Leppin's 2021 work on late-medieval piety "after the plague" is the newest major theological-historical contribution, and its book form was translated into English in 2025.

### Cited Findings

**František Graus (1987)**
- Graus, František. 1987. *Pest – Geissler – Judenmorde: Das 14. Jahrhundert als Krisenzeit*. Veröffentlichungen des Max-Planck-Instituts für Geschichte 86. Göttingen: Vandenhoeck & Ruprecht. Further editions: 2nd ed. 1988, 3rd ed. 1994 (same publisher). — [DNB](https://portal.dnb.de/opac.htm?method=simpleSearch&query=Graus+Pest+Geissler+Judenmorde). The series number is from my memory [unverified]; the DNB record gave title, publisher and the years 1987, 1988 and 1994.
- **Translations:** no English translation found. A Czech translation exists: Graus, František. 2020. *Mor, flagelanti a vraždění Židů: 14. století jako období krize*. Translated by Jan Dobeš. Praha: Argo. — [DNB](https://portal.dnb.de/opac.htm?method=simpleSearch&query=Graus+Mor+flagelanti)
- **Argument/relevance [knowledge-based]:** This is the classic German treatment of the 14th century as a *Krisenzeit*. Graus treats plague, the flagellant processions and the pogroms as linked responses within a broader "crisis of the late Middle Ages." He also critically examines the concept of "crisis" itself and the plague-causation model. It is essential for an argument that collective suffering produced both intensified penitential religiosity (flagellants) and violence and a challenge to clerical authority.

### Inferences
- In the German historiography, the dominant interpretive axis is *Krise* (Graus), the plague as catastrophe and its interpretation (Borst), and the social-political anatomy of the pogroms (Haverkamp). That suggests a framing for the article: religious responses (flagellants, processions, pogroms legitimised as divine-wrath remedies) were channelled by local power structures rather than by suffering per se.
- The flagellant evidence offers a strong example of faith *intensifying while trust in the institution eroded*. The movement was a lay-led penitential religiosity that bypassed clergy, and the pope condemned it in 1349. Hübner's edition of the songs and Segl's TRE article are the German anchors for this point.
- Leppin's 2021 work signals renewed German interest in post-plague *Frömmigkeit*. Because its book version now exists in English (2025), it is citable for English-reading reviewers.

- I could not verify a **Peter Segl monograph** on flagellants. Only the TRE article was found.
- I could not verify any **"Martin Bauer"** plague work. The name is likely a misremembering; drop it.
- I found no DNB record under **Bulst** with "Pest" in the title. Only the 1979 *Saeculum* article is verified (and his *Enzyklopädie des Märchens* article "Pest," DOI 10.1515/emo.10.144, date unknown).

## 2. French scholarship (Biraben, Chiffoleau, Ariès, Delumeau, Carpentier, Le Goff, Vauchez, Rapp, Vovelle, Schmitt)

### Takeaway
All the requested French classics are verified in the BnF. Among them, Biraben's two volumes (1975–76) and Chiffoleau's *La comptabilité de l'au-delà* (1980) are the most directly plague-religion-focused, and neither has an English translation. Ariès (*The Hour of Our Death*, 1981), Delumeau's *Le péché et la peur* (*Sin and Fear*, 1990), Le Goff (*The Birth of Purgatory*, 1984), Vauchez (*The Laity in the Middle Ages*, 1993) and Schmitt (*Ghosts in the Middle Ages*) do exist in English. I found no English translation of Delumeau's *La peur en Occident* (1978).

**Jacques Chiffoleau (1980) and related articles**
- Chiffoleau, Jacques. 1980. *La comptabilité de l'au-delà: Les hommes, la mort et la religion dans la région d'Avignon à la fin du Moyen Âge (vers 1320–vers 1480)*. Preface by Jacques Le Goff. - The French school (Annales / *histoire des mentalités*) supplies the article's strongest theoretical resources:

### Inferences
- A comparative English-language article can show non-English awareness most economically with the following core set:
  - Graus 1987 (crisis and flagellants and pogroms as linked)
  - Haverkamp 1981 (pogroms' political sociology; flagellant role pp. 43–46)
  - Bulst 1979 (research report)
  - Borst 1981 (interpretation of catastrophe)
  - Chiffoleau 1980 (post-plague "accounting" piety)
  - Biraben 1975–76 (religious responses survey)
  - Delumeau 1978/1983 (fear and guilt pastoral)
  - Carpentier 1962 (Orvieto)
  - Tenenti 1957 (counter-thesis)
  - Meersseman 1977 (confraternities)
  - López de Meneses 1956 (Aragon documents)
  - Leppin 2021 (recent German piety thesis)
- Citing English translations where they exist (Ariès, Delumeau's *Sin and Fear*, Le Goff, Vauchez, Schmitt) is standard practice. Chicago author-date style allows "Delumeau [1983] 1990" to show both dates.
- On the article's core question, continental scholarship broadly supports a **"both/and"** model:
  - suffering intensified penitential and intercessory religiosity (flagellants, Bianchi, masses for the dead, confraternities, plague saints);
  - and it channelled that religiosity into lay-controlled or extra-clerical forms, with the institutional Church sometimes co-opting it (Italy: the Bianchi; Avignon: masses and confraternities) and sometimes condemning it (the 1349 flagellants).
  - Whether faith "eroded" is not the dominant continental finding. Erosion of *clerical* trust is more commonly argued than erosion of *faith*. This last point is my synthesis, not a sourced claim.
