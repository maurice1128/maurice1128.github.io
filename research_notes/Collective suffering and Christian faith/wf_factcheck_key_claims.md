# Deep-research workflow result

**Question:** Adversarially fact-check the key historical claims behind a literature review on collective suffering and Christian faith. Verify or refute each with primary or peer-reviewed sources: (1) Pope Clement VI condemned the flagellants in a bull of 20 October 1349 and issued bulls in 1348 protecting Jews (names: Quamvis perfidiam / Sicut Judeis); (2) Bishop Ralph of Shrewsbury's 1349 letter allowed confession to a layperson in extremis; (3) Samuel Cohn's testament study places the shift in pious giving after the 1363 plague rather than 1348; (4) Robinson (2019) found post-plague ordinands were socially similar to earlier ones; (5) Dittmar & Meisenzahl (2020): each plague outbreak 1500–1522 raised the probability of adopting a Protestant church ordinance by about 14 percentage points; (6) Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama (2019): higher plague mortality associated with lower probability of Jewish persecution; (7) Bottan & Perez-Truglia (2015): Catholic abuse scandals reduced participation and giving without measurable decline in belief; (8) Belloc, Drago & Galbiati (2016): earthquakes strengthened religious authority only where bishops held political power; (9) Fountain (2010): fewer than 40% of WPA ex-slave respondents with datable conversions converted before emancipation; (10) AME membership about 20,000 (1856), 70,000 (1866), 207,000 (1876); (11) Presbyterian Church in Taiwan statements dated 29 Dec 1971, 1975 'Our Appeal', 16 Aug 1977 human rights declaration; (12) Kazoh Kitamori 'Theology of the Pain of God' first published 1946. Report each as confirmed / corrected / refuted / unverifiable with sources.

## Summary

Most of the review's checkable claims hold up against primary or peer-reviewed sources, but several need tighter wording. Confirmed: Ralph of Shrewsbury's plague mandate on lay confession (claim 2); Dittmar & Meisenzahl's 14-point first-stage estimate (claim 5, baseline only; the range across specifications is 11–14 points); the negative city-level link between mortality and persecution in Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama (claim 6, which needs the caveat that persecution rose overall); Bottan & Perez-Truglia's findings that participation and giving fell while belief showed no significant decline (claim 7); Fountain's under-40% figure (claim 9); and the 1946 Japanese first edition of Kitamori (claim 12). Three claims need correcting or qualifying. Claim 8 is the authors' interpretation, not a measured result: Belloc, Drago & Galbiati measure a delayed move to communal self-government in bishop-ruled cities, not religious authority itself. Claim 4 must cite Robinson's 2019 article in Archives, not his 2022 JEH article on tonsures. Claim 1 holds for Quamvis perfidiam (1348; secondary sources give 26 September 1348, with Sicut Judeis on 6 July 1348). The bull against the flagellants dated 20 October 1349 was not verified. Claims 3 (Cohn on testaments and the 1363 plague), 10 (AME membership figures) and 11 (the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan statement dates) found no supporting or refuting evidence in this verification run and remain unverifiable for now.

## Verified findings


### 1. (high, vote 3-0 (two claims merged))

Claim 2 is CONFIRMED. Bishop Ralph of Shrewsbury (Bath and Wells) issued a plague-time mandate, register entry no. 2130. It allowed the sick in articulo mortis to confess to a layman when no priest could be found, and to a woman if no man was available. It also let deacons give the Eucharist. The register dates it at Wiveliscombe, iv Id. Jan. A.D. 1348 (Old Style), which is 10 January 1349 in modern reckoning. Some secondary works (Gasquet) give 17 January 1349.

- Evidence: Holmes's introduction: 'On iv. Id. Jan., 1349, he issued (2130) a mandate concerning Confession ... In the absence of a priest they might confess to laymen and even if necessary to a woman.' Entry 2130 in vol. 10 is dated 'iv Id., Jan., A.D. 1348', with the year starting 25 March. The Latin endnote reads 'confessio facta laico ... sacramentum vero eucharistiae in absentia presbyteri poterit per diaconum ministrari.' Gasquet's translation adds that lay confessors were bound to keep what they heard secret. Cite the date as January 1349, preferably 10 January, following the register.
- Sources: https://somersetrecordsociety.org.uk/?p=237; Holmes (ed.), The Register of Ralph of Shrewsbury, Somerset Record Society vols 9-10 (1896), intro p. xxviii and entry 2130 at pp. 571-572 (archive.org); Wilkins, Concilia vol. 2, p. 745; Gasquet, The Black Death of 1348 and 1349, pp. 93-94; Horrox, The Black Death, pp. 271-272

### 2. (high, vote 3-0 (two claims merged))

Claim 5 is CONFIRMED for the baseline, with a refinement. In Dittmar & Meisenzahl (Review of Economic Studies 87(2), 2020), each additional plague outbreak in 1500–1522 raises the probability that a city adopted a Reformation church ordinance by 14 percentage points in the baseline first stage (Table 6, Panel A, col. 1: 0.14, s.e. 0.02, F≈34, N=239). Across specifications the estimate is 0.11–0.14.

- Evidence: The text on p. 986 says 'each additional plague outbreak between 1500 and 1522 increases the propensity of adopting a law by 14 percentage points. The F-statistic on the excluded instrument is above 34.' Table 6 Panel A, cols 1–6, gives 0.14, 0.13, 0.13, 0.12, 0.12, 0.11, all significant at 1%. Columns 5–6 add principality fixed effects, N=234, with weaker F statistics of 8.70 and 12.02. The outcome counts ordinances adopted by 1600. Suggested wording: 'about 11–14 percentage points (14 in the baseline)'.
- Sources: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/91195/3/Dittmar_public_goods_institutions_published.pdf

### 3. (high, vote 3-0 on three claims, 2-1 on the effect-size detail (four claims merged))

Claim 6 is CONFIRMED with a required qualifier. Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama (Journal of Economic Growth 24, 2019, pp. 345–395) find that at the city level, higher Black Death mortality is associated with a LOWER probability of persecuting Jews. In the aggregate, however, the plague raised the baseline probability of persecution. The relationship is non-monotonic.

- Evidence: Abstract: 'At an aggregate level we find that scapegoating led to an increase in the baseline probability of a persecution. However, at the city-level, locations which experienced higher plague mortality rates were less likely to engage in persecutions.' Baseline coefficient: -0.009*** per percentage point of mortality (N=124, constant 0.831). A 1 SD rise in mortality is associated with a 0.34 SD fall in persecution likelihood. In the working paper the SD is about 18 points and persecution probability peaks near 0.8 at 20% mortality before falling to 0 at 80%. In the 2019 published version the SD is about 18.5 points, the peak comes near 16% mortality (Fig. 3b), and the fall is about 17.1 points. The authors explain the result by economic complementarity between Jews and the majority. If citing the 2019 article, use its figures.
- Sources: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/77720/1/MPRA_paper_77720.pdf; https://core.ac.uk/download/213993671.pdf; econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:gwi:wpaper:2017-4; markkoyama.github.io/Papers/Black_Death_Persecutions_JEG.pdf (published version, doi:10.1007/s10887-019-09167-1)

### 4. (high, vote 3-0 (six claims merged))

Claim 7 is CONFIRMED, with precision on 'belief'. Bottan & Perez-Truglia, 'Losing my religion' (Journal of Public Economics 129, 2015, pp. 106–119), find that U.S. Catholic clergy abuse scandals caused a significant, long-lasting decline in religious participation in the affected zip code, about 3% of religious employees, which they equate to about 9% of the Catholic sub-population. They also find a long-lasting decline of about 1.3% in charitable giving, and no statistically significant decline in religious or pro-social beliefs.

- Evidence: Abstract: 'a scandal causes a significant and long-lasting decline in religious participation in the zip code where it occurs'; 'the scandals cause a long-lasting decline in charitable contributions'. The giving decline is said to be an order of magnitude larger than direct costs such as lawsuits. Introduction: 'a persistent 3% decline ... equivalent to a 9% decline in the Catholic sub-population'. The 9% is a rescaling, 3% divided by an assumed 30% Catholic share. The participation measure counts all-denomination religious employees. Giving fell about 1.3% in itemized IRS contributions, roughly $1.77B per year nationally. This is general charitable giving, not only giving to the church, and the drop is robust to excluding Catholic recipients. On belief: 'statistically insignificant. Due to a lack of precision, we cannot reject the possibility of small effects', though the authors can reject a decline in belief as large as the decline in participation. Suggested wording: 'no statistically significant decline in belief'.
- Sources: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:pubeco:v:129:y:2015:i:c:p:106-119; https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Perez-Truglia_2015_losing.religion.pdf

### 5. (high, vote 3-0 (four claims merged))

Claim 8 is CORRECTED (overstated). Belloc, Drago & Galbiati (Quarterly Journal of Economics 131(4), 2016, pp. 1875–1926) study northern-central Italian cities, 1000–1300. They find that earthquakes delayed the transition from autocratic rule to the self-governing commune only in episcopal see cities, where the bishop held both political and religious power. 'Strengthened religious authority' is the authors' interpretive mechanism, which they support with indirect evidence. It is not a measured outcome.

- Evidence: Abstract: 'the occurrence of an earthquake retarded institutional transition from autocratic regimes to self-government (the commune) in cities where the political and the religious leaders were the same person (episcopal see cities), but not in cities where political and religious powers were distinct.' Introduction: 'Our findings are consistent with the view that earthquakes, interpreted in the Middle Ages as manifestations of God's wrath against men, reinforced the authority of political leaders who at the same time were religious leaders.' The dependent variable is a city-year dummy for communal transition, in a difference-in-differences design across 121 cities, and the effect fades within about 10 years. The mechanism rests on three pieces of evidence: earthquakes that did no damage had the same effect, episcopal cities built and decorated more churches after earthquakes, and chronicle accounts. Suggested wording: 'earthquakes delayed communal self-government only where bishops held political power, which the authors attribute to enhanced religious-political authority.'
- Sources: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:131:y:2016:i:4:p:1875-1926; https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp5566.pdf

### 6. (high, vote 3-0 (three claims merged))

Claim 9 is CONFIRMED in substance, with a correction to the evidence base. Fountain, Slavery, Civil War, and Salvation (LSU Press, 2010), finds that fewer than 40% of individuals who gave a datable conversion experience became Christians before gaining freedom. The publisher attributes this to a quantitative survey of religious behavior together with the WPA slave narratives, not to WPA respondents alone. The finding is contested.

- Evidence: Publisher description: 'fewer than 40 percent of individuals who gave a datable conversion experience' became Christians before freedom; 'only after emancipation and the fulfillment of the predicted Christian deliverance' did conversion become more consistent. Patricia Hill's review describes an evidence base of about 4,000 WPA interviews plus narratives and autobiographies. Reviewers call the thesis revisionist. WPA respondents were mostly children at emancipation, which may push datable conversions later. Present this as Fountain's contested finding. The exact denominator in the book's tables was not checked.
- Sources: https://muse.jhu.edu/book/33081; https://mail.lsupress.org/books/detail/slavery-civil-war-and-salvation; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/475415; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/435222

### 7. (medium, vote 3-0)

Claim 12 is CONFIRMED, with a date distinction. Kitamori's Kami no itami no shingaku was first published in Japanese in 1946. The English translation, Theology of the Pain of God, first appeared in 1965 (John Knox Press, Richmond, VA). The British edition followed in 1966 (SCM Press, London, c1965), translated from the 5th Japanese edition.

- Evidence: CiNii record: 'Theology of the pain of God / Kazoh Kitamori ... London : SCM Press, 1966, c1965 ... First British edition, 1966 ... Translated from the Japanese Kami no itami no shingaku, 5th Japanese edition.' CiNii does not date the Japanese original. The 1946 date comes from Wikipedia and catalogue records, so the confidence is medium rather than high. If the review cites the English text, it should give 1965 or 1966 as the translation date.
- Sources: https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA38442752; English Wikipedia, 'Kazoh Kitamori', and library catalogues cited by the verifier

### 8. (medium, vote 3-0 (two claims merged))

Claim 4 is CORRECTED (citation) and partly supported. The relevant study is D. Robinson, 'The Black Death and clerical recruitment: the evidence of ordination lists', Archives 54/2 (2019), pp. 1–36. Its abstract says plague-period priests were socially and economically similar to those ordained earlier. It is not Robinson's 2022 JEH article on first tonsures (vol. 73(3), pp. 505–524), which deals only with pre-plague tonsures. Robinson's finding runs against Henry Knighton's contemporary charge that post-plague ordinands were widowers who were barely literate.

- Evidence: The 2022 JEH abstract covers only literacy, status and examination at first tonsure. The Archives 2019 abstract, found by the verifier through web search rather than read in full, says plague-period priests were socially and economically similar to earlier ones, and many came from families that had already supplied priests. Knighton, quoted in Moran, JEH 34:1 (1983), n. 56: 'confluebant ad ordines maxima multitudo quorum uxores obierant in pestilentia, de quibus multi illicterati et quasi meri laici.' Confidence is medium because only the abstract of the 2019 article was seen.
- Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-ecclesiastical-history/article/first-tonsures-in-england-in-the-first-half-of-the-fourteenth-century/74AFBB0FBAC56DE85AF44AF69B854419; https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-ecclesiastical-history/article/clerical-recruitment-in-the-diocese-of-york-13401530-data-and-commentary/DB61638191446F38BBE96DD337046837; Robinson, Archives 54/2 (2019), abstract found by verifier search

### 9. (medium, vote 3-0)

Claim 1 is PARTLY CONFIRMED. Clement VI issued Quamvis perfidiam in 1348, addressed to prelates, ordering protection of Jews against the charge that they caused the Black Death by poisoning wells. Secondary sources date Sicut Judeis to 6 July 1348 and Quamvis perfidiam to 26 September 1348. The bull against the flagellants dated 20 October 1349 was not verified in this run.

- Evidence: JVL: 'Quamvis perfidiam by Clement VI in 1348, addressed to various prelates, urged the protection of Jews against the accusation that they had brought on the Black Death by poisoning the wells.' Both sources are secondary. The review should cite Simonsohn, The Apostolic See and the Jews (the calendar number was not confirmed), or the papal registers. Some popular sources mix up the two bull names. The 20 October 1349 bull against the flagellants (often cited as Inter sollicitudines) found no supporting or refuting evidence.
- Sources: https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/papal-bulls (Encyclopaedia Judaica-derived); jewishcurrents.org; jewinthepew.org (secondary, cited by verifier)

## caveats

"Coverage is uneven. Five items (the 1349 flagellant bull, Cohn, the AME figures, the PCT dates, and Robinson 2019 beyond its abstract) had little or no primary verification. 'Unverifiable' means only that no claim survived this run, not that the claim is false. For Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama, the persecution peak is at about 20% mortality in the working paper and about 16% in the 2019 JEG version, and the SD is 18 versus 18.5 points; a CORE/Springer access block meant the published abstract was read only indirectly. For Bottan & Perez-Truglia, 'no decline in belief' means not statistically significant, with small effects not ruled out. For Belloc et al., 'religious authority' is the authors' inference, not a measured variable. Fountain's figure is drawn from WPA testimony that skews toward people who were children at emancipation, and it is disputed by reviewers. The Ralph of Shrewsbury mandate's day is 10 January 1349 in the register and 17 January in Gasquet."
