## Key Question 1: Early Christianity, the Antonine Plague (c. 165) and the Plague of Cyprian (c. 249–270): did epidemics drive Christian growth?

### Takeaway
Rodney Stark's 1996 thesis holds that Christians' care for the sick during the Antonine and Cyprianic epidemics raised their survival rates, drew converts and gave a theodicy that pagans lacked. It remains influential but is contested. Critics attack Stark's uncritical use of sources and his speculative arithmetic. Kyle Harper's work (2015, 2017) re-dates and re-diagnoses the Cyprianic pandemic, and Sabine Huebner (2021) revises its origin and chronology and cuts its link to the Decian persecution. For the article, the case shows a persecuted minority church that served as the sufferers' own community and offered a meaning-making theodicy. That fits the pattern "faith up + own institution up," though the causal claim about growth is debated.

### Cited Findings
- Stark, *The Rise of Christianity: A Sociologist Reconsiders History* (Princeton University Press, 1996; HarperSanFrancisco paperback, 1997). It argues that Christianity grew because it offered adherents a better quality of life. Stark devotes a chapter to the 2nd- and 3rd-century epidemics and argues that Christians stayed to nurse the sick and so survived at higher rates. — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rise_of_Christianity); [catalogue, ISBN 978-0-691-02749-4](https://www.sack.de/stark-the-rise-of-christianity/9780691027494); [Ehrman blog on the health-care thesis](https://ehrmanblog.org/superior-health-care-as-an-explanation-for-the-spread-of-christianity/)
- Commentators summarise the two epidemics in Stark as 165 CE (probably smallpox) and 251 CE (measles, in Stark's view). Stark estimates the Christian share of the empire at about 0.4% in 160 CE. — [Ehrman blog](https://ehrmanblog.org/?p=49782); [commentary summarised in search results](https://ehrmanblog.org/superior-health-care-as-an-explanation-for-the-spread-of-christianity/)
- Critique: Bart Ehrman calls Stark's "uncritical use of sources" the most criticised aspect of the book among early-Christianity specialists. He calls the health-care explanation "almost certainly wrong" and points to Elizabeth Castelli's 1998 response as especially trenchant. — [Ehrman blog](https://ehrmanblog.org/superior-health-care-as-an-explanation-for-the-spread-of-christianity/)
- Castelli, "Gender, Theory, and *The Rise of Christianity*: A Response to Rodney Stark," *Journal of Early Christian Studies* 6 (2) (1998): 227–257. It disputes Stark's readings of ancient evidence and his use of unitary "pagan" and "Christian" categories, while sharing his goal of a rational sociological explanation. — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/9959/summary)
- Hector Avalos, *Health Care and the Rise of Christianity* (Hendrickson, 1999, 166 pp.), argues that Christian health-care ideas formed a system that attracted converts. Reviewers doubt that health care was a primary growth factor. — [JECS review](https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/earl/summary/v009/9.2shelton.html); [CBHD](https://cbhd.org/bibliography/health-care-and-the-rise-of-christianity); [review](https://church.catholic-resources.org/Bible/Review_Avalos.htm)
- Harper, "Pandemics and Passages to Late Antiquity: Rethinking the Plague of c. 249–70 Described by Cyprian," *Journal of Roman Archaeology* 28 (2015): 223–260. It argues that the agent was probably a viral haemorrhagic fever (possibly Ebola-like) rather than smallpox or measles, which undercuts Stark's disease identification. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-archaeology/article/pandemics-and-passages-to-late-antiquity-rethinking-the-plague-of-c249270-described-by-cyprian/BF0A142F1864D05FDC0C3439D182B7B0); [DAI Zenon record](https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/001448245/Access)
- Harper, *The Fate of Rome: Climate, Disease, and the End of an Empire* (Princeton University Press, 2017). It identifies three great pandemics (Antonine 165, Cyprian 249, Justinian 541) and treats the Cyprianic plague as a massive exogenous shock. — [Harper CV / summaries](https://www.college-de-france.fr/sites/default/files/media/document/2023-07/Kyle-Harper-CV-2023.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Harper)
- Huebner, "The 'Plague of Cyprian': A Revised View of the Origin and Spread of a 3rd-c. CE Pandemic," *Journal of Roman Archaeology* 34 (2021): 151–174, doi:10.1017/S1047759421000349. She argues that the disease entered via Gothic invasions on the Danube rather than up the Nile. In her account it reached the empire only after Decius' death, so it "cannot be connected with" the 249 CE sacrifice edict. She also argues that it should "probably not be considered as the root of the [third-century] crisis itself," against Harper. — [Cambridge PDF](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4DD870D473D3DDB1DF0328CE2C7E3280/S1047759421000349a.pdf/plague_of_cyprian_a_revised_view_of_the_origin_and_spread_of_a_3rdc_ce_pandemic.pdf); [Zenon](https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/003018088/Description)
- A favourable reading: a 1998 FARMS/BYU review calls Stark's case that epidemics contributed to Christianity's rise "pervasive." — [BYU Studies](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/byusq/vol39/iss3/14); [Scripture Central](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/periodicals/journal-article/rodney-stark-rise-christianity-sociologist-reconsiders-history)

### Inferences
- Coding: (1) Sufferers' position: Christians were a marginal, at times persecuted minority with no state power. (2) Theology: Cyprian's *De mortalitate* frames plague as a trial of the faithful and death as release, and Christian care for the sick serves as charitable witness. (3) Church role: own community and protector. Stark's mechanism is mutual aid, while pagan civic cult was the "failing establishment." (4) Outcome: Christian devotion and institutional loyalty likely rose. Relative growth is plausible but numerically unproven.
- The case is analytically useful because the Christian church was *not* the establishment. That parallels the enslaved African American "invisible institution" more than Black Death Europe, and it supports a situational rather than ethnic explanation.
- Huebner's de-linking of the plague from the Decian persecution matters if the article claims that "plague + persecution" jointly intensified faith. The chronology is now disputed.

### Gaps
- Cyprian's *De mortalitate* and Dionysius of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *HE* 7.22) are the primary texts. Modern critical editions were not catalogue-verified in this pass.
- Harper's 2016 follow-up ("Another Eye-Witness to the Plague Described by Cyprian, with Notes on the 'Persecution of Decius'," *JRA* 29) was seen only as a title. Pages are **[unverified]**.
- Alain Bresson's review essay "Fates of Rome" in *Journal of Roman Studies* was seen only as a title. Year, volume and pages are **[unverified]**. The [Cambridge link](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/fates-of-romes/582207A45A234E88E0B7159CD94C27B6) exists.

## Key Question 2: Rwanda 1994: church complicity, institutional trust and post-genocide religious change

### Takeaway
Rwanda is the clearest modern case of a church acting as both establishment and perpetrator. Longman (2010) argues that the churches had consistently allied with the state and played ethnic politics. After the genocide, Christian devotion did not collapse. Instead it *migrated* to new Pentecostal and other Protestant churches (about 150 new Christian communities by 1997 and over 300 by 2000), and a smaller wave turned to Islam because Muslims had sheltered victims. The Catholic share fell from about 63% to roughly 50%. This is a strong "faith persists or rises, institutional trust in the complicit church falls" case.

### Cited Findings

The publisher's description: Rwanda's churches "consistently allied themselves with the state and played ethnic politics." - Conflicting figures: a 2002 *Washington Post* report gave Muslims as 14% of the population, while the 2022 census reports about 2%. The 14% figure is widely considered inflated. — [Wikipedia, Islam in Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Rwanda)

### Inferences
- Coding: (1) Position: Tutsi victims belonged to the same Catholic and Protestant churches whose hierarchies were allied with the Hutu Power state, so the sufferers were *inside* a church that was aligned with the perpetrators. (2) Theology: there was no dominant theodicy. Survivors' discourse and the new churches stress healing, deliverance and forgiveness, and some converts read the church's role as moral bankruptcy. (3) Church role: failing establishment and perpetrator (Longman; Rittner et al.). (4) Outcome: devotion is high but redistributed; trust in the Catholic institution fell, and Pentecostal, Adventist and Muslim communities gained.
- Rwanda is an excellent foil to enslaved African Americans. Both are Black populations with mission-derived Christianity. In one case the church was the sufferers' own (the "invisible institution," later the AME and Black Baptists), and in the other it was aligned with the perpetrators. That is strong evidence for a situational rather than racial explanation.

### Gaps
- No rigorous survey of survivors' personal faith (as opposed to affiliation) was found. Alana Tiemessen's 2005 paper "From Genocide to Jihad: Islam and Ethnicity in Post-Genocide Rwanda" is widely cited, but its venue is **[unverified]** (probably a conference paper).
- Statistics on Adventist growth were not retrieved. Rwanda's 2012/2022 census religion tables (NISR) should be consulted directly.

## Key Question 3: Japan: Nagasaki 1945 (Urakami Catholics, Nagai Takashi's *hansai* theology) and the Hidden Christians (Senpuku/Kakure Kirishitan), 1614–1873

### Takeaway
Japan offers two linked cases from the same Urakami/Nagasaki Catholic community. (i) Under 250 years of persecution, faith was preserved without clergy. When toleration came, some communities returned to the Catholic Church, while the Kakure kept their own lay-led tradition and declined institutional reintegration. (ii) In 1945 the atomic bomb fell on Urakami. Nagai Takashi's "burnt offering" (浦上燔祭説 *Urakami hansai-setsu*, the name philosopher Takahashi Shinji gave it) framed the dead as a sacrificial lamb that brought peace. The theology consoled the community and anchored rebuilding, but later critics (Takahashi; Diehl 2018; McClelland 2019; Shijo 2015) argue that it absolved the Emperor and the US and silenced survivors' anger.

### Cited Findings

US occupation authorities refused publication until an appendix on Japanese atrocities in Manila was added. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bells_of_Nagasaki); [NDL record](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/en/books/R100000002-I000001703305); [ECU catalogue](https://librarycatalog.ecu.edu/catalog/260209)
- Takahashi Shinji 高橋眞司 (Nagasaki University professor emeritus) named Nagai's interpretation "浦上燔祭説" (Urakami burnt-offering theory). ### Inferences
- Coding (Hidden Christians): (1) A persecuted, outlawed minority without clergy. (2) Theology centred on martyrdom, contrition (*contrição*) and ancestral fidelity, with origin narratives such as the *Tenchi* re-reading suffering. (3) The church had been driven out, so the community *became* its own church (lay *chōkata* and *mizukata* leaders) **[not sourced this pass]**. (4) Devotion was preserved across seven generations. After the 1865 "discovery" at Ōura and the 1873 lifting of the ban, part of the community rejoined Rome, while the Kakure declined, out of loyalty to ancestors' practice **[not sourced this pass; covered in Turnbull and Whelan]**. So faith stayed up while trust in the *returning* institution was conditional.
- Coding (Nagasaki 1945): (1) A historically persecuted Catholic minority inside an Imperial Japan that had treated them as suspect. (2) The dominant interpretation was a sacrificial theodicy (hansai: providence and atonement). (3) Church role: own community and consoler. Nagai, a lay Catholic, became the community's voice, and the church did not fail them. (4) The short-term outcome was intensified devotion and community loyalty (Diehl). The long-term outcome was contested memory: survivors' anger and critiques of the theodicy (McClelland, Takahashi, Shijo), which bear on *trust in the theodicy* more than trust in the church.
- The pairing shows that a *theological frame* can intensify faith (sacrificial meaning) and later become an object of critique. That is a useful nuance for the article's "situation" thesis: the same community, at different moments, re-evaluates the institution's interpretive authority.

### Gaps
- Hiroshima comparison: no Christian-specific study was retrieved. Hiroshima's Christian population was small, and John Hersey's *Hiroshima* features Jesuits and a Methodist pastor (Tanimoto Kiyoshi) **[not sourced this pass]**.
- The Urakami Yonban Kuzure (浦上四番崩れ, 1867–73 exile of Urakami Christians) was not sourced. It bridges the two cases and should be added from Turnbull or Japanese sources.
- Takahashi's original article coining 浦上燔祭説 (possibly in 1980s Nagasaki journals) was not located.

## Key Question 4: Taiwan: (A) the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT), the 228 Incident (1947) and the White Terror; (B) the post-1945 mass conversion of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples

### Takeaway
(A) The PCT, rooted among Hoklo- and Hakka-speaking Taiwanese and Indigenous peoples, was a persecuted "own community" church under the KMT party-state. Its 1971, 1975 and 1977 public statements (the 1977 "Declaration on Human Rights" called for Taiwan to become "a new and independent country"), the 1979 arrest of General Secretary Kao Chun-ming, and its later 228 truth-seeking turned shared political suffering into a contextual theology (homeland theology 鄉土神學, Chhut-thau-thiⁿ 出頭天 theology). The PCT's members and institutional standing grew. Mainland-affiliated churches that aligned with the KMT generally read the same events as punishment for political activity rather than as persecution. (B) Between about 1945 and 1960, under Japanese suppression of Indigenous religion, postwar dislocation and poverty, entire Indigenous villages converted, the so-called "mountain miracle" or "miracle of the twentieth century" (二十世紀的神蹟). Christian affiliation reached about 80% or more. Later the Indigenous Presbyterian churches became vehicles of Indigenous nationalism (Sia 2018; Stainton 1995).

## Key Question 5: Dalit mass conversion movements in India (c. 1860s–1940s) and later disillusionment

### Takeaway
Dalits (then "untouchables," "Pariahs") converted to Christianity *as communities* ("mass movements") under caste oppression and recurrent famine. Pickett's 1933 survey documented this, and many converts experienced Christianity as dignity and liberation. Later scholarship (Forrester, Webster, Mosse, Viswanath) shows caste persisting *inside* churches (separate seating, cemeteries, upper-caste clergy dominance). Missionaries also framed Pariah suffering as a religious problem, obscuring agrarian labour exploitation. This produced Dalit theology and some disaffiliation. It is a case of "faith up, then trust in the institution eroded by internal injustice."

- Pickett, *Christian Mass Movements in India: A Study with Recommendations* (New York: Abingdon Press, 1933). ## Key Question 6: African cases: Aladura (1918 influenza), Kimbanguism (1921), South African Zionists, the Uganda Martyrs and Biafra

### Takeaway
African Independent Churches (AICs) are the clearest pattern of "faith up, mission-church trust down, new own-church up." Epidemic (the 1918 influenza for Aladura), colonial oppression (Belgian Congo for Kimbangu) and dispossession (South African Zionists) led Africans to seek healing and prophecy that mission churches would not provide. They built churches under African leadership. These are the closest African analogues to the African American independent church movement, and they make "own community" a matter of institutional control rather than race.

## Key Question 7: Indigenous peoples and church-run residential schools (Canada) and child removal (Australia's Stolen Generations); Indigenous-led Christianity (Elcho Island 1979)

## Key Question 8: Pacific: Hawai'i's depopulation and Christianisation; Western Samoa's 1918 influenza

### Takeaway
In Hawai'i, epidemic depopulation from 1778 to the 1850s produced fatalism that made some Kānaka Maoli *more receptive* to Christianity (Archer 2018). The Calvinist mission became, through the ali'i (notably Ka'ahumanu), an establishment church. Later, Native resistance (Silva 2004) used both Christian and Hawaiian idioms against American annexation, which missionary descendants supported. In Western Samoa, the New Zealand administration's failure to quarantine in 1918 killed about 7,542 people by year's end. This is a case of a *colonial state* failing rather than a church, and the Samoan church remained the communal backbone **[outcome not sourced this pass]**.

## Key Question 10: Latin America after conquest: Nahua Christianity (Mexico) and Taki Onqoy (Peru, 1560s)

### Takeaway
Conquest-era epidemics killed most of Mesoamerica's and the Andes' population. In Mexico, mendicant friars built a Nahua Christianity that Burkhart shows was a "moral dialogue" re-encoding Christian concepts in Nahua categories. Adoption occurred, but on Nahua terms. In the Andes, the Taki Onqoy ("dancing sickness," c. 1564–c. 1580) explicitly rejected Christianity and urged a return to huaca worship. Its message held that the huacas were punishing natives with disease for accepting the Christian god. This is the clearest case of "faith down/rejection" when Christianity is identified with the conqueror causing the suffering.

## Key Question 11: Coding table and synthesis: which cases show (a) faith up + institutional trust down, (b) faith up + own institution up, (c) faith down/rejection; and (d) which cases best strengthen the article

### Takeaway
Across 22 cases, the variable that best predicts the outcome is **the church's structural position relative to the sufferers and the perpetrators**, not the sufferers' race or ethnicity. When the church is the sufferers' own (persecuted with them, led by them), suffering raises both devotion and institutional loyalty. When the church is a failing establishment or a perpetrator, devotion often survives or intensifies but migrates to new, sufferer-controlled institutions, and trust in the old church falls. Outright rejection of Christianity occurs when Christianity is identified with the perpetrator *and* an alternative sacred system is still available.

### Inferences
**(a) Faith up (or persisting) + institutional trust down.** The clearest cases:
1. **Rwanda 1994.** Devotion persisted, but people left the complicit Catholic Church for Pentecostal and Adventist churches and some for Islam (Kubai 2007; van 't Spijker). This is the single best counterpart to Black Death Europe's "failing establishment."
2. **African Independent Churches (Aladura 1918, Kimbanguism 1921, Zionists).** Suffering intensified faith, while mission churches lost trust to new African-controlled churches.
3. **Canada and Australia.** The church as perpetrator produced a large disaffiliation and a revival of Indigenous spirituality, but also persistent Christian identity and Indigenous-led revival (Elcho Island).
4. **Kakure Kirishitan.** Faith was preserved under persecution, but many declined to re-enter the returning Catholic institution.
5. **Dalits (later phase).** Intra-church caste eroded trust while Christian identity and Dalit theology deepened.

**(b) Faith up + own institution up (the African American pattern).**
1. **Taiwan PCT under the KMT (228 and White Terror).**
2. **Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church underground.**
3. **Early Christians in the 3rd-century plagues** (if Stark's mechanism holds).
4. **Taiwan Indigenous mass conversion.**
5. **Urakami Catholics in 1945** (short term).
6. **Vietnamese Catholic exodus 1954, the Philippines 1986, Indonesia 1965–66, Uganda martyrs.**

**(c) Faith down / rejection.**
1. **Taki Onqoy (1560s).** Explicit rejection of Christianity as the conqueror's religion that brought disease.
2. **Canada's Indigenous "no religion" (47%) and Indigenous-spirituality revival.** Partial rejection where the church was perpetrator.
3. **Rwanda's converts to Islam.** A minority, partly instrumental.
4. **Kakure as "folk religion"** (Miyazaki). Drift away from orthodox Christian identity rather than rejection.

**(d) The 2–3 cases that would most strengthen the article.**
1. **Taiwan: the PCT under 228 and the White Terror, contrasted with KMT-aligned Mainland churches, with the Indigenous mass conversion as a companion.** This is the strongest choice for a Taiwan-based author. Within one society and one era, Christian churches occupied different structural positions toward the same state violence. The PCT was the Taiwanese people's own persecuted church; its theology (homeland, Chhut-thau-thiⁿ, the 1977 human-rights Declaration) turned suffering into faith and institutional legitimacy. Churches aligned with the regime read the same events as political punishment. That holds religion, era and place roughly constant while varying the church's situation. The Indigenous case adds a third population (colonised minorities) where suffering produced near-total conversion. Chinese-language sources (黃應貴 1991; 邱韻芳 2004/2013; 周沛忻 2015; 鄭仰恩 2005) give the author a local scholarly base.
2. **Rwanda 1994.** It is the cleanest test of the "situation, not race" claim against the African American pairing. Both populations are African-descended and mission-Christianised. When the church was the sufferers' own (the African American "invisible institution," later the AME), faith and institution rose together. When the church was allied with the killers, faith survived but trust in the institution collapsed and devotion migrated to new churches and Islam.
3. **AICs (Aladura and Kimbanguism) or the Canadian residential schools.** Either shows the *mechanism* of institutional migration. Aladura arose in the 1918 influenza, a natural disaster comparable to the Black Death, and shows sufferers creating their own church when the establishment church failed them. The Canadian case shows the extreme of church-as-perpetrator with measurable disaffiliation (2021 census). The Taki Onqoy can serve as a brief "limit case" of outright rejection.

Overall analytic claim: the variation follows a two-by-two of (i) whether the church is controlled by or aligned with the sufferers and (ii) whether it is aligned with the perpetrator or the failing order. A theodicy (martyrdom, sacrifice, Exodus) supplies meaning only when the institution delivering it is credible to the sufferers. Nagasaki shows that even a community-owned theodicy can later lose credibility.

### Gaps
- Several outcome codings (Biafra, Samoa, Hawai'i's later split, post-1989 UGCC, post-1986 Philippine trust) rely on general knowledge and are marked **[not sourced]**. They need survey or ethnographic sources before publication.
- No study was found that directly compares PCT and Mainland-church responses to 228 using membership data. This is a research opportunity for the author.
