## 1. Classic comparative arguments: Tannenbaum, Elkins, Degler, Klein, de la Fuente, Herskovits, Bastide, Verger, Mintz and Price

### Takeaway
The comparative field began with Tannenbaum's (1946) claim that Iberian law and the Catholic Church recognized the enslaved person's moral personhood, so that slaves had routes into rights and eventual citizenship that Anglo-Protestant societies closed off. Elkins built on that claim, and Degler, Klein and the "material conditions" school pushed back on it. De la Fuente (2004) then turned the debate from "how humane was the law" to how enslaved people actually used legal and religious institutions to make claims. That move from institution-as-protector to institution-as-resource is the framing most useful for the article.

### Inferences
- Taken together, the debate supplies the article's two axes. Tannenbaum, Elkins and Klein treat the church as an *institutional mediator* between master and slave. Degler and the materialists answer that demography and economy matter more. De la Fuente's claims-making turn reconciles the two: the church matters as a *resource enslaved people can use*, and that depends on whether they have access to its structures (sacraments, courts, brotherhoods).
- None of these classic works is centrally about *faith intensification versus institutional distrust*. That is where the Brazil and British Caribbean case studies below do the real work.

## 2. Brazil: irmandades, Catholic establishment, the church as slaveholder, and African religion

### Takeaway
Brazil is the case where (a) Catholicism was established, (b) religious orders, notably the Benedictines and Jesuits, were among the largest slaveholders, and yet (c) enslaved and freed Africans held their *own* chartered Catholic corporations, the black lay brotherhoods (irmandades of Our Lady of the Rosary, São Benedito, Santa Efigênia and others). The scholarship shows that where Africans controlled church structures, devotion was intense and the brotherhoods were defended. Reis's 1836 Cemiterada was a riot *in defense of* church burial against a secularizing reform. Yet resistance could also be organized outside or against Catholicism, as in the 1835 Malê (Muslim) uprising and in calundu and other healing practices prosecuted by the Inquisition.

### Inferences
- In Brazil, collective suffering, above all mortality, fed a dense Afro-Catholic institutional life *because Africans had chartered corporate bodies within the church*. These bodies handled burial, festivals, election of kings and queens, and manumission aid. Reis's Cemiterada shows the laity defending a church practice against secular modernizers, which is the opposite of institutional distrust.
- At the same time, distrust or rejection of the *hierarchy* and the *slaveholding orders* coexists with this. Muslims organized revolt outside Catholicism (Reis 1986/1993), and calundu and healing persisted under Inquisition prosecution (Mello e Souza; Sweet). The distinction the article needs is therefore between *lay-controlled* and *hierarchy- or master-controlled* church structures, not "Catholic vs. Protestant" alone.
- Demography is a confound. A large African-born population (Mina in Rio, Angolas and Nagôs in Bahia) made ethnic brotherhoods and non-Christian alternatives viable. Degler's and Sweet's emphasis on African-born majorities should be controlled for.

## 3. Spanish America and Cuba: cofradías, cabildos de nación, the Inquisition and courts

### Takeaway
Spanish American scholarship shows Africans using the full apparatus of the Catholic establishment, including sacraments, ecclesiastical courts and the Inquisition, and above all cofradías and (in Cuba) cabildos de nación. They used these to build "Afro-creole" consciousness, ethnic identities and upward mobility. Cabildos were also the organizational base for the 1812 Aponte conspiracy. The institutional church thus functioned both as a channel of integration and as a scaffold for collective action, much as in Brazil.

## 4. British (and Danish/Dutch) Protestant Caribbean: missions, the Baptist War, Demerara, "Christian slavery"

### Takeaway
In the Protestant Caribbean the established Anglican Church was planter-aligned. Planters long resisted baptizing slaves under an ideology of "Protestant Supremacy" (Gerbner). Conversion came largely through *dissenting* missions: Moravians from the 1730s, then Methodists and Baptists. Enslaved converts used mission structures, including chapels, deacon and class-leader roles and literacy, to build their own leadership. In Demerara in 1823 and Jamaica in 1831–32 those structures became the networks of large rebellions. Enslaved Christians read spiritual equality as a claim to freedom and pushed missionaries toward abolitionism. This is the clearest case of suffering intensifying Christian faith while generating distrust of the planter-aligned establishment and of missionaries' "neutrality."

### Cited Findings
- **Turner, Mary. 1982. *Slaves and Missionaries: The Disintegration of Jamaican Slave Society, 1787–1834*. Urbana: University of Illinois Press. Reprint, Kingston: University of the West Indies Press, 1998.** [verified] Turner opens with the Kensington estate fire of 27 December 1831, which began the "Baptist War" led by Samuel Sharpe. The revolt's leaders were also leaders in the mission churches and the independent "native Baptist" sects. They expected missionaries to support their bid for wage labor and freedom, but the missionaries were committed to neutrality. Enslaved Christians turned spiritual equality into political action and so made the missionaries into antislavery campaigners. — [Cambridge (Schuler review metadata)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/slaves-and-missionaries-the-disintegration-of-jamaican-slave-society-17871834-by-mary-turner-chicago-university-of-illinois-press-1982-pp-223-maps-notes-bibliography-index-2505/6B99275A3C518FF61C69D3B136F844AE); [LibraryThing description](https://www.librarything.com/work/5798958)

### Inferences
- The British Caribbean is the best natural experiment on the "who controls the church" variable within Protestantism. The *established* Anglican Church, tied to planters and in places a slaveholder (Gerbner discusses the SPG), attracted little trust. *Dissenting* missions that gave enslaved people offices (deacons, class leaders, "daddies") produced intense faith. When missionaries counseled obedience or "neutrality," enslaved Christians acted on their own reading anyway (Turner; da Costa).
- This pattern, in which faith intensifies while trust in the *official* religious leadership erodes, is the closest Caribbean analogue to US independent Black churches and the "invisible institution."

## 5. Haiti and the French Caribbean: the Code Noir, Catholic missions, Vodou and the Revolution

### Takeaway
French law (the 1685 edict later called the Code Noir) *required* Catholic baptism and instruction. In practice, missions were thin, planter-dependent and often distrusted by planters as a "dangerous zeal" (Peabody). Vodou formed from West African vodun and Kongolese Catholicism and served as an idiom of insurgent solidarity in 1791 (Bois Caïman, whose details are contested). The independent Haitian state then repeatedly criminalized Vodou (Ramsey). Here a weak institutional church plus mandatory nominal baptism yielded religious autonomy *outside* the church, and suffering fed an alternative religious institution rather than Christian church loyalty.

## 6. Africa-side: Kongolese Christianity before enslavement

### Takeaway
A significant share of captives from West Central Africa came from the Kingdom of Kongo, which had been officially Catholic since the early sixteenth century and had its own "Kongo Christianity." Thornton, Fromont and Heywood show that many enslaved people arrived *already Christian*, with an African-controlled Christianity and, in the Antonian movement, prophetic critiques of European missionaries. This undercuts the assumption that the American church was their sole source of Christianity and helps explain both quick uptake of Catholic forms (brotherhoods) and independence from clerical control.

## 8. Analytic synthesis: which Atlantic comparison would most sharpen a US–Black Death comparison?

### Takeaway
**Recommendation: use colonial and imperial Bahia and Rio (Brazil) as the primary Atlantic comparison, with the British Caribbean mission colonies (Jamaica 1831–32, Demerara 1823) as the secondary Protestant control.** Brazil is the only slave society that reproduces the *institutional* features of the Black Death setting:
- an established Catholic Church;
- clergy and religious orders that were themselves wealthy landholders (and here slaveholders);
- a dense web of *lay confraternities* that ordinary people controlled and that specialized in death, burial and intercession.

The same people both intensified their devotion and contested the institution, which isolates the variable the article argues for: *who controls the religious structures through which suffering is processed*.

### Cited Findings (supporting points, drawn from works above)
- Brazilian black brotherhoods controlled burial, funerals, festivals and elected kingships. These are the same death-and-intercession functions at the center of late-medieval lay piety. — [Soares/Duke](https://www.dukeupress.edu/People-of-Faith); [Kiddy/PSU](https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/0-271-02693-6.html); [Reginaldo/UNICAMP](https://www.repositorio.unicamp.br/acervo/detalhe/335419)
- When the state attacked church burial in 1836, Bahians rioted to defend it (the Cemiterada). Mass-mortality religion attached to lay-controlled ecclesiastical practice, not to state or hierarchy. — [UNC Press, *Death Is a Festival*](https://uncpress.org/book/9780807854457/death-is-a-festival)
- The Benedictines ran the most extensive sugar operations of any corporate body in the colony, so the church as master was concrete, as was the church as landlord in plague-era Europe. — [Schwartz, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/plantations-of-st-benedict-the-benedictine-sugar-mills-of-colonial-brazil/1E8E88B92614EF44E908CF188D22784C)
- Some enslaved people organized collective resistance through a *rival* religious institution (Islam, 1835) or ritual healing outside the church. This is an analogue to heterodox or extra-ecclesial responses to plague. — [Reis, JHU Press](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/2365/slave-rebellion-brazil); [Sweet, UW](https://history.wisc.edu/publications/recreating-africa-culture-kinship-and-religion-in-the-african-portuguese-world-1441-1770/)
- Protestant control: in Jamaica and Demerara, enslaved Christians intensified faith through *dissenting* congregations where they held office, while acting against missionaries' counsel of "neutrality" and against the planter-aligned establishment. — [Turner](https://www.librarything.com/work/5798958); [Gerbner](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780812224368); [da Costa/NHC](https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/fellows-books/crowns-of-glory-tears-of-blood-the-demerara-slave-rebellion-of-1823)

### Inferences
- **Why Brazil over Cuba or Spanish America.** Cuba and Mexico have the same Catholic-and-confraternity structure, but the scholarship there centers on law and claims-making (de la Fuente; Bennett) rather than on mass mortality and death ritual. Reis's *Death Is a Festival* gives Brazil a ready-made "religion of death under collective suffering" study that maps directly onto plague-era themes: burial in sacred ground, intercession, confraternal funerals, and conflict between laity and institutional reformers.
- **Why Brazil over Haiti.** Saint-Domingue lacked a functioning Catholic institutional presence for the enslaved, so faith moved outside the church entirely (Vodou). It is a useful *limit case*, showing that the outcome is no church loyalty at all when no institutional access exists. But it shares too little institutionally with fourteenth-century Europe to anchor the comparison.
- **The design this implies.** A three-way comparison on two institutional variables:

  | Case | Church established? | Church a slaveholder or landlord? | Sufferers control their own church structures? | Predicted response to suffering |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | US South | No (Protestant pluralism) | Yes, some denominations and institutions | Largely no until the independent Black churches and the "invisible institution" | Intensified faith, distrust of white churches |
  | Brazil | Yes (Catholic) | Yes (Benedictines, Jesuits, Carmelites) | **Yes (irmandades)** | Intensified Afro-Catholic devotion; hierarchy contested; non-Catholic resistance at the margins |
  | British Caribbean | Yes (Anglican) | Planter-aligned (SPG estates) | Yes in dissenting missions (deacons, class leaders) | Intensified faith; revolt organized through chapels |
  | Black Death Europe | Yes (Catholic) | Yes (monastic landlords) | Yes (lay confraternities) | [outside this researcher's scope; to be supplied from medieval sections] |

  The Brazilian row is the one that matches the Black Death row on all three institutional variables while differing on race. That is exactly what the claim that institutional role, not race, shapes the response requires.
- **Caveat for the writer.** Demographic weight is a confound. Brazil's large African-born population sustained ethnic brotherhoods and Islam. The article should acknowledge Degler's demographic critique of Tannenbaum ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neither_Black_nor_White)) and Sweet's argument that much Catholic practice was nominal.

### Gaps
- Claims about Black Death lay confraternities, clerical mortality and anticlericalism are outside this research scope and were not verified here. The writer should source them from the medieval section.
- No study was found that explicitly compares Afro-Brazilian brotherhoods with post-1348 European confraternities. That absence is itself a contribution the article can claim.
