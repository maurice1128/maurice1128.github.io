## 1. Foundational and classic works (pre-1990 framing of the field)

### Takeaway
The classic literature (1903–1987) set up the two big debates that frame the "faith vs. institution" question.
- **Did African religion survive the Middle Passage?** Herskovits said yes; Frazier said slavery stripped it away.
- **Was slave Christianity accommodation or resistance?** This runs from Genovese's paternalism model to the "invisible institution" of Raboteau, Levine, Blassingame, Sobel, and Stuckey, in which enslaved people built a Christianity they controlled, apart from their masters' churches.

The classic consensus already separates *faith* (often intensified) from the *masters' institutions* (often rejected). It tends to assume broad Christianization, an assumption that later work (Section 8) challenges.

### Cited Findings

**Raboteau, Albert J. 2004. *Slave Religion: The "Invisible Institution" in the Antebellum South*. Updated ed. Oxford; New York: Oxford University Press. (Orig. 1978.) [V]**
- Library catalogues confirm the 2004 "Updated ed." (paperback, 416 pp., ISBN 9780195174120). One citation source calls it the "2nd ed.", but catalogues use "Updated ed." The book's contents run from an "African heritage" section to "The Invisible Institution", with chapters on catechesis and conversion, religious life in the slave community, and "religion, rebellion, and docility." — [MnLINK catalogue](https://search.mnlink.org/Record/b7f223bd-fac7-439b-b8fa-181bafbe09db); [Pluralism Project](https://pluralism.org/publications/slave-religion-%E2%80%9Cinvisible-institution%E2%80%9D-antebellum-south); [Biblio listing](https://biblio.co.uk/9780195174120)
- The 25th-anniversary edition adds a new chapter in which Raboteau reflects on the book's origins and its reception over 25 years. — [Biblio listing](https://biblio.co.uk/9780195174120)
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is the anchor text for the claim that enslaved people practised a Christianity they controlled, in secret "hush harbors" separate from the masters' supervised worship, while also attending biracial churches. It is the central source for the "invisible institution" side of the working premise. [GK for the hush-harbor detail]

**Genovese, Eugene D. 1974. *Roll, Jordan, Roll: The World the Slaves Made*. New York: Pantheon Books. [V]**
- Pantheon, 1974, about 823 pp. — [TCU review page](https://personal.tcu.edu/swoodworth/genoveseroll.htm); [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL5202429M/Roll_Jordan_roll)
- Masters and slaves both embraced paternalism, but for different reasons and with different ideas of what it meant. Religion is a major theme. Chapters include "Slave religion in hemispheric perspective" and "The Black preachers." — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roll,_Jordan,_Roll:_The_World_the_Slaves_Made); [Open Library TOC](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL5202429M/Roll_Jordan_roll)
- Black religion helped perpetuate paternalism and also shaped a distinctive Black reading of it. Enslaved people could use paternalism "as a weapon for defense and a potential weapon for liberation." — [TCU review](https://personal.tcu.edu/swoodworth/genoveseroll.htm); [NYRB review](https://www.nybooks.com/?p=27223)
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is the Marxian "accommodation-within-resistance" pole. Slave Christianity sustained both endurance and self-assertion and was never simply the masters' tool. It is useful for testing whether intensified faith *dampened* revolt.

**Levine, Lawrence W. 2007. *Black Culture and Black Consciousness: Afro-American Folk Thought from Slavery to Freedom*. 30th anniversary ed. Oxford; New York: Oxford University Press. (Orig. 1977.) [V]**
- First published by OUP in 1977 and in paperback in 1978. The 2007 edition (xxxiv + 522 pp.) adds a new preface. Chapter 1 is "The Sacred World of Black Slaves", and chapter 3 is "Freedom, Culture, and Religion." — [CiNii record](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA83206437); [Itinerario review record](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/itinerario/article/abs/lawrence-w-levine-black-culture-and-black-consciousness-afroamerican-american-folk-thought-from-slavery-to-freedom-30th-anniversary-edition-oxford-oxford-university-press-2007-xxxiv-522-pp-isbn-9780195305685-pbk-1895/CABD526F68A11A307DD5023EAD00191B)
- **Faith vs. institution:** the spirituals and folk tales show a "sacred world" in which enslaved people saw themselves as a chosen people and fused the sacred and the secular. After emancipation the "rise of secular song" (blues) marks a partial shift away from that sacred world. It is useful for showing how collective suffering produced a folk theology independent of white churches. [GK for the chosen-people and sacred-world argument]

**Blassingame, John W. 1979. *The Slave Community: Plantation Life in the Antebellum South*. Rev. and enl. ed. New York: Oxford University Press. (Orig. 1972.) [V]**
- The 1979 revised edition runs to xviii + 414 pp. The original appeared in 1972 and was substantially expanded after Blassingame answered critics at a 1976 conference. — [AltPress library record](https://www.altpress.org/apc_library/display/689); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Slave_Community)
- It was one of the first US slavery histories told from the enslaved person's point of view. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Slave_Community)
- **Faith vs. institution:** it presents religion as part of the culture that let enslaved people keep autonomy and personality against the "Sambo" thesis. [GK]

**Sobel, Mechal. 1979. *Trabelin' On: The Slave Journey to an Afro-Baptist Faith*. Westport, CT: Greenwood Press. Abridged pbk. ed., Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 1988. [V; place verified: Open Library]**
- The 1979 Greenwood edition (Contributions in Afro-American and African Studies, no. 36; xxv + 454 pp.) includes an appendix listing Black Baptist churches from 1758 to 1864. The 1988 Princeton paperback is abridged and drops the appendices. — [NEICON catalogue](https://arch.neicon.ru/xmlui/handle/123456789/972265); [TCU review](https://personal.tcu.edu/swoodworth/SobelTrabelin.html); [Booktopia](https://www.booktopia.com.au/trabelin-on-mechal-sobel/book/9780691006031.html)
- **Thesis:** enslaved people fused African sacred cosmologies with Baptist teaching into a distinct "Afro-Baptist" sacred cosmos. Critics call the evidence for a coherent worldview thin. — [TCU review](https://personal.tcu.edu/swoodworth/SobelTrabelin.html)
- **Faith vs. institution:** conversion was a reworking, not an adoption. Black Baptist congregations, many formally organized before 1865, were institutions Black people partly controlled.

**Stuckey, Sterling. 1987. *Slave Culture: Nationalist Theory and the Foundations of Black America*. New York: Oxford University Press. [V-partial: page count varies]**
- OUP 1987, paperback 1988. Stuckey treats the counterclockwise ring shout as an African ritual directed toward ancestors and gods, which unified diverse African peoples and underlies the later Pan-African impulse. — [Encyclopedia.com "Ring Shout"](https://encyclopedia.com/humanities/applied-and-social-sciences-magazines/ring-shout); [Taylor & Francis chapter "Slavery: Circle Culture"](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9780203415924-7/4-slavery-circle-culture-sterling-stuckey)
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is the strongest "Africanist" reading. Christianity was largely a surface over an African-derived religious core, which supports the claim that many enslaved people's deepest religious allegiance was not to the masters' church.

**Herskovits, Melville J. 1941. *The Myth of the Negro Past*. New York: Harper & Brothers. [verified: Open Library, place New York]**
- The first publication of the Carnegie Corporation's Study of the American Negro. It set out to debunk the "myth" that African Americans had lost their African culture under slavery. — [Wikipedia: Herskovits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melville_J._Herskovits); [Encyclopedia.com "African American Religions: History of Study"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/african-american-religions-history-study)

**Frazier, E. Franklin. 1963/1964. *The Negro Church in America*. [verified: Open Library — Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1963; New York: Schocken Books, 1964; combined Frazier/Lincoln ed., Schocken, 1974]**
- Sources disagree on the year (1963 or 1964). Open Library confirms Liverpool University Press 1963 and Schocken (New York) 1964.
- **Frazier's position:** slavery stripped Africans of their social heritage, so African American culture "was born in America." The Herskovits–Frazier disagreement is often described as one of degree rather than kind, and Frazier accepted that African survivals existed in the Caribbean and Latin America. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/african-american-religions-history-study)
- **Faith vs. institution:** Frazier gives the institutional reading, in which the church became the "nation within a nation", the main social institution of a people denied others. [GK for the phrase]

**Du Bois, W. E. B., ed. 1903. *The Negro Church*. Atlanta: Atlanta University Press. [V]**
- Report of the Eighth Atlanta Conference for the Study of the Negro Problems (May 1903). It is often called the first book-length sociological study of African American religion and covers finances, public opinion, denominational diversity, and belief. — [DocSouth summary](https://docsouth.unc.edu/church/negrochurch/summary.html)
- **Faith vs. institution:** Du Bois treats the church as a social institution with roots in Africa (he points to the obeah or "medicine man" as a precursor of the Black preacher [GK]). The *institution* is analysed apart from personal piety.

**Woodson, Carter G. 1921. *The History of the Negro Church*. Washington, DC: Associated Publishers. [V]**
- The title page reads Associated Publishers, Washington, 1921, and the preface is dated September 1921. Reportedly the first book Associated Publishers issued. One Goodreads date of 1927 is an error. — [Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38963.html.images); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/african-american-religions-history-study)
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is the first historical survey of the Black church, two decades after Du Bois's sociological study, and it focuses on independent denominations as achievements of self-direction.

**Mays, Benjamin E. 1938. *The Negro's God as Reflected in His Literature*. Boston: Chapman & Grimes. Reprint, New York: Russell & Russell, 1968; Atheneum. [V]**
- Chapman & Grimes, c1938, 269 pp. It studies how Black writers conceived of God and related God to their temporal circumstances. — [TAMU catalogue](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000151089/Description); [Encyclopedia.com: Mays](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/mays-benjamin-e); [Open Library reprint](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL5618249M/The_Negro)
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is a direct early source on the theodicy split. Mays distinguished "compensatory" ideas of God (otherworldly consolation) from "constructive" ones (God demanding social change), and noted strands of doubt or abandonment of God in Black literature. **Clarke, Erskine. 2005. *Dwelling Place: A Plantation Epic*. New Haven: Yale University Press. [V]**
- Yale, c2005. It covers 1805–1869 and the parallel lives of the Jones family and the people they enslaved, based on the family's letters. **Wilmore, Gayraud S. 1998. *Black Religion and Black Radicalism: An Interpretation of the Religious History of African Americans*. 3rd ed., rev. and enl. Maryknoll, NY: Orbis Books. (Orig. 1972.) [V for 3rd ed.]**
- Orbis, c1998, 328 pp. The third edition adds a new chapter on African roots. Its thesis is that Black religious history is bound up with the freedom struggle, from slave rebellions through Black Power. — [Hull library record](https://library.hull.ac.uk/record=b2088980~S3); [Bookshare](https://www.bookshare.org/browse/book/3651222)
- **Faith vs. institution:** Wilmore separates "Black religion" (radical and folk-rooted) from "the Black church" (which can be deradicalized and institution-bound). This analytic distinction is useful for the paper.

### Inferences
- The classic historiography already **separates faith from institution**. Raboteau, Levine, Blassingame, and Stuckey all locate authentic slave religion *outside* the masters' supervised churches. Wilmore distinguishes radical Black religion from the institutional Black church.
- The **Herskovits–Frazier debate** maps directly onto the premise. If African systems survived (Herskovits, Stuckey, Gomez), then "moving toward God" may really be a move between gods or a blending of traditions. If they did not (Frazier, Butler), Christianity filled a vacuum created by trauma.

## 2. Conversion and slaveholders' resistance to baptism (17th–19th centuries)

### Takeaway
Since the 1990s, colonial historiography has shown that conversion was **slow, contested, and resisted by masters** for more than a century, partly because baptism was feared to imply freedom.
- Protestant missionaries answered by building a "Christian slavery" ideology tied to race (Gerbner, Goetz, Glasson).
- Mass Black conversion came only with the evangelical revivals after c. 1760–1830 (Frey & Wood). It produced biracial congregations whose white leaders became the authors of proslavery Christianity (Irons, Snay, Sparks).
- Antebellum "slave missions" (Cornelius, Clarke) both offered a controlled gospel and opened space for Black religious leaders.

This is strong support for the "slow and contested conversion" part of the premise.

### Cited Findings

**Gerbner, Katharine. 2018. *Christian Slavery: Conversion and Race in the Protestant Atlantic World*. Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press. [V]**
- Penn Press, Early American Studies, 2018. Missionaries reaching the plantation colonies in the 1670s found that most slave owners rejected the idea of converting the enslaved. Quaker, Anglican, and Moravian missionaries answered with a vision of "Christian Slavery" (Christianity would make slaves loyal and hardworking) and increasingly used racial language. Enslaved Christians developed an alternative Protestantism that linked conversion to literacy and freedom. — [New Books Network](https://newbooksnetwork.com/katharine-gerbner-christian-slavery-conversion-and-race-in-the-protestant-atlantic-world-u-pennsylvania-press-2018); [De Gruyter front matter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.9783/9780812294903-fm/pdf); [J. British Studies review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-british-studies/article/katharine-gerbner-christian-slavery-conversion-and-race-in-the-protestant-atlantic-world-early-american-studies-philadelphia-university-of-pennsylvania-press-2018-pp-280-3995-cloth/5D3927F325A15445479C5AE37E524333)
- **Critique:** an *Itinerario* reviewer calls the subsidiary claim, that enslaved people's baptism challenged proslavery evangelicalism, "far too thin." — [Itinerario review (Cambridge Core)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/itinerario/article/katharine-gerbner-christian-slavery-conversion-and-race-in-the-protestant-atlantic-world-philadelphia-university-of-pennsylvania-press-2018-296-pp-isbn-9780812224368-2495/E04EFA82B63D3CF310699E3804A57FF8) (URL rebuilt from a proxy link in the search results)
- The coverage is Atlantic-wide (Barbados, Jamaica, and others), so mention the Caribbean only in passing.

**Goetz, Rebecca Anne. 2012. *The Baptism of Early Virginia: How Christianity Created Race*. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press. [verified: Open Library, place Baltimore]**
- JHUP 2012, about 240 pp. Its central concept is **"hereditary heathenism"**: the belief that Africans and Indians were inherently incapable of true conversion. The idea grew with plantation slavery and turned loose ideas about difference into fixed racial categories. Enslaved people and some Anglican ministers resisted the planters' racial ideas. The evidence is county court records from Accomack, Northampton, Surry, and York. — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/17321); [Mixed Race Studies summary](https://mixedracestudies.org/wp/?p=41482)
- **Faith vs. institution:** the colonial church and law actively *excluded* Africans from Christian privilege. Virginia's 1667 statute declared that baptism did not free a slave [GK]. This is the institutional root of later Black distrust of white churches.

**Glasson, Travis. 2012. *Mastering Christianity: Missionary Anglicanism and Slavery in the Atlantic World*. New York: Oxford University Press. [V]**
- OUP 2012 (some listings say 2011; paperback 2017). A study of the SPG (founded 1701). Its missionaries started out hoping to convert enslaved people and improve their treatment, but the Society increasingly allied with masters and came to treat slavery and Christianity as compatible, even mutually beneficial. Case sites are the Codrington plantation in Barbados, which the SPG owned, and Cape Coast Castle. — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/27227); [Church History review record](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/church-history/article/mastering-christianity-missionary-anglicanism-and-slavery-in-the-atlantic-world-by-travis-glasson-oxford-oxford-university-press-2012-x-318-pp-5500-cloth/A96B75FDB7CAD281D002ED6320DCC7C6)
- **Faith vs. institution:** the Church of England as a *slaveowning institution* is direct evidence that enslaved people's distrust of the masters' church had concrete grounds.

**Butler, Jon. 1990. *Awash in a Sea of Faith: Christianizing the American People*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. [verified: Open Library, place Cambridge, Mass.; chapter title still unverified]**
- Harvard UP, copyright 1990 (paperback c. 1992). Butler argues that the 18th century, not Puritanism, shaped American religion. In the chapter on slavery he argues that slavery produced an **"African spiritual holocaust"** that largely destroyed African religious *systems* (though not all practices). White clergy then offered a Christianity of paternalism and obedience. Reviewers dispute whether "holocaust" is the right word and whether the destruction was as complete as he claims. — [Cameron Blevins quals notes](https://www.cameronblevins.org/quals/Butler_AwashinaSeaofFaith.html); [First Things review](https://firstthings.com/the-social-history-of-american-religion); [Readings listing](https://www.readings.com.au/product/9780674056015/9780674056015)
- The exact chapter title (the brief's "Enslavement and the African Spiritual Holocaust") is **[unverified]**. The *phrase* is attributed to Butler in secondary sources, and his 1979 *AHR* article on Anglican slave conversion is also unchecked.
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is the Frazierian counter-pole to Stuckey and Gomez. It implies that the turn to Christianity followed a traumatic collapse of the African religious *institutions* themselves.

**Frey, Sylvia R., and Betty Wood. 1998. *Come Shouting to Zion: African American Protestantism in the American South and British Caribbean to 1830*. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press. [V]**
- UNC Press, March 1998, 304 pp. It traces conversion from the transfer of African religions through the growth of Protestantism up to 1830. Conversion was a two-way process between Black and white Christians, and "Africa is the starting point." One reviewer says the American South dominates over the Caribbean. — [UNC Press](https://uncpress.org/book/9780807846810/come-shouting-to-zion); [The North Star review](https://swh.princeton.edu/~jweisenf/northstar/volume1/frey_wood.html)
- **Faith vs. institution:** mass conversion came late, after the Great Awakening and the post-Revolutionary Baptist and Methodist revivals. It was driven by evangelical forms (ecstatic worship, Black exhorters) that let African practices survive in new form.

**Irons, Charles F. 2008. *The Origins of Proslavery Christianity: White and Black Evangelicals in Colonial and Antebellum Virginia*. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press. [V]**
- UNC Press 2008. White evangelicals' proslavery theology grew out of their dealings with Black evangelicals, so Black Christians unintentionally shaped the proslavery argument. Its periods are "Fishers of men, 1680–1792"; "Growing pains, 1792–1815"; and "The flourishing of biracial Christianity, 1815–1831." White authorities allowed Black religious activists considerable autonomy even while preaching proslavery doctrine. — [Elon University news](https://www.elon.edu/u/news/?p=170063); [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/378757); [UAM TOC](https://biblioteca.izt.uam.mx/vufind/Record/JSTOR_ocn607863666/TOC)
- **Faith vs. institution:** biracial churches were real institutions that enslaved people joined in large numbers. Black membership simultaneously *intensified* Black faith and *fed* the white institution's proslavery ideology. This complicates any simple "rejected the masters' church" claim.

**Cornelius, Janet Duitsman. 1999. *Slave Missions and the Black Church in the Antebellum South*. Columbia: University of South Carolina Press. [V]**
- USC Press 1999, xii + 305 pp. It covers white missions to the enslaved in the 1840s and 1850s and how Black religious leaders used those missions to build an organized Black church. Sources are church records and slave narratives. — [Church History review](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/church-history/article/slave-missions-and-the-black-church-in-the-antebellum-south-by-janet-duitsman-cornelius-columbia-university-of-south-carolina-press-1999-xii-305-pp-3495-cloth/76D09AAF4A443108672C6768AEC565BD); [Nashville PL catalogue](https://catalog.library.nashville.org/Record/CARL0000144075)
- Cornelius also wrote *"When I Can Read My Title Clear": Literacy, Slavery, and Religion in the Antebellum South* (Columbia: University of South Carolina Press). The publisher page exists ([USC Press](https://uscpress.com/When-I-Can-Read-My-Title-Clear)), but the year (1991 [GK]) is **[unverified]**.

**Sparks, Randy J. 1994. *On Jordan's Stormy Banks: Evangelicalism in Mississippi, 1773–1876*. Athens: University of Georgia Press. Reprint 2012. [V]**
- UGA Press 1994, reprinted in paperback 2012. A social history of Baptists, Methodists, and Presbyterians. It shows that the typical Mississippi evangelical church was **biracial** and that African American influence shaped southern ritual and practice. — [UGA Press](https://ugapress.org/book/9780820341477/on-jordans-stormy-banks); [Encyclopedia.com: Sparks](https://encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/sparks-randy-j-1957)
- **Faith vs. institution:** evidence that many enslaved Christians worked *inside* white-controlled institutions and were not only in hush harbors.

**Snay, Mitchell. 1993. *Gospel of Disunion: Religion and Separatism in the Antebellum South*. New York: Cambridge University Press. Reprint, Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1997. [V]**
- CUP 1993, UNC reissue 1997. Religion contributed much to southern separatism through the religious defence of slavery, the denominational schisms of the 1830s–40s, and the religious logic of secession. It focuses on elite urban ministers of the Deep South. — [UNC Press](https://uncpress.org/book/9780807846872/gospel-of-disunion); [TCU review](https://personal.tcu.edu/swoodworth/Snay.html); [Cambridge Core conclusion](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/books/gospel-of-disunion/conclusion-religion-the-origins-of-southern-nationalism-and-the-coming-of-the-civil-war/158FA30CE6B4E42B789B71FB023319CF)
- **Faith vs. institution:** this is the white institutional context that enslaved Christians saw as "slaveholding religion."

**Sensbach, Jon F. 1998. *A Separate Canaan: The Making of an Afro-Moravian World in North Carolina, 1763–1840*. Chapel Hill: Omohundro Institute and University of North Carolina Press. [V]**
- UNC/OIEAHC, March 1998, 368 pp. It draws on German church records, including Black Moravian memoirs. - **Faith vs. institution:** a case of sincere Black conversion into a white denomination, followed by *institutional* exclusion. This sequence (embrace, then segregation) recurs across the field.

**Boles, Richard J. 2020. *Dividing the Faith: The Rise of Segregated Churches in the Early American North*. New York: New York University Press. [V]**
- NYU Press, Early American Places no. 17, 2020/21. Based on records of more than 400 congregations, it shows that many 18th-century northern churches were interracial but unequal. Separate Black and Indigenous congregations formed by the late 1700s, more over access and equality than over theology, and churches were largely segregated within about fifty years. — [New Books Network](https://newbooksnetwork.com/dividing-the-faith); [Phi Beta Kappa Key Reporter](https://www.keyreporter.org/articles/2020/dividing-the-faith-tracing-the-rise-of-americas-segregated-churches/); [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.18574/nyu/9781479801657.001.0001/html)
- **Faith vs. institution:** Black Christians left white *institutions* because of unequal treatment while keeping (and often intensifying) their *faith*. This directly supports the faith/institution split.

### Inferences
- The conversion literature supports the premise's claim that conversion was **slow and contested**, but for a reason the premise does not stress: for much of the 17th–18th centuries **masters**, not the enslaved, were the main obstacle (Gerbner, Goetz, Glasson).
- The literature also complicates "rejected the enslavers' church." Irons, Sparks, Sensbach, and Boles show that many Black Christians *joined* biracial churches and left them when institutional inequality became intolerable. Rejection was often a *later response* to exclusion, not a starting stance.

## 3. African and Islamic continuities; conjure; non-Christian religion

### Takeaway
A strong body of work since 1984 shows that **a significant minority of enslaved Africans were Muslims** and that some kept Islamic practice for decades (Austin, Diouf, Gomez). Other work shows that **African-derived ritual traditions** persisted alongside or inside Christianity: Kongo-derived practices, conjure, ancestor-focused burial, and spirit possession (Creel, Young, Chireau, Rucker, Stuckey).

The best recent work (Chireau especially) rejects a sharp Christian-vs-African binary. Many enslaved people treated conjure and Christianity as **complementary**. This supports the premise that some enslaved people did *not* move toward the Christian God, but warns against counting "African vs. Christian" as a clean either/or.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Islam and African-derived practice are **well documented** but involved minorities: Muslims were a minority of captives, and conjure usually ran alongside Christianity. They are strong counterexamples to a universal "turn to God" but do not overturn the overall Christianizing trend by the late antebellum period.
- Chireau's complementarity thesis suggests a better framing for the paper. Collective suffering drove enslaved people toward **multiple religious resources at once**. Christianity, conjure, and Islam were not always competing loyalties.

## 4. Theodicy, Exodus, resistance, and doubt or unbelief

### Takeaway
This literature most directly addresses whether suffering intensifies faith or erodes trust.
- **Exodus** was the master narrative of Black collective identity and politics (Glaude, Smith, Raboteau, Wilmore). It turned suffering into chosen-ness, a pattern of *intensified* but *reinterpreted* faith.
- **Revolt leaders** (Turner, Vesey, Gabriel) drew on apocalyptic or Exodus readings (Breen, Egerton, Rucker), while the institutional churches were suppressed after each revolt.
- **Douglass's distinction** between "the Christianity of Christ" and "the slaveholding religion of this land" is the classic primary-source statement of keeping faith while rejecting the institution.
- **Theodicy and humanism scholarship** (Mays, Jones, Pinn, Cameron) documents a real but minority tradition of doubt and unbelief rooted in slavery's brutality.

### Cited Findings

**Douglass, Frederick. 1845. *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave*. Boston: Anti-Slavery Office. "Appendix." [V for text; imprint GK]**
- The appendix explains that his attacks were on "the slaveholding religion of this land," not "Christianity proper." He "loves the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ" and "hates the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land." ## 5. Gender and enslaved women's religious life

### Takeaway
Gender-focused work argues that "slave religion" was never gender-neutral. Enslaved women's religious cultures took shape around sexual violence, motherhood, and kin loss (Wells-Oghoghomeh). Women's spiritual autobiographies turned slavery's "hell without fires" into a "New Jerusalem" (Pierce). Womanist theology reads Hagar's wilderness as a theology of *survival* rather than *liberation* (Williams). This offers a third model: faith as survival, neither liberation nor erosion.

### Cited Findings

**Wells-Oghoghomeh, Alexis. 2021. *The Souls of Womenfolk: The Religious Cultures of Enslaved Women in the Lower South*. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press. [V]**
- UNC Press, September 2021, 320 pp. It runs from West African origins through the US Lower South to the Civil War and argues that gender and race together formed the religious subject. Chapters cover motherhood, sexuality, ritual, and spiritual power. It won the 2022 AAR Best First Book in the History of Religions and the ASWAD prizes, and was a Frederick Douglass Prize finalist. — [UNC Press](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469663609/the-souls-of-womenfolk); [New Books Network](https://newbooksnetwork.com/the-souls-of-womenfolk); [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/97313); [UNC Press Blog](https://uncpressblog.com/2021/10/27/the-souls-of-womenfolk-the-birth-of-the-enslaved-female-soul/)
- **Faith vs. institution:** enslaved women's religion drew on African-derived and Christian resources outside formal church structures. It is crucial for avoiding a male-preacher-centered account.

**Pierce, Yolanda. 2005. *Hell Without Fires: Slavery, Christianity, and the Antebellum Spiritual Narrative*. Gainesville†: University Press of Florida. [V-partial: place]**
- UPF, March 2005 (paperback 2021). It studies five early Black spiritual autobiographers: George White, John Jea, David Smith, Solomon Bayley, and Zilpha Elaw. **Williams, Delores S. 1993. *Sisters in the Wilderness: The Challenge of Womanist God-Talk*. Maryknoll, NY: Orbis Books. (20th anniversary ed., Orbis, 2013.) [verified: Open Library, place Maryknoll, N.Y.]**

## 6. Northern free Black churches and the AME (c. 1787–1865)

### Takeaway
Independent Black denominations were founded explicitly in response to discrimination inside white Methodist and other churches. The AME was formalized in 1816 under Richard Allen after the St. George's walkout tradition. These churches are the institutional embodiment of the faith/institution split: **keep the faith, leave the white institution, build your own.** Recent scholarship treats Allen as a "Black founding father" and the AME as both a freedom church and an institution with its own internal tensions.

## 7. Civil War and Reconstruction (1861–1877)

### Takeaway
Emancipation produced a **mass exodus of Black Christians from white-controlled southern churches** into independent Black congregations and denominations (Dvorak, Montgomery, Stowell, Walker, Hildebrand). It is the clearest large-scale evidence for the faith/institution split: Black membership in white Southern Baptist and Methodist churches collapsed while Black church membership rose. Freedpeople read emancipation through Exodus, Jubilee, and apocalyptic frameworks (Harper). Black churches became political hubs, though not uniformly (Turner, Hahn). White northern and southern Protestants rebuilt a "white republic" that abandoned freedpeople (Blum).

## 8. Recent scholarship (2010–2026) and revisionist challenges

### Takeaway
Post-2010 scholarship pushes in three directions relevant to the premise:
1. **Quantitative revisionism.** Fountain argues that most enslaved people were *not* Christians before emancipation, which challenges Raboteau-era assumptions of near-universal slave Christianity.
2. **Gendered and Africana-centred approaches.** Wells-Oghoghomeh and S. Johnson reframe "slave religion" through gender, colonialism, and empire.
3. **The study of nonbelief and of representation.** Cameron on Black freethought, and Evans on how the trope of innate Black religiosity was constructed and used against Black people.

Gerbner (2018), Boles (2020), and Turner (2020) extend the institutional critique.

## 9. Testing the working premise: evidence map

### Takeaway
The premise holds **in its main outlines but needs three corrections**:
1. **"Moving toward God" was real but late and partial.** Mass conversion came in the late 18th and 19th centuries, and Fountain argues most commitment came only after 1865. The main obstacle before about 1760 was *masters'* refusal, not slaves' rejection.
2. **"Rejecting the enslavers' church" was often a response to exclusion, not a starting stance.** Many enslaved and free Black people joined biracial churches (Irons, Sparks, Sensbach, Boles) and left when inequality hardened, culminating in the post-1865 exodus (Dvorak, Montgomery).
3. **Doubt or unbelief and non-Christian religion are documented but are minority currents** (Diouf, Austin, Gomez, Chireau, Cameron, Pinn). Even Black-controlled churches developed their own institutional exclusions of women and folk practice (Turner, Walker, Dickerson).

### Inferences
- **For the comparative journal article,** the enslaved African American case is best read as a case of **faith-institution decoupling**. Collective suffering at the hands of a Christian slaveholding order produced:
  - (a) intensified but *reinterpreted* faith, through Exodus, the chosen people, damnation of oppressors, and survival faith;
  - (b) the *construction of alternative institutions* (the invisible institution, then independent Black denominations);
  - (c) minority currents of *non-Christian persistence* and *doubt*.
- Erosion of trust attached to **specific white institutions** (the SPG, the slaveholding churches, the MECS, and white northern churches after 1877) far more than to God.
- **Timing matters.** Fountain and Harper suggest that collective *liberation* (1865) produced the largest wave of formal Christian commitment. Arguments that *suffering* drove faith should therefore be qualified. For many people, freedom made institutional Christianity possible and desirable for the first time, because Black people could control it.
- **The main live debates to present in the lit review:**
  1. Herskovits/Stuckey/Gomez vs. Frazier/Butler on African continuity.
  2. Raboteau's "invisible institution" (broad Christianization) vs. Fountain's minority-Christian thesis.
  3. Genovese's accommodation vs. Wilmore/Rucker's resistance.
  4. Exodus-liberation (Glaude, Cone) vs. wilderness-survival (Williams).
  5. Faith vs. freethought (Cameron and Pinn vs. the "naturally religious" trope critiqued by Evans).
