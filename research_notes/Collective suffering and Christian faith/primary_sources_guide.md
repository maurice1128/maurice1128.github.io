## Black Death (Europe, 1347–c.1500): what primary sources exist, in which editions and translations, where to access them, and what they show

### Takeaway
The best way into the Black Death material is still two English anthologies: Horrox (1994) and Aberth (2005; 2nd ed. 2017). Both translate excerpts from almost every chronicle, bull, episcopal letter and medical tract listed below. Many of the underlying 19th-century Latin and vernacular critical editions are free on the Internet Archive or dMGH. The sources split usefully on the question. Lay-driven devotion (flagellant songs, processions, wills and guilds, ars moriendi) shows faith intensifying, often outside clerical control. Institutional documents (episcopal letters allowing lay confession, papal bulls the towns ignored, chroniclers' complaints about fleeing or greedy priests) show strain on trust in the clergy.

#### Black Death: source-critical issues
- **The chroniclers are almost all clergy, especially mendicants and monks**: Venette (Carmelite), Herford (Dominican), Clyn and Piazza (Franciscans), li Muisis (Benedictine abbot), Knighton (Augustinian canon). Their complaints about the secular clergy's flight and greed partly reflect mendicant-versus-secular rivalry, so they do not straightforwardly measure lay distrust. Corroborate with wills (A41), guild ordinances (A42) and episcopal registers (A35). [Inference, standard in the historiography; individual affiliations as listed, unverified per item]
- **Literary borrowing.** Boccaccio and Kantakouzenos model their plague descriptions on earlier classical or early-medieval accounts (Paul the Deacon; Thucydides) [unverified attribution]. Their "breakdown of rites" passages are partly topoi.
- **Anthology dependence.** Horrox and Aberth give excerpts. A journal article should cite the critical edition for every quotation and give the translation's page. Many 19th-century Latin editions (Rolls Series, SHF, Potthast, Hegel) are on IA as public-domain scans [OK-IA rows above].
- **Hostile sources on flagellants and Jews.** Nearly all descriptions of the flagellants come from clerical opponents, and the Jewish "confessions" were extracted under torture (Marcus/Fordham, A6). They cannot be read as neutral reports.
- **Delayed composition.** Knighton (c.1390s), Stefani (1370s–80s) and Königshofen (c.1400) write decades later. Their judgments that the clergy declined may project onto 1348–50 the anticlericalism of the Lollard and Schism era.
- **Most open texts are older editions.** The modern critical editions (Branca, Porta, Rodolico, Hofmeister, Martin) are mostly print-only. dMGH (A18) is the main exception [OK-200].

### Inferences
- The sources support a **two-track model**. Devotion intensified through lay-controlled channels: flagellant songs (A19), pilgrimage (A23), guilds (A42), testamentary chantries (A41) and ars moriendi (A43). Institutions lost some trust where they failed to deliver sacraments (A33), could not enforce their decrees (A31, A32), or were seen as greedy (A20, A36, A13).
- The plague bishops' own letters (A33, A34, A36) are the strongest evidence. In them the institution records its own weakness, which counters the charge that the chroniclers' anticlericalism is only mendicant polemic.
- The Islamic and Byzantine comparanda (A25–A27) let the author test whether Latin anticlerical currents depend on Latin ecclesiology, such as the confession requirement and the priest-dependent sacraments.

## Slavery among African Americans (c.1619–1877): what primary sources exist, in which editions, where to access them, and what they show

### Takeaway
Two kinds of source dominate. Twentieth-century ex-slave testimony (WPA 1936–38 and Fisk 1927–30) is vast but mediated by interviewers. Nineteenth-century published narratives and Black church writings are authored by African Americans but shaped by abolitionist editors and genre. Almost all the antebellum narratives are free on UNC's *Documenting the American South* (DocSouth). Together they show a sharp, repeated distinction between devotion to "the Christianity of Christ" and distrust of "slaveholding religion" and its white institutions (Douglass's phrase, B6), which led to independent Black churches (Allen, AME).

#### Slavery: source-critical issues
- **Mediation by white interviewers and editors (WPA).** Most WPA interviewers were white Southerners. Informants were elderly, poor and often dependent on relief, and their memories were of childhood. Dialect transcription and state-office edits shape the texts (Blassingame 1975; Escott 1979; Musher 2001; Stewart 2016, all in B5 [unverified this session]). Religious testimony about kind masters or white preachers may be deference.
- **The Fisk interviews (B3, B4)** were collected by Black interviewers in the late 1920s, which provides a control. They were selected for religious conversion, which inflates evidence of intense devotion.
- **Abolitionist mediation of the antebellum narratives.** Prefaces by white sponsors (Garrison for Douglass, Child for Jacobs), amanuenses (Wilson for Northup, Eliot for Henson, Gilbert for Truth, Fisher for Ball) and genre conventions push toward attacks on "slaveholding religion". Their anticlericalism toward white churches is partly a campaign strategy. Use church records (B30, B33) and missionary reports (B26, B27) to corroborate.
- **Coerced or hostile records.** Gray's *Confessions* (B16) and the Vesey trial (B32) were produced by white authorities after a rebellion.
- **Survivorship and literacy bias.** Published narrators were mostly fugitives, often male, literate and Protestant. Muslim (B15) and African-derived practice is under-represented.
- **Institutional sources are written by insiders.** Planter-missionary (B26, B27), SPG (B29) and Moravian (B30) records register enslaved people's distrust only indirectly, through walkouts, attendance figures and discipline cases.

### Inferences
- The slavery evidence fits the same two-track pattern as the Black Death evidence. Devotion intensifies in self-controlled spaces: hush harbours (B19), conversion visions (B3), spirituals (B23) and independent Black churches (B12, B31). Distrust targets white-controlled institutions: Douglass's appendix (B6), Walker's Article III (B17), the walkouts in Jones's reports (B26), and the exits from biracial churches (B33, B34).
- Jones and Allen's 1794 Philadelphia yellow-fever pamphlet (B13) is a strong pivot text. In it, an epidemic visibly strengthens a Black church that had already broken with a white institution.
