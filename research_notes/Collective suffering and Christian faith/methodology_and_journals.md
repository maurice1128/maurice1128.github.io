## A1. Comparative-historical method: how to justify comparing cases ~400 years and two continents apart

### Takeaway
The canonical literature gives the author clear warrant for a "contrast-of-contexts" / variation-finding comparison of non-contiguous cases. Sewell's reading of Bloch makes comparison a logic of hypothesis-testing, not a matter of proximity. Skocpol and Somers, and later Mahoney, show that small-N comparison works when cases are matched on theoretically defined variables rather than on time or place. The main objections are Lieberson's small-N determinism critique, histoire croisée and transnational critiques of treating cases as independent units, and Steinmetz on incommensurability. The article should name and answer each one.

## A2. Comparative-religion methodology

### Takeaway
J. Z. Smith's critique, that comparison is a scholarly act of selection made "for the scholar's own purposes" and is not a discovery of essences, is now the standard starting point. Freiberger (2019, 2018) gives the most usable current procedural model. The author should state the *tertium comparationis* (the third term under which both cases are compared, here "collective suffering × institutional response") and the scholar's explicit purpose.

### Cited Findings
- Smith, Jonathan Z. 1982. "In Comparison a Magic Dwells." In *Imagining Religion: From Babylon to Jonestown*, 19–35. Chicago: University of Chicago Press. **[verified: Open Library (book, 1982); chapter pages 19–35 unverified]**. Smith criticizes comparison by "similarity and contiguity" (associative, magical thinking) and calls for comparison disciplined by theory and the scholar's stated interests. *(content not re-checked)*
- Smith, Jonathan Z. 1990. *Drudgery Divine: On the Comparison of Early Christianities and the Religions of Late Antiquity*. Jordan Lectures in Comparative Religion 14. Chicago: University of Chicago Press. **[verified: Open Library, 1990]**. Comparison is a disciplined exaggeration in the service of knowledge, and it is always triadic ("x resembles y more than z with respect to …"). *(content not re-checked)*
- Patton, Kimberley C., and Benjamin C. Ray, eds. 2000. *A Magic Still Dwells: Comparative Religion in the Postmodern Age*. Berkeley: University of California Press. **[Publisher ✓]** (https://www.ucpress.edu/books/a-magic-still-dwells/paper). Per UC Press, the volume tries to "bridge the divide between postmodern scholars who reject comparison and those who defend it" and argues comparison "can work at different scales and for different purposes." ### Inferences
- In the methods section, use Smith's triadic formula explicitly. For example: "Black Death Christianity resembles enslaved African American Christianity *more than it resembles* [e.g., post-1918-flu mainline Protestantism] *with respect to* the joint presence of high collective mortality and low power position under a clerical class allied with power." - Freiberger's "redescription and rectification" maps onto the claim that "race" redescribes poorly, while "power position / church role" redescribes both cases well.

## A3. Race as an analytic variable: critiques and the medieval-race counterpoint

### Takeaway
There is a strong, citable consensus across history (Fields), sociology and demography (Zuberi; Omi and Winant), and causal-inference methodology (Holland; Sen and Wasow; VanderWeele and Robinson; Greiner and Rubin) that "race" is not a well-defined causal variable. It is a bundle of socially constructed exposures, positions, and ascriptions. That directly licenses the author's move to disaggregate it into situational variables. But medievalists (Heng; Hahn; Bartlett) show that medieval Europe did practise racialization, especially of Jews and Muslims, and the Black Death pogroms are a central example. The author must therefore say that they are *not* claiming the medieval case was race-free. Their claim is narrower: the outcome is explained by power position, theology, and church role rather than by a "race" variable.

- A useful byproduct: medieval Jews in 1348–1351 become a **third, within-period contrast case** (low power position, non-Christian theology, persecuted by church-adjacent crowds). That strengthens the variable logic.

## A4. Published precedents comparing pre-modern and modern cases (religion / disaster / slavery)

### Takeaway
There are genuine, verifiable precedents for long-durée or distant-case comparison involving plague, religion, persecution, slavery, and trust. The strongest are in economics, history, and sociology journals (QJE, AER, AHR, Historical Research, AJS, ASR, Law and History Review), not specifically in CSSH or JSSR. I could verify **no specific CSSH, JSSR, Social Science History, or Journal of World History article** making a Black-Death-to-modern comparison before the search budget ran out. That is a gap, not evidence that none exist.

### Cited Findings

"The Slave Trade and the Origins of Mistrust in Africa." *American Economic Review* 101 (7): 3221–3252. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.7.3221 **[Crossref ✓]**. Compares medieval plague with modern (post-1894) bubonic plague across about 500 years. It is a methodological precedent for using distant-period comparison to question identity assumptions.

## Synthesis: five strongest arguments for comparability, and a journal fit ranking

### Takeaway
Comparability rests on (1) the logic of the method rather than proximity, (2) theoretically defined variables observable in both cases, (3) within-case mechanisms, (4) a shared theological repertoire (entanglement), and (5) established long-durée precedents. A fit-ranked journal table follows.

### Cited Findings: the 5 strongest arguments for comparability

1. **Comparison is a logic of hypothesis-testing, not of proximity.** Bloch's preference for neighbouring societies was practical; Sewell shows the comparative method is a way of testing explanatory hypotheses wherever the relevant variables vary. Skocpol and Somers's "contrast of contexts" and Tilly's "variation-finding" comparison explicitly accommodate distant cases. — Sewell 1967 (https://doi.org/10.2307/2504361); Skocpol and Somers 1980 (https://doi.org/10.1017/S0010417500009282); Tilly 1984 (Russell Sage); Mahoney 2004 (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.soc.30.012703.110507).
2. **Cases are matched on theorized variables, not on era or "race"; "race" is unbundled into observable sticks.** A most-different / diverse-case design is legitimate when the analytic variables (power position, theological interpretation, church role) are defined independently of either case. Sen and Wasow's "bundle of sticks", Fields on race as ideology produced by domination, and Zuberi on deracializing statistics justify replacing "race" with its components. Kolchin's slavery–serfdom comparison is the template for comparing American slavery with a non-racial European hierarchy. — Seawright and Gerring 2008 (https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912907313077); Sen and Wasow 2016 (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-032015-010015); Fields 1990 (NLR I/181); Zuberi 2001; Kolchin 1987 [unverified]; Patterson 1982 [unverified].
3. **Causal claims rest on within-case process tracing, answering the small-N critique.** Each case gets its own temporal sequence, e.g., clerical flight or complicity → lay interpretive autonomy → intensified personal faith alongside institutional distrust. Lieberson's objection is met by not relying on Millian two-case elimination. — Lieberson 1991 (https://doi.org/10.2307/2580241); Mahoney 2000 (https://doi.org/10.1177/0049124100028004001); Mahoney 2012 (https://doi.org/10.1177/0049124112437709); Bennett and Checkel 2015 (https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139858472); Beach and Pedersen 2013 (https://doi.org/10.3998/mpub.2556282); Steinmetz 2004 (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0735-2751.2004.00225.x).
4. **The cases are entangled through a shared Christian theodicy repertoire, which makes "theological interpretation" a common variable.** Histoire croisée and transnational critics argue that cases are linked, not independent. Here that linkage is an asset: the same scriptural resources (Exodus, Job, providential punishment) circulate from Latin Christendom into Protestant evangelicalism. Smith and Freiberger require the scholar to state a triadic, purpose-driven comparison, which this design supplies. — Werner and Zimmermann 2006 (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2303.2006.00347.x); Kocka 2003 (https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2303.00228); Smith 1982 [unverified]; Patton and Ray 2000 (UC Press ✓); Freiberger 2019 (https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780199965007.001.0001); Freiberger 2018 (https://doi.org/10.3390/rel9020038).
5. **Established long-durée precedents link pre-modern suffering to religion, trust, and persecution, and reviewers accept them.** Voigtländer and Voth (600 years, Black Death to Nazi era), Cohn's comparisons of plagues from Athens to AIDS, Nunn and Wantchekon (slavery → mistrust), Bentzen (disaster → religiosity), and Gorski's long-period historical sociology of religion all show that the discipline accepts comparisons across centuries when variables and mechanisms are explicit. The medieval-race scholarship (Heng, Hahn, Bartlett) lets the author pre-empt the charge of anachronism by acknowledging medieval racialization while coding it as power position. — https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs019; https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2281.2012.00603.x; https://doi.org/10.1086/ahr/107.3.703; https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.7.3221; https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/uez008; https://doi.org/10.2307/2657295; https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108381710; https://doi.org/10.1215/10829636-31-1-1.
