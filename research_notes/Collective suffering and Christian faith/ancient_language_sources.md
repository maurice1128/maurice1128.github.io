# Sources in Ancient and Historical Languages: Original-Language Texts, Critical Editions, Manuscripts and Language Issues

**Scope.** This file complements `primary_sources_guide.md`, which is organised by topic and points mainly to English anthologies (Horrox 1994; Aberth 2005/2017). This file is organised by **language**. For each text it gives the original-language critical edition, an English translation, an open scan or digitised manuscript where one could be confirmed, what the text shows about devotion versus institutional trust, and a note on language difficulty. Where the earlier guide already gives a row (e.g., A13 Venette), this file adds only what the earlier guide lacks (catalogue confirmation, the Latin/vernacular edition, language notes) and cross-refers to it.

**Verification legend (this session, 5 Nov 2026; web search unavailable, so all checks were made by API or direct fetch).**
- **[V-IA]** Internet Archive catalogue or metadata record opened; identifier, title and date match. Many IA items are lending-only; noted where the metadata says `access-restricted-item=true`.
- **[V-OL]** Open Library work or edition record opened; title, editor/translator, publisher and year match.
- **[V-CR]** Crossref record opened; DOI, volume, issue and pages as shown.
- **[V-LoC]** Library of Congress JSON record opened.
- **[V-200]** Page fetched with HTTP 200 and matching title.
- **[V-WB]** Site blocks automated access; a 2026 (or stated) Wayback snapshot confirms the URL.
- **[V-TXT]** IA OCR text downloaded and the passage or table of contents read.
- **[unverified]** From prior knowledge only. Do not print without checking.

Chapter and verse references to the primary texts themselves (e.g., Eusebius HE 7.22; Procopius *Wars* 2.22–23; Gregory of Tours *Hist.* 10.1) are standard scholarly references; they are not catalogue-verifiable and are marked [ref. unverified] where I am less than certain.

Language difficulty is my assessment on a 1–5 scale for someone with no prior knowledge of the language (1 = a few months of a reading course; 5 = years, plus palaeography or a non-Latin script and little reference support).

## 1. Biblical Hebrew and Aramaic

**Main point.** The Hebrew Bible supplies the grammar of collective lament that Christians, Jews and Muslims later reuse. Several of its arguments are carried by form and wording that translation flattens: the alphabetic acrostics of Lamentations, the communal "we" of the lament psalms, the ketiv/qere at Job 13:15, and the word *ʿebed* ("servant") in Isaiah 52:13–53:12.

## 2. Greek (Septuagint, New Testament, patristic and Byzantine)

**Main point.** Greek gives (a) the New Testament vocabulary that makes suffering a share in Christ (*pathēmata*, *thlipsis*), (b) the earliest Christian plague narrative (Dionysius of Alexandria in Eusebius), and (c) the Byzantine and Justinianic plague accounts that let the Black Death be compared across Latin and Orthodox Christianity.

## 3. Latin

**Main point.** Latin is the single most productive language for this project. It holds the patristic theodicies of plague and catastrophe (Cyprian, Augustine), the papal and episcopal acts of 1348–50, almost all Black Death chronicles, the ars moriendi, scholastic accounts of evil, and the colonial sacramental registers of enslaved people.

## 5. Classical Arabic

**Main point.** Arabic plague literature lets the author test whether Christian anticlericalism after 1348 depended on Latin ecclesiology. Islamic jurists framed plague as **martyrdom and mercy** for believers, and debated contagion against hadith. The institutional conflict was between physicians and jurists, not between laity and priests.
