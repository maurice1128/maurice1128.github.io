# Contemporary Evidence: Christian Belief vs. Trust in Churches (21st century), read through the historical model

**The model being tested (from the project's literature review).** Collective suffering raises the demand for meaning. Outcomes depend on three things: (1) the sufferers' power position, (2) the theology of suffering on offer, and (3) the church's role.
- **Branch A.** The church sides with power or fails the sufferers. Faith tends to persist but **migrates away from that institution** ("believing without belonging"), toward rival churches or toward unaffiliated belief.
- **Branch B.** The church is the sufferers' own community. Faith and institution grow together.

**How the notes were checked (read this first).**
- **Access date.** All web pages were opened on **2026-11-05** unless stated otherwise.
- **Bibliographic data.** Checked against the Crossref API. Abstracts were read via OpenAlex, publisher pages, or the Google Books metadata feed. The Library of Congress JSON returned 403 and Open Library connections reset, so I used neither.
- **Statistics.** Every number below comes from a page or file I opened. The source URL sits next to the number. Survey PDFs (TSCS, CIASE) and data files (fowid .xlsx, Gallup Datawrapper CSVs) were downloaded and read directly.
- **[unverified]** means I could not open a source that confirms the claim.
- **[secondary]** means the fact was taken from Wikipedia because the primary page blocked access (403, connection reset or bot check). The primary citation is given for the author to check.
- **Overlap with a sibling file.** Bottan & Perez-Truglia 2015, Hungerman 2013, Bentzen 2021 and Boguszewski et al. 2022 are verified in full in `quantitative_shocks_religiosity.md` §6. Here they are only cross-referenced, to avoid duplication.
- **Tone.** On contested issues (sexuality and marriage, abortion, race politics, COVID rules, partisan politics), these notes describe churches' positions and how those positions relate to trust and affiliation. They do not evaluate the positions.

## 1. Believing vs. belonging: theory and the core trend data

### 1.1 Theory

- **Davie, Grace. 1990.** "Believing without Belonging: Is This the Future of Religion in Britain?" *Social Compass* 37 (4): 455–69. https://doi.org/10.1177/003776890037004004.
  - The abstract (French résumé via OpenAlex) treats belonging and belief as "two distinct but linked indicators." It finds that late-20th-century Britain shows "a clear imbalance between the fall in belonging and the much slower fall in belief."
- **Davie, Grace. 1994.** *Religion in Britain since 1945: Believing without Belonging.* Oxford: Blackwell. 244 pp. ISBN 9780631184447 (Google Books feed).
- **Davie, Grace. 2015.** *Religion in Britain: A Persistent Paradox.* 2nd ed. Chichester: Wiley-Blackwell. https://doi.org/10.1002/9781394260249.
  - The publisher description says it is "based on … Religion in Britain Since 1945 … but extensively revised with the majority of the text re-written." It examines the paradox that Britain has become "increasingly secular" while religion is "increasingly present in public debate."
  - Chapter 4, "Cultural Heritage, Believing without Belonging and Vicarious Religion," pp. 71–90 (Crossref).
- **Davie, Grace. 2007.** "Vicarious Religion: A Methodological Challenge." In *Everyday Religion*, edited by Nancy T. Ammerman, 21–36. New York: Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195305418.003.0001.
  - Editor named from general knowledge [editor unverified this session].
  - Relevance: vicarious religion means a minority practises on behalf of a majority that approves. This sits close to the model's idea of an institution that keeps residual legitimacy without participation.
- **Critique: Voas, David, and Alasdair Crockett. 2005.** "Religion in Britain: Neither Believing nor Belonging." *Sociology* 39 (1): 11–28. https://doi.org/10.1177/0038038505048998.
  - BHPS and BSA data show "belief has in fact eroded in Britain at the same rate as two key aspects of belonging." Decline is generational. Only "about half of parental religiosity is successfully transmitted, while absence of religion is almost always passed on."
  - **Implication for the model.** Believing without belonging may be a *stage*: belief outlives belonging by about one generation. It is not a stable equilibrium.
  - **[WF: medium, 3-0]** The verified run confirms this reading independently. Its verifier adds supporting work by Molteni & Biolcati Rinaldi (*Social Forces*, 2022/23), which attributes European attendance decline to cohort replacement **[not opened by me]**. Counter-positions it lists: Callum Brown's 1960s period shock; the general problem of separating age, period and cohort effects; and the disputed 2025 Bible Society "Quiet Revival" report (compare Pew, January 2026, in §1.2).
- **Voas, David, and Mark Chaves. 2016.** "Is the United States a Counterexample to the Secularization Thesis?" *American Journal of Sociology* 121 (5): 1517–56. https://doi.org/10.1086/684202.
  - "American religiosity has been declining for decades," produced by "the generational patterns underlying religious decline elsewhere in the West: each successive cohort is less religious than the preceding one. America is not an exception."
  - The debate that followed:
    - Schnabel, Landon, and Sean Bock. 2017. "The Persistent and Exceptional Intensity of American Religion: A Response to Recent Research." *Sociological Science* 4: 686–700. https://doi.org/10.15195/v4.a28. They argue "only moderate religion is on the decline."
    - Voas, David, and Mark Chaves. 2018. "Even Intense Religiosity Is Declining in the United States: Comment." *Sociological Science* 5: 694–710. https://doi.org/10.15195/v5.a29. They reply that "even the intensely religious segment … is indeed shrinking."
- **Taylor, Charles. 2007.** *A Secular Age.* Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press. https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674044289.
  - The book asks "what happens when a society in which it is virtually impossible not to believe in God becomes one in which faith, even for the staunchest believer, is only one human possibility among others" (publisher description via Google Books).
  - Belknap imprint from general knowledge [unverified].
  - Used in §8 as a major reason the historical model fails for the present.

### 1.2 United States: belief vs. belonging, with numbers

**Pew Research Center, 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study (RLS).** Released 2025-02-26. N = 36,908; fieldwork 17 July 2023 to 4 March 2024. — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/decline-of-christianity-in-the-us-has-slowed-may-have-leveled-off/
- **Affiliation.**
  - 62% Christian. That is −9 points since 2014 and −16 since 2007. The share has hovered between 60% and 64% since 2019.
  - 29% unaffiliated, up from 23% (2014) and 16% (2007).
- **Belief vs. belonging.**
  - 86% believe in a soul or spirit; 83% in God or a universal spirit; 79% in "something spiritual beyond the natural world"; 70% in an afterlife.
  - But only 33% attend religious services at least monthly, and 44% pray daily.
  - This is the textbook believing-without-belonging profile.
- **Political sorting.** Among self-described liberals, Christian identity fell from 62% (2007) to 37%, and "nones" rose from 27% to 51%. "There are now more religious 'nones' than Christians among liberals." Change among conservatives was "much less pronounced."
- **Cohorts.**
  - Ages 18–24 vs. 74+: Christian identity 46% vs. 80%; pray daily 27% vs. 58%; attend monthly 25% vs. 49%.
  - Among 18–24-year-olds raised in highly religious homes, only 28% are highly religious now. Among the same age group raised non-religious, 76% remain non-religious.
- **Childhood vs. now.** 40% attend less often than as children; 5% attend more often.
- **Congregational diversity.** About one-third of attenders are in a congregation where they are not part of the racial or ethnic majority.

## 2. Churches' stances on social and political issues, and disaffiliation

### 2.1 The politics-to-religion causal arrow (US)

- **Hout, Michael, and Claude S. Fischer. 2002.** "Why More Americans Have No Religious Preference: Politics and Generations." *American Sociological Review* 67 (2): 165–90. https://doi.org/10.1177/000312240206700201.
  - Nones doubled from 7% to 14% in the 1990s.
  - "Most people with no preference hold conventional religious beliefs, despite their alienation from organized religion. In fact, these 'unchurched believers' made up most of the increase."
  - The increase was "confined to political moderates and liberals … a symbolic statement against the Religious Right."
  - **This is the founding empirical statement of the model's (a″) branch for the US.**
- **Hout, Michael, and Claude S. Fischer. 2014.** "Explaining Why More Americans Have No Religious Preference: Political Backlash and Generational Succession, 1987–2012." *Sociological Science* 1: 423–47. https://doi.org/10.15195/v1.a24.
  - Nones were 20% in 2012, vs. 7% in 1987.
  - GSS panel data show "the causal direction … likely runs from political identity as a liberal or conservative to religious identity."
  - Values of personal autonomy, including "in the sphere of sex and drugs," explain the generational differences.
  - Abstract read on the journal page: https://www.sociologicalscience.com/articles-vol1-24-423/
- **Djupe, Paul A., Jacob R. Neiheisel, and Kimberly H. Conger. 2018.** "Are the Politics of the Christian Right Linked to State Rates of the Nonreligious? The Importance of Salient Controversy." *Political Research Quarterly* 71 (4): 910–22. https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912918771526.
  - Uses expert reports and interest-group counts measuring the Christian Right's state-level prominence (2000–2010), plus the census of the unchurched and CCES estimates of nones.
  - Findings: "Rising none rates are more common in Republican states in this period. Moreover, when the Christian Right comes into more public conflict, such as over same-sex marriage bans, the rate of religious nones climbs."
- **Campbell, David E., Geoffrey C. Layman, and John C. Green. 2021.** *Secular Surge: A New Fault Line in American Politics.* Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108923347. Crossref online date 3 December 2020.
  - "Secular identity is in part a reaction to the Religious Right." There "may not yet be a Secular Left to counterbalance the Religious Right."
- **Margolis, Michele F. 2018.** *From Politics to the Pews: How Partisanship and the Political Environment Shape Religious Identity.* Chicago: University of Chicago Press. https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226555812.001.0001.
  - Crossref-verified. No abstract was available; the thesis as stated in the title.
- **Whitehead, Andrew L., and Samuel L. Perry. 2020.** *Taking America Back for God: Christian Nationalism in the United States.* New York: Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190057886.001.0001.
  - Christian nationalism "fights to preserve a particular kind of social order."
  - It is analytically distinct from religiosity. This matters for §4: religiosity and Christian nationalism predicted opposite COVID behaviours.
- **Martí, Gerardo. 2020.** *American Blindspot: Race, Class, Religion, and the Trump Presidency.* Lanham, MD: Rowman & Littlefield. https://doi.org/10.5040/9798216455820.

### 2.2 Why people say they leave (survey self-reports)

**PRRI. 2024. "Religious Change in America."** Published 27 March 2024; 2023 survey of more than 5,600 adults. — https://www.prri.org/research/religious-change-in-america/

Overall picture:
- 26% unaffiliated in 2023, vs. 21% in 2013.
- 18% of Americans left a religion to become unaffiliated; 35% of these were formerly Catholic and 35% mainline.
- Atheists rose from 2% to 4% and agnostics from 2% to 5% (2013 vs. 2023). "Nothing in particular" stayed at 16–17%.

| Reason | 2016 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|

| Family never that religious | 32% | 41% |

## 3. Abuse crises and institutional trust

### 3.1 Causal evidence (cross-reference)

- **Bottan & Perez-Truglia 2015** (*Journal of Public Economics* 129: 106–19). US Catholic abuse scandals caused a "significant and long-lasting decline in religious participation" and in giving, but no significant decline in religious beliefs. → See `quantitative_shocks_religiosity.md` §6.

### 5.2 Black churches as the sufferers' own institution

**Pew Research Center. 2021. "Faith Among Black Americans."** 16 February 2021. N = 8,660; fieldwork 19 November 2019 to 3 June 2020, so mostly before George Floyd's killing on 25 May 2020. — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/02/16/faith-among-black-americans/

- **Congregations.** **60%** of Black attenders worship where most attendees and clergy are Black; 25% in multiracial congregations; 13% in predominantly white or other-race congregations.
- **Faith and racism.** Most Black adults "view opposing racism as essential to their religious faith."
- **Credit for equality.** About three-quarters credit Black churches with at least "some" role in moving Black people toward equality; three in ten say "a great deal."
- **Unaffiliated Black Americans:**
  - 21% are unaffiliated: Gen Z 28%, Millennials 33%, Silent Generation 5%.
  - **Black "nones" are 90% believers in God or a higher power**, vs. 72% of all US "nones."
  - 66% of Black nones credit Black churches with helping equality.
  - **Model reading:** where the church is the sufferers' own institution, even those who leave keep belief *and* goodwill toward it. Contrast the 43% "more harm than good" among nones generally (§1.2).
- **Generational erosion.** About half of Black Gen Z attenders are in Black congregations, vs. two-thirds of Boomers and the Silent Generation. 47% say Black churches are less influential than 50 years ago.
- **What Black Americans want from churches [WF: medium, 3-0; I spot-checked the 60/29 figures].** Pew 2021, chapter "Churches and Religion in Black American Life," https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/02/16/churches-and-religion-in-black-american-life/
  - Essential in a house of worship: spiritual comfort 72%; community or fellowship 71%; help with bills, housing or food 55%; sermons on political topics 25%.
  - Credit for equality, "a great deal": civil rights organizations 60%, Black churches 29%. Asked which institution did the *most*: civil rights groups 74%, churches 10% (forced choice).
  - **Refinement of Branch B:** Black churches retain members as communities of care and spiritual comfort, not mainly as political agents. Their legitimacy rests on serving sufferers *directly*. Advocacy is credited more to secular organizations.
- **Partisanship within Black worship.** 43% of Black Republican attenders go to Black congregations vs. 64% of Black Democrats; 22% of Black Republicans vs. 11% of Black Democrats attend mostly-white congregations.

**Pew Research Center (Data Labs). 2021. "Pastors Often Discussed Election, Pandemic and Racism in Fall of 2020."** 8 July 2021. Machine analysis of sermons posted online, 31 August to 8 November 2020. — https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2021/07/08/pastors-often-discussed-election-pandemic-and-racism-in-fall-of-2020/
- **Racism.** 44% of congregations heard at least one reference to racism; Catholic congregations only 32%.
- **The election:**
  - 67% of congregations heard it mentioned: Catholic 41%, mainline and historically Black Protestant 63% each, evangelical 71%.
  - Of evangelical election sermons, 48% discussed specific issues, parties or candidates.
  - **43% of historically Black Protestant election sermons encouraged voting**, "roughly double" any other group, with vocabulary of "suppress[ion]" and "register [to] vote."
- **Neutral reading.** Traditions differed in *what* they addressed: voting rights and racism vs. issues and candidates. This matches each tradition's constituency's experience.

**Black exit from white or multiracial evangelical churches after 2016–2020.**
- The scholarly basis is Edwards 2008, Edwards & Kim 2019/2024 and Barron 2016 (mechanisms).
- Direct quantitative evidence of an "exodus" was **[unverified]**: I located no peer-reviewed panel measurement.
- A frequently cited journalistic source: Campbell Robertson, "A Quiet Exodus: Why Black Worshipers Are Leaving White Evangelical Churches," *New York Times*, 9 March 2018 **[unverified, not opened]**.
- Jemar Tisby's *The Color of Compromise* (2019) is a popular history **[not verified this session]**.

**Trend contrast (Gallup, 2026-03-03):** "religion very important" among Black adults fell 22 points to 63% between 2001–05 and 2021–25. So the Branch-B community is also secularizing, though from a much higher base.

## 8. Synthesis: reading today's church through the historical model

### 8.1 (a) Belief persists while institutional trust falls

- **The pattern is the modal 21st-century Western pattern:**
  - US: 83% believe in God or a universal spirit vs. 33% monthly attendance. Confidence in organized religion fell from 68% to 31–36%. Clergy honesty fell from 67% to 27%.
  - Most US nones believe in a higher power. 47% cite dislike of religious organizations.
  - Western Europe's non-practising Christian majority.
  - Germany's ex-Catholics: 58% "can believe without the church."
  - Taiwan: a stable Christian share alongside halving high trust in religious organizations.
- **The modern trigger most like the Black Death's "institution failed to protect" is the abuse crisis.**
  - It hits trust in the hierarchy hardest. In 2019, 59% of US Catholics trusted their own priests, but only about one-third rated the US bishops' response positively.
  - It causes exit with little change in belief (Bottan & Perez-Truglia).
  - Germany's exit series and France's CIASE language ("complete, even cruel, indifference") are the clearest institutional-failure cases.
- **Caveat.** Belief also declines across cohorts (Voas & Crockett; Voas & Chaves; Pew's finding that religious upbringing is less "sticky" among the young). Believing without belonging is better modelled as **a lagged decay** (belonging falls first, belief a generation later) than as a stable state.

### 8.2 (b) Institution and faith grow together where the church is the sufferers' own community

- **US Black Protestantism:**
  - Highest retention (82%).
  - Opposing racism is seen as essential to faith.
  - Black nones retain belief (90%) and goodwill (66% credit Black churches).
  - Net positive spiritual impact of COVID among Black Protestants.
  - In 2020, sermons focused on voting and voter-rights language.
- **Global South growth** is consistent with Branch B, but Pew attributes it largely to fertility, which confounds it.
- **China.** Persecution-era Christianity is hard to measure: 2% identify, up to 8% have some connection.
- **Taiwan's PCT 1971–1990.** Explicit identification with "台灣住民" against the party-state, plus disaster ministry in indigenous areas. Yet the PCT's share of the population does not visibly surge in TSCS: total Protestants are 4–5.6%. *Branch B produces loyalty and legitimacy, not necessarily numerical growth, in a pluralist society.*
- **Erosion even here.** Black Gen Z and Millennials are 28–33% unaffiliated. "Religion very important" among Black adults fell 22 points. Branch B slows decline; it does not suspend generational secularization.

### 8.3 (c) Outright disaffiliation

- Rising atheist and agnostic shares (PRRI); 67% of nones "stopped believing"; Taiwan's "no religion" at 18.4% in 2023; UK Christian identity at 44% (LFS 2025).
- Driven chiefly by **generational replacement and loss of transmission**, not by discrete suffering shocks.

### 8.4 A distinctive modern variant: politicized alignment and partisan sorting

- The historical model's "church sides with power" maps onto **partisan alignment** today:
  - The US Christian Right (Hout & Fischer; Djupe et al.; Campbell et al.).
  - Korean Protestantism and the martial-law episode (CEMK 2026).
  - Taiwanese conservative mobilization in 2013–2018 (Huang 2017).
- **Exit is asymmetric.**
  - Those outside the coalition leave: liberal Christian identity fell 25 points (Pew), and confidence in organized religion is 21% among Democrats.
  - Those inside may intensify: Republicans 64%; white evangelical retention 66% → 76%; young men's rise "concentrated among young Republicans" (Gallup 2026).
- The outcome is **polarization, not uniform decline**. The same pattern appears in Polish COVID data (Boguszewski et al.) and in the SBC: shrinking membership, a growing active core.

### 8.5 Where the historical model fails or needs amendment for the present

1. **Exit costs are low.** Medieval or enslaved Christians could not easily leave Christendom. Leaving now costs little; in Germany it even saves church tax (63% of ex-EKD cite this). Exit therefore registers immediately in membership statistics, rather than as heterodoxy inside the church.
2. **Pluralism and "secular alternatives."** Taylor's "faith … only one human possibility among others." Meaning-making demand after suffering can be met by therapy, politics, nation or secular humanism. In the US, 32% of nones cite *mental health* as a reason for leaving (PRRI). Most Americans (69%) said COVID had *no* effect on their religious life (Pew 2025), which is inconsistent with a strong suffering-drives-religiosity effect in a pluralist, high-security society.
3. **The "suffering" is often institution-inflicted, not exogenous.** Abuse scandals are harms caused *by* the church. This is closer to the model's "church fails the sufferers" than to plague, but the meaning-demand mechanism (theodicy) is not engaged. Victims seek justice, not theodicy.
4. **Politics, not suffering, is the main proximate driver of today's institutional exit** in the US and Korea. The model needs a "political sorting" pathway alongside the "suffering → meaning" pathway.
5. **The demographic engine of global growth is fertility** (Pew 2025), so global-South growth cannot be read as suffering-induced faith without decomposition.
6. **Measurement under persecution** (China) and opt-in polling artefacts (the UK "Quiet Revival"; Pew January 2026 found the BSA share of monthly-churchgoing Christians at 12% in 2018 and 9% in 2024, with no revival) mean "revival under suffering" claims need probability samples.
7. **Rival institutions inside the same tradition recover.** In Korea, Catholic trust rose while Protestant trust fell. In the US, Hungerman's substitution. Branch A predicts *migration*. The modern market makes migration cheap and visible. This is consistent with the model, and a refinement of it.
8. **But scandal can also delegitimize "the church" generically.** In Germany, Catholic scandals raised exits from the *uninvolved* Protestant church (Frick, Moser & Simmons 2021) [WF: high, 3-0]. Whether failure produces migration to a rival (US, Korea) or generalized exit (Germany) seems to depend on how far the public sees the churches as one establishment. In Germany both large churches are linked to the state through the church tax, so they read as a single institutional bloc. Medieval Christendom had the same single-church structure, which makes Germany the closer analogue to the Black Death case.
