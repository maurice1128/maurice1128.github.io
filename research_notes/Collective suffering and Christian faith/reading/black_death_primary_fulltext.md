# Black Death primary sources: full-text reading notes

Reader's notes for the project "Collective suffering and Christian faith" (brief: `_READING_BRIEF.md`). Read November 2026.

**Method.** I downloaded every text below as an archive.org `_djvu.txt` OCR file, a Project Gutenberg plain-text file, or an HTML page. Copies are in the session scratchpad (`dl_blackdeath/`). Quotations are copied from those files.

- **Latin spelling.** Older Latin editions print the long s, which the OCR reads as "f" (e.g. `peftilentia`). Where the OCR is garbled, I quote it verbatim and give my reading in [square brackets].
- **Page numbers.** These are the printed page numbers of the edition, taken from the running heads in the OCR. "p. 555–56" means the passage runs across the page break.
- **Notes.** In each entry, "Text" is what the source says. "Interpretation" is my reading for the model.

Model shorthand used below:

- **POW**: power position
- **THEO**: theology of suffering
- **REM**: who controls the remedy
- **CH**: church role (own community, failing establishment, or ally of perpetrators), including its capacity to deliver care
- **REC**: duration and recurrence
- **TRANS**: transmission between generations
- **Rival explanations**: DELIV (timing of deliverance), PROV (provision and relief), POL (political alignment)

## 1. Ralph of Shrewsbury, Bishop of Bath and Wells: register entries 1348–1353

### *Register of Ralph of Shrewsbury*, vol. 2 (1348–53): Holmes 1896, Somerset Record Society 10

- **Edition.** Holmes, Thomas Scott, ed. 1896. *The Register of Ralph of Shrewsbury, Bishop of Bath and Wells, 1329–1363*. Vol. 2. Somerset Record Society 10. [London]: Printed for subscribers.
- **URLs.** https://archive.org/download/registerralphsh00holmgoog/registerralphsh00holmgoog_djvu.txt (the IA item is vol. **X**, entries 1601 onward, 1341–63).
- **Latin text of no. 2130.** Wilkins, David. 1737. *Concilia Magnae Britanniae et Hiberniae*. Vol. 2. London. Pp. 745–46. https://archive.org/download/conciliamagnaebr02wilk/conciliamagnaebr02wilk_djvu.txt
- **Read:**
  - all entries 2041–2160 (Aug 1348–Jan 1349);
  - nos. 2297, 2300–2305 and 2321–2326 (1349–50);
  - the Keynsham visitation (pp. 708–09) and no. 2788 (1353);
  - Holmes's notes to nos. 2043–2486 (pp. 807–11);
  - a crude count of institution entries across vol. 10.
- **Length read:** about 60 printed pages of about 800.

**Key finding on no. 2130.** Holmes does **not** print the text of the famous mandate. He gives a one-line calendar entry: "2130. Mandate of Bishop Ralph concerning confessions during the time of the plague. Dated at Wyvelescombe, iv Id., Jan., A.D. 1348. [Printed in Wilkins's 'Concilia,' vol. 2, p. 745.]" (p. 571–72). He then quotes three Latin phrases in his Notes (p. 808). The full Latin is in Wilkins 1737, vol. 2, pp. 745–46, "Ex reg. Wellen. fol. 333", which I read in full.

**Date.** *iv Id. Jan. 1348* (Old Style, with the year beginning on Lady Day, 25 March) is **10 January 1349**. Gasquet (1893, 81) gives "January 17th, 1349", and the project guides copy that date; it should be corrected to 10 Jan 1349. Wilkins prints the place as "Wynchelcomb", an error for Wiveliscombe; Holmes reads "Wyvelescombe".

**Summary.**

- **First warning (no. 2043, 17 Aug 1348).** The register opens the crisis with a mandate dated xvi Kal. Sept. 1348, when the plague had reached France. It orders processions and stations every Friday. It frames the plague as God's scourge that penitence can turn aside, citing Nineveh and Pharaoh.
- **Rush of institutions.** The register then fills with institutions to vacant benefices, densest from January to May 1349.
- **Lay confession (no. 2130, 10 Jan 1349).** The mandate states that priests cannot be found "for zeal or for any stipend". It authorises the dying to confess to a layman, "or if a layman is lacking, to a woman". It says a deacon may give the Eucharist, and that where there is no priest for extreme unction, "faith must suffice".
- **Royal and ecclesiastical responses.** Later entries record the king prorogueing Parliament because of the plague (no. 2147) and the Ordinance of Labourers (no. 2297, 18 June 1349).
- **Yeovil riot (nos. 2301–05, Nov–Dec 1349).** The "community" of Yeovil attacked the bishop during his visitation and imprisoned him. He laid the town under interdict, then suspended the interdict so that the "devotion of the beloved of God" would not weaken.
- **Afterwards.** A 1353 union of benefices (no. 2788) records that the plague of "a few years past" so reduced the population that two parishes cannot each have their own priest.

**Key passages.**

> "Omnipotens Deus de cujus trono precedunt tronitrua et fulgura aliaque flagella quibus filios quos recipit flagellare disponit. &c, Unde quia clades pestilcnciatis hujusmodi a plaga orientali ad vicina regna pervcnit timendum est valde … ac devote et incessanter orandum" [OCR: *precedunt*, *pestilencie*, *pervenit*]

- Locator: no. 2043, 17 Aug 1348, Holmes 1896, 555.
- Why it matters: THEO. The plague is God's rod for the sons he receives, to be pre-empted by penance.

> "Processiones ac staciones in singulis collegiatis, regularibus, et parochialibus ecclesiis saltem singulis sextis feriis fieri faciatis in quibus popuUim inducatis, quod … de peccatis suis contereantur et peniteant" [*populum*]

- Locator: no. 2043, Holmes 1896, 555.
- Why it matters: REM. The institution controls the first remedy, which is liturgical.

> "Radulphus Contagium peftilentiae moderni temporis undique fe dilatans, multas ecclefias parochiales, et alias curas, ipfarumque populum noftrae dioec. abfque curatore et facerdote defolatas reliquit, et cum non inveniantur presbyteri, qui velint devotionis zelo, aut pro ftipendio aliquo curas praedidtorum locorum fubire, et infirmos viiitare, eifque facramenta ecclefiaftica miniftrare ; forfitan propter infectionem, et contagionis horrorem, multi abfque poenitentiae facramento, ut accepimus, moriuntur"

- Reading: [*pestilentiae … se … ipsarumque … nostrae … absque … sacerdote desolatas … stipendio … praedictorum … subire … visitare, eisque sacramenta ecclesiastica ministrare*].
- Locator: no. 2130, Wilkins 1737, 2:745.
- Why it matters: CH/REM. The institution records in its own register that it cannot supply its core service. It gives the reason as "fear of infection", not death alone.

> "quod in mortis articulo, fi presbyterum rite ordinatum habere non valeant, ullo modo confiteantur juxta doctrinam apoftoli alterurri fua peccaca etiam laico, et fi defit vir laicus, mulieri"

- Reading: [*si … alterutri sua peccata … si desit vir laicus, mulieri*].
- Locator: no. 2130, Wilkins 1737, 2:745.
- Why it matters: REM. The sacramental remedy is formally passed to the laity, women included.

> "facramentum vero euchariftiae in abfentia presbyteri poterit per diaconum miniftrari. Si vero non fit presbyter, qui facramentum unctionis extremae miniftret, debet, ficut in aliis, fides fufficere facramenti."

- Locator: no. 2130, Wilkins 1737, 2:746.
- Why it matters: THEO/REM. Faith replaces the sacrament where the institution is absent.

> "Denuncietis praeterea omnibus … laicis fua peccata confitentibus, quod fi convalefcant, eadem peccata confiteantur iterum, ut tenentur, fuo proprio facerdoti"

- Locator: no. 2130, Wilkins 1737, 2:746.
- Why it matters: REM. The concession is **temporary and reclaimed**. Survivors must re-confess to their own priest. The institution delegates the remedy without giving up control of it.

> "Quia tamen subit plaga pestilencie mortalis et loco predicto et aliis partibus circumvicinis adeo indies invalescit, quod de securo accessu hominum ad locum ilium formidatur admodum"

- Locator: no. 2147, royal writ of 1 Jan 1349 proroguing Parliament, Holmes 1896, 573–74.
- Why it matters: shows the scale of the crisis.

> "certain sons of perdition, forming the community of the said town, having assembled in a numerous multitude with bows, arrows, iron bars, stones and other kinds of arms, fiercely wounded very many of our servants of God … they entered into the said church with great strife and shut us and our servants in the said church until the darkness of the night … And afterwards they incarcerated us and our servants in the rectory"

- Locator: no. 2301, Yeovil, Sunday before St Martin [Nov] 1349; Holmes's English calendar, 596.
- Why it matters: CH/POW. Lay violence against the bishop in the plague year. The cause is not stated (see the source-critical notes).

> "Lest the instruments of divine praise there be suspended, the teaching of Christ diminished and the devotion of the beloved of God be weakened, we suspend the said interdict until the Epiphany"

- Locator: no. 2303, 5 Dec 1349, Holmes 1896, 597.
- Why it matters: the institution needs the community's devotion and backs down.

> "The cemetery of the parish church of Jevele is polluted by the violent effusion of human blood that dead bodies cannot be buried before reconciliation … We, nevertheless, wishing to mitigate this rigour, order you that … you receive and admit them to burial"

- Locator: no. 2304, Holmes 1896, 597.
- Why it matters: care (burial) is kept up during sanction.

> "exigentibusque peccatis, hominum multitudo populi per pestilenciam paucis annis preteritis humane nature insidiantcm in predictis locis quamplurimum decreuit, dicteque ecclesie de Coriryvel et Erneshull … sicque duos, sicut olim, non debeant aut poterint habere proprios sacerdotes"

- Locator: no. 2788, 1353, Holmes 1896, 731.
- Why it matters: REC/CH. A lasting contraction of pastoral provision, still blamed on sin.

> Holmes's note: "These conditional appointments afford evidence of the uncertainty caused by the Black Death."

- Locator: note to no. 2255, Holmes 1896, 809.

**Crude count (my tally).** I counted entries in vol. 10 that contain "institut-", "admitt-" or "conferred", in blocks of 50 entry numbers.

| Entry block | Dates covered | Count per 50 entries |
|---|---|---|
| Nos. 1600–2050 | Mar 1341–Aug 1348 | 8–27 |
| Nos. 2150–2299 | Jan–Jun 1349 | 39–40 |

On these counts, entries 2100 (4 Jan 1349) to 2350 (22 May 1349) hold roughly as many institutions in about 4.5 months (≈160) as nos. 1601–2042 hold in about 7.5 years (≈155). This is a regex count on OCR, not a verified series. Use Holmes's printed text or Gasquet's monthly table (Gasquet 1893, 83–84) before citing any figure.

**Evidence for and against the model.**

- **CH (failing establishment), for.** The bishop admits in writing that no priests will serve, "zeal" or "stipend" notwithstanding.
- **REM, mixed.** The bishop himself transfers confession to the laity, including women. But he does so as an act of episcopal authority and requires re-confession on recovery. The institution delegates the remedy but does not lose it.
- **THEO.** Plague is consistently a divine scourge that penance can turn aside (no. 2043). The Canterbury and Islip letters in Wilkins take the same line (§2).
- **POW/CH.** The Yeovil riot (Nov 1349) shows lay hostility to the bishop. Holmes's calendar gives no plague-related motive, so it should not be read as a plague effect without further evidence. The cemetery order shows burial care continuing.
- **REC.** No. 2788 (1353) records a lasting fall in the number of parish priests.
- **PROV.** Bishop and king respond on different tracks: the bishop with sacraments and processions, the king with wage control (no. 2297).

**Source-critical notes.** The register is institutional self-documentation: it is not polemic, but it records what the bishop chose to enregister. Holmes gives English calendars for most entries and prints Latin only selectively. Several famous texts (including no. 2130) are only referred to Wilkins. Dates follow the year beginning on 25 March: "Feb 1349" in the register is February 1350 New Style.

**Usable in:** Article 2 (core); Article 1.

## 2. Episcopal and archiepiscopal plague letters: Wilkins, *Concilia* (1737) and Raine, *Northern Registers* (1873)

### Canterbury chapter (1348), Ralph's mandate (1349), and Islip's mandates (1349, 1350, 1362)

- **Edition.** Wilkins, David. 1737. *Concilia Magnae Britanniae et Hiberniae*. 4 vols. London. Vols. 2–3.
- **URLs.** https://archive.org/download/conciliamagnaebr02wilk/conciliamagnaebr02wilk_djvu.txt and https://archive.org/download/conciliamagnaebr03wilk/conciliamagnaebr03wilk_djvu.txt
- **Read** (all in full):
  - letter of the prior and chapter of Canterbury, *sede vacante*, 1348 (2:738–39);
  - Ralph's mandate (2:745–46);
  - Islip's mandate for processions celebrating the Calais victory "with remembrance of the pestilence" (2:752);
  - Islip, *Effrenata generis humani cupiditas*, Maghfeld [Mayfield], 5 kal. Jun. [28 May] 1350 (3:1–2);
  - the 1362 Lambeth constitution reissuing *Effrenata* (3, pages not legible in OCR).

**Summary.**

- **Canterbury chapter (1348).** The chapter writes during the vacancy after Archbishop Stratford's death. The king had asked for prayers, and Stratford died before he could order them. The chapter orders masses, sermons, and processions on Wednesdays and Fridays, with indulgences, against "pestilentias".
- ***Effrenata* (1350).** Islip complains that the priests who survive think they were spared for their own merits. He says they abandon the cure of souls for better-paid chantry masses ("annualia") and demand "excessive stipends". Their avarice is "even copied by lay workers". He fixes the chaplain's salary at one mark above the previous rate.
- **1362 reissue.** The reissue after the second plague sharpens the language. The priests "spumant in libidinem", "in virorum ecclesiasticorum detestabile scandalum, et exemplum perniciosum laicorum".

**Key passages.**

> "Terribilis fuper filios hominum Deus … quos diligit, arguit et caftigat, variifque flagitiorum generibus quandoque punit temporaliter, ut eos aeternaliter non condemnet, ac languores, peftilentias, et famis miferias … peccatis hominum exigentibus eos terreat et affligat"

- Locator: Canterbury chapter letter, 1348, Wilkins 1737, 2:738.
- Why it matters: THEO. Temporal punishment is mercy, sparing people eternal punishment.

> "Dum ad memoriam reducimus admirandam peftilentiam, quae nuper partes iftas fubito fic invafir, ut nobis multo meliores et digniores fubtraxerat ; nos, qui refidui fumus, quamvis immeritos … refervando"

- Locator: Islip, 1349, Wilkins 1737, 2:752.
- Why it matters: THEO/TRANS. Survivor guilt is turned into liturgy that combines thanks for the Calais victory with remembrance of the plague. POL: the plague is folded into royal victory celebrations.

> "facerdotes, qui jam fuperfunt, non confiderantes, quod … propter populum Dei et utilitatem publicam, non propter fua merita, difpofitione divina, a periculis peftilentiae praeteritae praefervantur, nec erubefcentes quod eorum infatiabilis avaritia ab aliis operariis etiam laicis nequiter et perniciofe trahitur in exemplum ; de animarum cura … non curant"

- Locator: Islip, *Effrenata*, 1350, Wilkins 1737, 3:1.
- Why it matters: CH. The hierarchy itself states that surviving clergy are failing the people, and that their greed sets a bad example to lay workers.

> "ad celebrand. annualia, et ad alia peculiaria fe conferunt obfequia … pro eorum fervitiis ftipendia exigunt exceffiva"

- Locator: Islip, *Effrenata*, 1350, Wilkins 1737, 3:1.
- Why it matters: REM/CH. Clergy move to privately paid intercession (lay-funded chantries) and away from parish cure.

> "facerdotefque praedicti fic delicati et cupidi falariis exceffivis hujufmodi farcinati evomunt … in virorum ecclefiafticorum deteftabile fcandalum, et exemplum perniciofum laicorum"

- Locator: Lambeth constitution 1362, Wilkins 1737, vol. 3 (page not legible in OCR).
- Why it matters: REC. After the second plague the hierarchy's language about its own clergy is harsher.

### Archbishop William Zouche of York, 1348–49 (Raine 1873)

- **Edition.** Raine, James, ed. 1873. *Historical Papers and Letters from the Northern Registers*. Rolls Series 61. London: Longman. Nos. CCXLVIII–CCLI, pp. 395–401.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/historicalpapers00rain/historicalpapers00rain_djvu.txt
- **Read:** the four plague documents in full.

**Summary.**

- **28 July 1348 (Cawood).** Zouche orders processions every Wednesday and Friday with litanies, and a daily collect "pro pestilentia". He gives 40 days' indulgence. The cause is "hominum peccata".
- **Spring 1349.** A papal grant (dated X kal. Apr., forwarded on 28 Apr 1349) lets each person choose a confessor for plenary remission once *in articulo mortis*.
- **12 Oct 1349.** A papal dispensation allows four extra ordinations within a year. The reason given is "presbyteri, qui sufficere possint ad curam … ac ministrandum ecclesiastica sacramenta, nequeunt inveniri".

**Key passages.**

> "Quantae siquidem mortalitates, pestilentiae, et aeris infectio in diversis mundi partibus … immineant … et hoc quippe hominum peccata efficiunt, qui, arridentes prosperis, summi Largitoris beneficia negligunt reminisci"

- Locator: Zouche, 28 July 1348, Raine 1873, 396. Lightly normalised from OCR "Quant?e si- quidem … negn- gunt".
- Why it matters: THEO. Prosperity bred ingratitude, and plague follows.

> "propter mortalitatis pestem, quae in provincia tua imminet de praesenti, presbyteri, qui sufficere possint ad curam et regimen hujusmodi, ac ministrandum ecclesiastica sacramenta, nequeunt inveniri"

- Locator: Clement VI to Zouche, Avignon, 12 Oct 1349, Raine 1873, 401.
- Why it matters: CH. The capacity crisis is confirmed by the Pope's own letter, and the answer is faster ordination.

**For/against.** THEO and the institutional control of REM appear before the plague arrived. Episcopal and papal answers to the capacity crisis follow two lines. Sacramental power is widened (any confessor, plenary remission, lay confession), and new clergy are produced faster. *Effrenata* is the hierarchy's own indictment of clerical greed, so the "greedy priest" theme is not only mendicant or Lollard polemic.

**Usable in:** Article 2; Article 1.

## 3. Henry Knighton, *Chronicon*, 1348–49 and 1361

- **Edition.** Knighton, Henry. 1895. *Chronicon Henrici Knighton*. Vol. 2. Edited by Joseph Rawson Lumby. Rolls Series 92. London: HMSO.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/chroniconhenrici02knig/chroniconhenrici02knig_djvu.txt
- **Read:** pp. 56–64 (1348–49) in full; p. 116 (1361); Lumby's introduction on 1350 (p. lxv).

**Summary.**

- **Spread and mortality.** Knighton (an Augustinian canon of Leicester, writing c. 1390s) traces the plague from India to "Saracens", Christians and Jews. He counts dead friars at Avignon and in Provence and adds sardonic asides about other orders. He gives Leicester parish death counts.
- **Church responses.** He records two measures. The Bishop of Lincoln gave "all priests, regular and secular" full episcopal power to absolve. The Pope granted plenary remission *in periculo mortis* and free choice of confessor.
- **Economy.** He describes cheap livestock, unharvested crops, and high wages.
- **The Scots.** They mocked "the foul deth of Engelond", then were struck themselves.
- **Clergy.** Archbishop Bradwardine died within days of his consecration. He then gives his account of the dearth of priests, inflated stipends, and the flood of widowed, barely literate ordinands.
- **1361.** He records the "pestis secunda", which struck "maxime juvenes et infantes" and killed 11 canons of his own house.

**Key passages.**

> "Tunc episcopus Lyncolniensis misit per totum episcopatum, et dedit generalem potestatem omnibus et singulis sacerdotibus tam regularibus quam secularibus audire confessiones, et quosque absolvere plena auctoritate et integra episcopali excepto solo casu debiti"

- Locator: s.a. 1349, Knighton 1895, 61.
- Why it matters: REM. The institution widens access to the remedy inside the clergy, in parallel with Ralph's lay confession.

> "Similiter papa concessit plenam remissionem de cunctis peccatis cuilibet in periculo mortis existenti una sola vice esse absolutus … et cuilibet liceret eligere sibi confessorem ad placitum"

- Locator: Knighton 1895, 61.
- Why it matters: REM. Penitents choose their own confessor, a shift toward lay choice.

> "Scoti audientes de crudeli peste Anglorum suspicati sunt de manu dei vindici hoc eis evenisse, et sumpserunt in juramentum … Per foedam mortem Anglorum, Anglice, be the foul deth of Engelond"

- Locator: Knighton 1895, 62.
- Why it matters: THEO/POL. Plague read as God's judgement on the enemy nation.

> "Eodem tempore tanta penuria erat sacerdotum ubique, quod multae ecclesiae viduatae erant carentes divinis officiis, missis, matutinis, vesperis, sacramentis et sacramentalibus. Vix posset homo habere unum capellanum infra x. libras vel x. marcas ministrare alicui ecclesiae"

- Locator: Knighton 1895, 63. Ligatures lightly normalised; OCR "multz ecclesiz viduatee".
- Why it matters: CH. Failure of care: churches are left without sacraments.

> "vix erat in isto tempore qui acceptare vellet unam vicariam ad xx. libras aut xx. marcas ; sed infra breve confluebant ad ordines maxima multitudo quorum uxores obierant in pestilentia, de quibus multi illiterati et quasi meri laici nisi quatenus aliqualiter legere sciebant licet non intelligere"

- Locator: Knighton 1895, 63. OCR "raulti" is read as *multi*.
- Why it matters: CH/TRANS. Clerical quality declines. The new clergy are "almost mere laymen", which blurs the line between clergy and laity.

> "De fratribus praedicatoribus in regione Provinciae in Quadragesima mortui sunt ccclviij. … Apud Marsiliam de cxl. et x. minoribus non remansit solus unus qui nunciaret ceteris; bene quidem. … De Heremitis Angliae non remansit unus in Avinonia; nec cura."

- Locator: Knighton 1895, 59. OCR "exl." read as *cxl.*
- Why it matters: source-critical. Inter-order sarcasm ("bene quidem"; "nec cura") shows that clerical rivalry colours the chronicle.

> "mortalitas generalis oppressit populum quae dicebatur pestis secunda. Et moriebantur tam majores quam minores, et maxime juvenes et infantes. Et de congregatione nostra xj. canonici mortui sunt."

- Locator: s.a. 1361, Knighton 1895, 116.
- Why it matters: REC/TRANS. The second plague hit the young hardest, so it struck exactly the generation that would carry memory and practice forward.

**For/against.**

- CH, failing establishment: strong. Knighton gives both the quantity failure (empty churches) and the quality failure (illiterate ordinands).
- REM: institutional widening inside the clergy (Lincoln) and papal widening of choice.
- THEO: vengeance (the Tharsis king and the Scots).
- PROV: the labour statute shows the state, not the church, acting on material relief.

**Source-critical notes.** Knighton wrote about 40 years later, in Lollard-era Leicester. The priest-shortage passage may project later anticlerical complaint backwards (as the project guide already notes). His jibes at the friars mark him as a regular canon. The Lincoln and papal measures are corroborated by Zouche's papal grant (§2).

**Usable in:** Article 2; Article 1.

## 4. Francis Aidan Gasquet, *The Great Pestilence* (1893)

- **Edition.** Gasquet, Francis Aidan. 1893. *The Great Pestilence (A.D. 1348–9), Now Commonly Known as the Black Death*. London: Simpkin, Marshall, Hamilton, Kent. Project Gutenberg eBook 45815, with original page markers [pNNN].
- **URL.** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45815/pg45815.txt
- **Also downloaded, not separately read:** the 1908 revised edition, *The Black Death of 1348 and 1349*, https://archive.org/download/blackdeathof1908gasq/blackdeathof1908gasq_djvu.txt
- **Read:** preface (pp. xv–xviii); ch. V on Somerset (pp. 71–85); ch. X conclusions (pp. 194–219), skimmed with targeted reading on clergy. Not read: the county-by-county chapters.

**Summary.** Gasquet, a Benedictine and later cardinal, argues that the plague destroyed the late-medieval English church. He says it killed about 5,000 beneficed clergy "who fell victims to their duty", forced the ordination of unfit men, and caused "religious paralysis". After that came a devotional recovery that was "perfect Catholic" and not proto-Wycliffite. He translates Ralph's mandate in full. He uses the Bath and Wells institution lists to date the peak of mortality (December 1348 to February 1349) and to show repeated vacancies, with four parsons at Bathampton.

**Key passages.**

> "The immediate effect on the people was a religious paralysis. Instead of turning men to God the scourge turned them to despair, and this not only in England, but in all parts of Europe."

- Locator: preface, Gasquet 1893, xvii.
- Why it matters: a 19th-century "erosion" thesis. It is Gasquet's own interpretation, not the sources'.

> "these tracts are perfect Catholic in tone, spirit, and doctrine, and differ essentially from those of men inspired by the teaching of Wycliffe."

- Locator: Gasquet 1893, xviii.
- Why it matters: apologetic bias. He insists the post-plague devotional revival was orthodox.

> "it proves the depth of degradation to which the whole religious life was reduced by the terror inspired by the disease. Every bond was loosed, and every ordinary ecclesiastical regulation and provision set aside, because none could now be enforced, or, indeed, observed."

- Locator: on Ralph's letter, Gasquet 1893, 81.
- Why it matters: Gasquet reads lay confession as collapse forced by circumstance, not as a choice.

> "Singly, or in twos and threes, often four and five, once, at least, ten together, the clergy came to be instituted to cures which the disease had left without a priest."

- Locator: Gasquet 1893, 84.
- Why it matters: vivid on capacity. Check against Holmes.

> "some 5,000 beneficed clergy fell victims to their duty."

- Locator: Gasquet 1893, 203.
- Why it matters: apologetic framing (clergy as martyrs of duty). The figure is extrapolated by assuming "half the total number of benefices".

> "the empiric and enthusiast in the attempts at social reconstruction, as may be found in the works of Wycliff, could only aggravate the evil."

- Locator: Gasquet 1893, 217.
- Why it matters: anti-Lollard bias.

**For/against.** Gasquet's own synthesis supports **erosion first, then orthodox revival**. His evidence is mostly institution counts, which measure clergy deaths and the capacity of the institution, not lay belief.

**Source-critical notes.** The translations are generally faithful. I checked his Ralph translation against Wilkins and it is close. But he dates Ralph's letter to 17 Jan 1349, where Wilkins and Holmes give iv Id. Jan., which is 10 Jan. He presents clergy as dying at their posts, against Venette's "sacerdotes timidi recedebant" (§7). He is defensive about pre-Reformation Catholicism. Use him for documents and counts, not for verdicts.

**Usable in:** Article 1 (historiography); Article 2 (with caution).

## 5. Boccaccio, *Decameron*, introduction to the First Day (c. 1349–53)

- **Edition.** Boccaccio, Giovanni. 1886. *The Decameron of Giovanni Boccaccio*. Translated by John Payne. London: Villon Society. Undated American reprint (Walter J. Black), Project Gutenberg eBook 23700.
- **Translator.** The Gutenberg text is **John Payne**'s (1886), not Rigg's. Fordham's excerpt (`boccacio2.asp`) is a different older translation.
- **URL.** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/23700/pg23700.txt
- **Read:** the whole plague introduction (Payne's "Day the First", proem through the gathering at Santa Maria Novella).

**Summary.**

- **Remedies fail.** Civic sanitation, quarantine of the sick and "humble supplications … in ordered processions" all failed. So did physicians, many of them untrained men and women.
- **Responses.** People isolated themselves, carousèd, took a middle path, or fled.
- **Collapse of order.** "the reverend authority of the laws, both human and divine" dissolved for lack of officers. Kin abandoned kin. Care was left to friends or to servants paid "high and extravagant wage".
- **Funerals.** The rites collapsed. Hired *becchini* carried the dead to the nearest church behind "five or six priests". Corpses were stacked in trenches.
- **Countryside.** Peasants "died, not like men, but well nigh like beasts".
- **The frame.** It opens in a church: seven ladies meet after "divine service" at Santa Maria Novella and leave off saying paternosters to plan their retreat.

**Key passages.**

> "which, through the operation of the heavenly bodies or of our own iniquitous dealings, being sent down upon mankind for our correction by the just wrath of God"

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: THEO. Astral and penal causes sit side by side; plague is "correction".

> "nor yet humble supplications, not once but many times both in ordered processions and on other wise made unto God by devout persons"

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: REM. Ritual failure is recorded without loss of belief.

> "In this sore affliction and misery of our city, the reverend authority of the laws, both human and divine, was all in a manner dissolved and fallen into decay, for [lack of] the ministers and executors thereof, who, like other men, were all either dead or sick or else left so destitute of followers that they were unable to exercise any office"

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: CH. Institutional authority fails because its officers die, not through apostasy.

> "there remained unto those … who fell sick, none other succour than that which they owed either to the charity of friends (and of these there were few) or the greed of servants, who tended them, allured by high and extravagant wage"

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: PROV/REM. Care is bought on the market.

> "shouldered the bier and bore it with hurried steps, not to that church which the dead man had chosen before his death, but most times to the nearest, behind five or six priests, with little light and whiles none at all, which latter, with the aid of the said pickmen, thrust him into what grave soever they first found unoccupied, without troubling themselves with too long or too formal a service."

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: CH. Priests are present but perfunctory, and work alongside hired *becchini*.

> "two priests going with one cross for some one, three or four biers, borne by bearers, ranged themselves behind the latter, and whereas the priests thought to have but one dead man to bury, they had six or eight, and whiles more."

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: capacity overwhelmed, not absent.

> "there foregathered in the venerable church of Santa Maria Novella, one Tuesday morning when there was well nigh none else there, seven young ladies … who had heard divine service in mourning attire … having seated themselves in a ring, after divers sighs, let be the saying of paternosters and fell to devising"

- Locator: Payne trans., Day 1 intro.
- Why it matters: devotion is continued but set aside for a lay-organised escape.

**For/against.** This supports ritual and institutional *incapacity* without anticlerical revolt. Priests appear, overwhelmed, not fled. THEO is penal. The remedy is privatised (flight, regimen, paid nursing). It does not support "faith erosion" as a direct claim.

**Source-critical notes.** This is a literary frame, partly modelled on Paul the Deacon and Thucydides-style plague topoi. Boccaccio was a lay humanist. The Payne translation is archaising and uses square brackets for supplied words; check key phrases against Branca's Italian before quoting.

**Usable in:** Article 1; Article 2.

## 6. Heinrich von Herford, *Liber de rebus memorabilioribus* (c. 1355): Jews and flagellants, 1349

- **Edition.** Heinrich von Herford. 1859. *Liber de rebus memorabilioribus sive Chronicon Henrici de Hervordia*. Edited by August Potthast. Göttingen: Dieterich.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/bub_gb_rfw0AAAAQAAJ/bub_gb_rfw0AAAAQAAJ_djvu.txt
- **Read:** the opening of Charles IV's reign (portents, pp. 277–79) and the 1349 sections on the Jews and the flagellants (pp. 280–83), in full.

**Summary.**

- **Signs of the reign.** Herford, a Dominican of Minden, lists the start of Charles IV's reign among its "monstra et portenta". Plague; "a people without a head, the flagellants, announced the coming of Antichrist"; wells reportedly poisoned; Jews cruelly slaughtered; simony unchecked at the Curia.
- **The Jews.** He rejects the poisoning charge and suspects greed for Jewish wealth.
- **The flagellants.** He describes their scourges and ritual precisely, and saw them himself ("Vidi"). He admits their performance moved spectators to tears. He condemns them for usurping preaching, speaking "not soberly" of clergy and sacraments, rejecting correction, and stoning a Dominican to death. Bishops (Utrecht) joined them. Osnabrück refused them entry, to the fury of its women. They vanished "as suddenly as they came".
- **Source and the bull.** He takes some material from Gerhard of Cosvelde's treatise. A footnote by Potthast points to Clement VI's bull "ap. Raynald., Ann. eccl. ad a. 1349. nro. 20".

**Key passages.**

> "Gens sine capite flagellariorum adventum Anticristi pronuntiavit … Judei crudeliter mactabantur. … Omnia per omnes etiam religiosos, in curia Romana procurabantur et impetrabantur. Symoniacam heresim validissimo regnantem nulli detestabantur."

- Locator: Herford 1859, 277. OCR "8ymoniacam … vali- dissimo".
- Why it matters: CH. A Dominican attacks the Curia's simony in the same breath as the plague.

> "Item hoc anno Judei per Theutoniam pluresque provincias alias universi cum mulieribus et parvulis ferro vel igne crudeliter et inhumaniter absumuntur, aut propter divitias eorum copiosissimas, quas plerique et nobiles et alii pauperes et indigentes vel etiam eorum debitores usurpare querebant; quod verum esse credo … aut propter aquarum invenenationes per eos … quod verum esse non credo"

- Locator: Herford 1859, 280. OCR "proWncias … quorebant … esso".
- Why it matters: Jews. A clerical chronicler denies the poisoning charge and names debt and greed as the motive.

> "Fuit quodlibet flagellum baculus quidam, a quo tres cordule … nodos magnos habentes, dependebant, sic quod utrimque duo ferramenta, sicut acus acuta per medios nodos in modum crucis transeuntia … Vidi, cum se flagellarent, aliquando ferramenta dicta carni taliter infigi, quod uno tractu quandoque, quandoque duobus, non extrahebantur."

- Locator: Herford 1859, 281. OCR "cami … tracta".
- Why it matters: eyewitness. This is devotion intensifying under lay control.

> "Cor lapideum esset, quod talia sine lacrimis posset aspicere."

- Locator: Herford 1859, 281.
- Why it matters: even the hostile observer concedes the movement's emotional and devotional power.

> "predicationis etiam officium cum penitentie flagellis infeliciter et pertinaciter indocti stolidique presumunt. De religiosis et clericis et ecclesie sacramentis non sobrie sentiunt et loquuntur, objurgationes et correctiones respuunt"

- Locator: Herford 1859, 281–82. OCR "dieationis … sentinnt … objur-/pntionoB"; my normalisation.
- Why it matters: REM/CH. The real offence is that laymen take over preaching and judge the clergy.

> "Si forte dicebatur eis: 'Cur predicatis, cum non sitis missi … et docetis quod non intelligitis, cum sitis illitterati?' responderunt …: 'Et quis misit vos, et per quid scitis, quod corpus Cristi consecratis, aut quod verum est ewangelium, quod predicatis?'"

- Locator: Herford 1859, 282. OCR "(|Uod non intelliptie … veas … cwanfjclium"; my normalisation.
- Why it matters: REM/CH. This is the clearest statement in the corpus of lay challenge to clerical sacramental authority.

> "fratres duos predicatores eis occurrentes in campo volentes occidere, cum agilior elapsus aufugisset, alium lapidaverunt"

- Locator: Herford 1859, 282.
- Why it matters: violence against the Dominicans who opposed them.

> "Sententiam etiam excommunicationis in eos per episcopos latam vel negligunt vel contempnunt. Processus pape contra se non attendunt"

- Why it matters: institutional sanctions fail.

> "Osnabrugenses eos numquam intromiserunt, uxoribus eorum et mulieribus aliis hoc impatientissime ferentibus."

- Locator: Herford 1859, 282.
- Why it matters: gender. Women supported the flagellants against their town's magistrates.

> "Fuerunt inter eos etiam quidam spectabiles, probi et honesti viri, sed et episcopi, puta Trajectensis et ceteri, quamquam communiter de plebeis terrarum et etiam de ribaldis collecti fuerint."

**For/against.**

- REM: strong evidence of lay seizure of preaching and penance, and of contempt for clerical "keys".
- THEO: penance as answer to God's anger, unchanged in substance.
- Jews: clerical scepticism of the poisoning charge.
- POW: the movement was mostly plebeian, with some elite and episcopal participation.

**Source-critical notes.** Herford is hostile (a Dominican, and Dominicans were the flagellants' targets). Parts depend on Gerhard of Cosvelde. He wrote about 1355, after the suppression. Still, he is an eyewitness ("Vidi").

## 7. Gilles li Muisis, abbot of St-Martin, Tournai, *Annales* 1349 (to Feb 1350)

- **Edition.** Lemaître, Henri, ed. 1906. *Chronique et annales de Gilles le Muisit, abbé de Saint-Martin de Tournai (1272–1352)*. Paris: Renouard (Société de l'histoire de France).
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/chroniqueetannal00lemuuoft/chroniqueetannal00lemuuoft_djvu.txt
- **Note on editions.** De Smet's 1841 edition (*Corpus chronicorum Flandriae* II) was **not** used. Lemaître's covers the same 1349 annals.
- **Read:** the whole 1349 run, pp. 222–259, in full (Jews, flagellants, plague, the city ordinances, pilgrimages).

**Summary.** This is the single richest text in the corpus for the model.

- **The Jews.** Li Muisis believes the rumour that Jews poisoned wells, and reports rumours of their burning across Germany.
- **Before the plague.** He moralises at length about the dress, dancing and luxury of 1349, clergy included ("Ut populus sic sacerdos").
- **The flagellants.** Their companies arrive from Bruges on 15 Aug 1349; he lists the towns and numbers. They split the city. A Dominican travelling with the Liège company preaches that their blood is the noblest shed since Christ's. He calls the mendicants who preach against them "scorpions and antichrists". The people turn against the mendicants and "the whole clergy". The chapter stages a counter-procession with the Host, and the Augustinian preacher is shouted down.
- **Tournai's own flagellants.** About 565 Tournai men form their own company with the governors' licence, accompanied by the Augustinian prior and two secular priests to hear confessions.
- **Moral change.** Dice-makers turn to making paternoster beads; concubines are married; feuds are pardoned.
- **The flagellants take over funerals.** By Advent the companies meet by parish, disrupt services, and carry and bury the dead with cross and candles. The dying are induced to leave them bequests "as is customary for curates, chaplains, hospitals and the poor". The clergy dare not object "because of the commune".
- **Suppression.** It is proclaimed in Lent 1350 by the town governors and the king. The bishop and chapter arrange a sermon at which "the Pope's mandate" is announced, "but the bull not shown". The penitents cease "with great murmuring, unwillingly".
- **The plague itself.** The bishop dies of plague, apparently, in June 1349. When parish clergy and gravediggers ring bells "for their own profit" day and night, the **city governors**, seeing the dean, chapter and clergy do nothing "because it was in their interest and they were profiting", issue ordinances. These cover concubinage, immediate burial without bier, limited bell-ringing, no mourning gatherings, a ban on dice and oaths, and Sunday rest.
- **Who died.** The rich who drank wine and avoided the sick survived. Curates and chaplains who heard confessions died in numbers.
- **Pilgrimages.** Pilgrimages to St Sebastian's relics surged, and "when the storm ceased, pilgrimage and devotion ceased too".

**Key passages.**

> "Penitencia autem debet esse discreta, rationabilis, ac ordinata et incepta de superioris voluntate et habentis injungendi potestatem, quia, quod sine superioris fit auctoritate vel voluntate, presumpcioni deputabitur et vane glorie, non mercedi."

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 228.
- Why it matters: REM. The clerical principle at stake: penance needs authorisation from a superior.

> "Homines ecclesiasticos, quod dolendum est, non excludo tota litera predictis quia, ve Ecclesie! videtur tempus venisse quod dictum est : 'Ut populus sic sacerdos.'"

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 229.
- Why it matters: CH. An abbot's own verdict on clerical worldliness.

> "Populus autem utriusque sexus, qui nunquam tale quid viderant, ceperunt compati personis, et penitentie condolere, et Deo gratias reddere super tanta penitentia"

- Locator: first arrival of the flagellants, 15 Aug 1349, Lemaître 1906, 231.
- Why it matters: lay devotion intensifies.

> "vocans tales fratres scorpiones et antichristos, comparans eciam sanguinem illorum quos vocabat rubeos milites … sanguini Domini nostri Jhesu Christi … Finitaque predicatione, placuit communitati ultra modum, et ceperunt pene omnes contra ordines Mendicantium et etiam supra totum clerum murmurare."

- Locator: sermon of a Dominican, 30 Aug 1349, Lemaître 1906, 233.
- Why it matters: CH/REM. Clerical support for the flagellants exists (a friar). The people's anger turns on the clergy as a whole.

> "et tota illa die fuit ingens tumultus per totam civitatem super dictos ordines et super totum clerum."

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 235.
- Why it matters: open lay revolt against the clergy inside the cathedral city.

> "Item intrantes debent a suo curato petere licentiam et ab ipso crucem recipere, et ab uxore sua legitima petere licentiam"

- Locator: the flagellants' admission oath, Lemaître 1906, 243.
- Why it matters: REM nuance. The rule itself asks the parish priest's licence. The movement was not anticlerical by design.

> "pro juribus sancte Ecclesie, honore et libertate, fide, doctrina et lege defendere, tenere et servare, corpus et possessiones exponemus"

- Locator: oath, Lemaître 1906, 243.
- Why it matters: the movement understood itself as defending the Church.

> "orare pro tota sancta Christianitate, quod Deus cessare faciat hanc mortalitatem et nobis indulgeat nostra peccata"

- Locator: oath, Lemaître 1906, 244.
- Why it matters: THEO. Plague is penal and penance is the remedy, a lay-performed version of the clergy's own theology.

> "populus autem, viri et mulieres, scripturas ignorantes, nobiles et ignobiles factum ultra modum approbabant … Et maxime populus universus super ordines Mendicantes precipue imponebat, quod per eos impedimentum totum erat. … Et magna pars vulgi factum talium plus quam divinum officium approbabant, reputantes approbabile, quia per eorum adventum fuit tanta mutatio mali in bonum … quod propter hoc homines ecclesiasticos, qui opinionem suam tenebant, sepissime deridebant."

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 239.
- Why it matters: **key quotation**. Lay devotion is valued above the clergy's liturgy, with derision of clergy, and is justified by its moral fruits.

> "modo adunant se mutuo per parrochias, et in sabbatis et in festis in cathedrali ecclesia Beate Marie et in aliis ecclesiis faciunt suum ritum, sepe impedientes divina servicia; nec a clero possunt super hoc reprehendi, quia propter communitatem non auderent."

- Locator: Advent 1349, Lemaître 1906, 240.
- Why it matters: POW. The clergy are cowed by the commune.

> "nam collegi et curati, secundum quod est rationabile et consuetum, ad corpora levanda, portanda et sepelienda, accedere non oportet, quia societates penitentium cum cruce et cereis corpora levant, portant et sepeliunt; et sepe inducti sunt morientes ut talibus donent, sicut solet fieri curatis, capellanis, hospitalibus et pauperibus."

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 240.
- Why it matters: **key quotation for REM**. Lay companies take over burial, the clergy's ritual and economic role, together with the bequests that went with it.

> "Fuit eciam ex parte regis proclamatum quod omnes eciam cessarent, quia rex eos sectam vocabat in sua lettera, et hoc sub pena perditionis corporum et bonorum."

- Locator: Lent 1350, Lemaître 1906, 251.
- Why it matters: POW. Suppression came through secular force.

> "ibique mandatum domini pape pro penitentibus divulgatum, bulla tamen non ostensa, propter quod fuit in populo murmur magnum. … A dicta autem die penitentes predicti cum magno murmure inviti cessaverunt."

- Locator: 21 Feb 1350, Lemaître 1906, 252.
- Why it matters: institutional trust. People suspected the papal mandate (the bull "not shown"), and complied unwillingly.

> "Et in omnibus parrochiis curati, clerici parrochiarum et fossatores, pro lucro suo faciebant pulsaciones campanarum de die et de nocte … Et sic ceperunt pavere in civitate universaliter viri et mulieres … et nemo super hoc remedium apponebat. Gubernatores civitatis, videntes quod decanus, et capitulum, et clerus totus, de remedio apponendo non curabant, quia sua intererat et in facto lucrabantur, habito consilio, fecerunt ordinationes"

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 255.
- Why it matters: **key quotation for REM/CH**. Lay civic authority takes the remedy away from a clergy seen as profiting from death.

> "Audeo dicere … quod predicta proclamatio fecit maximam multitudinem virorum et mulierum per matrimonium conjungere, qui antea erant tenentes concubinas … magistri taxillos facientes … de materia de qua taxillos quadratos faciebant, facere inceperunt res rotundas, de quibus Pater noster faciebant."

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 257.
- Why it matters: devotion intensifies under **lay** regulation.

> "Et pro certo curati ac capellani, confessiones audientes et sacramenta ministrantes, clerici etiam parrochiarum et cum eis infirmos visitantes, de talibus multi decesserunt."

- Locator: Lemaître 1906, 258.
- Why it matters: CH, counter-evidence. Many clergy who served the sick died at their work.

> "Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio."

- Locator: St Sebastian pilgrimages at Hasnon and Soissons, Lemaître 1906, 258.
- Why it matters: **key quotation for REC/DELIV**. Devotion tracks acute threat and fades when it lifts.

**For/against.**

- **REM: strongly for.** Remedies move from clergy to lay companies and city governors. Lay penance is preferred to the divine office, and the movement takes over funerary roles and bequests.
- **CH: mixed.** The clergy are depicted as profiting and passive, the mendicant orders are hated, and the clergy fear the commune. But curates and chaplains died serving, and individual friars led the flagellants.
- **POW.** The clergy depend on the commune, and suppression needed the king and the town governors.
- **REC/DELIV.** Devotion ends with the plague. That supports a threat-timing rival explanation for devotional peaks.
- **Trust.** Suspicion of the papal mandate ("bulla … non ostensa").
- **Jews.** A clerical author credits the poisoning rumour, in contrast to Herford and Venette.

**Source-critical notes.**

- Li Muisis was an elderly Benedictine abbot, partly blind, who dictated the text. He was hostile to novelties but tries to be fair ("non interest mea approbare aut reprobare").
- He is an eyewitness for Tournai and repeats hearsay for elsewhere (he says so himself).
- Lemaître omits a poem and the "false miracles" section (his notes at pp. 227 and 252).
- The bull's date of 20 Oct 1349 is Lemaître's note (p. 252 n. 1). It matches Raynaldus (§10).

**Usable in:** Article 2 (core); Article 1.

## 8. Continuation of Guillaume de Nangis attributed to Jean de Venette, 1348–49

- **Edition.** Géraud, Hercule, ed. 1843. *Chronique latine de Guillaume de Nangis de 1113 à 1300 avec les continuations de cette chronique de 1300 à 1368*. Vol. 2. Paris: Renouard (Société de l'histoire de France).
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/bub_gb_vO9W31qvfpQC/bub_gb_vO9W31qvfpQC_djvu.txt
- **Read:** pp. 210–218 in full (1348–49); pp. 317–20 (1360–62).

**Summary.**

- **The plague.** Contagion is spread by visiting the sick. "Timid priests withdrew" in many towns, leaving ministration to bolder religious.
- **Care.** The nuns of the Hôtel-Dieu tended the dying "not fearing death" and were repeatedly renewed as they died.
- **Good deaths.** Yet almost everyone died confessed and with the viaticum. The Pope granted absolution *a pena et culpa*, so people "died more willingly, leaving many inheritances and temporal goods to churches and religious, because they saw their own heirs die before them".
- **The Jews.** They were blamed and burned. The chronicler doubts the poisoning ("revera tales intoxicationes … non potuissent tantam plagam … infecisse") and attributes the plague to God's will.
- **Afterwards.** Marriages and births abounded, and children were born with fewer teeth. But the world was not improved: people were more avaricious, and "Charity began to grow very cold", with ignorance and few to teach the young.
- **Flagellants.** They were barred from France by the king on the advice of the Paris theologians. The Pope condemned them, and they "submitted, receiving absolution from their prelates". Many honest women did the same penance.

**Key passages.**

> "Unde in multis villis parvis et magnis sacerdotes timidi recedebant, religiosis aliquibus magis audacibus administrationem dimittentes"

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:211.
- Why it matters: CH. Secular priests fled and religious stepped in. This is mendicant self-presentation (Venette was a Carmelite).

> "Et istae sanctae sorores Domus-Dei, mori non timentes, dulcissime et humillime omni honore postposito pertractabant ; quarum multiplex numerus dictarum sororum, saepius renovatus per mortem, in pace cum Christo, ut pie creditur, requiescit."

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:212.
- Why it matters: CH/PROV. Institutional care was delivered by women religious.

> "Nec erat aliquis quin confessus et cum viatico sacratissimo moreretur ; et … dominus papa Clemens VI … absolutionem a poena et culpa decedentibus per suos confessores dedit … unde libentius moriebantur, haereditates multas et bona temporalia ecclesiis et religiosis dimittentes, quia proprios haeredes ante se mori videbant."

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:213.
- Why it matters: devotion and wealth flow **toward** the institution. This counter-evidence is important.

> "sed revera tales intoxicationes, posito quod factae fuissent, non potuissent tantam plagam et tantum populum infecisse. Alia igitur fuit causa, ut puta, Dei voluntas"

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:214.
- Why it matters: Jews. A clerical chronicler rejects the poisoning theory.

> "Sed, proh dolor ! ex hujus renovatione saeculi non est mundus propter hoc in melius commutatus. Nam homines fuerunt postea magis avari et tenaces"

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:215. OCR "honnes … to plura" is partly garbled.
- Why it matters: no moral reform after deliverance.

> "Charitas etiam ab illo tempore refrigescere coepit valde, et iniquitas abundavit cum ignorantia et peccatis; nam pauci inveniebantur qui scirent aut vellent in domibus, villis et castris informare [pueros in grammaticae] rudimentis."

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:216. The OCR is garbled ("informare La 2 in FRNQEeS"); the bracketed words are my best reading.
- Why it matters: TRANS. Teaching the next generation broke down.

> "novam sectam auctoritate propria inchoantes"

- Locator: on the flagellants, Géraud 1843, 2:217.
- Why it matters: REM. The offence is self-authorisation.

> "Parisius autem non venerunt … prohibiti per dominum regem Franciae … de consilio magistrorum in theologia Universitatis Parisiensis, dicentium quod nova secta erat contra Deum inchoata, et contra formam sanctae matris Ecclesiae"

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:217.
- Why it matters: POW. The alliance of crown and university stopped the movement.

> "quia jam inveniebant tales flagellatores, fulti aliquibus sacerdotibus fatuis et religiosis, sectas erroneas … Nam dicebant quod eorum sanguis, sic per flagella tractus et effusus, cum Christi sanguine miscebatur"

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:218.
- Why it matters: some clergy supported the flagellants, and the doctrine of blood rivalled the sacrament.

> "moniti destiterunt, absolutionem et poenitentiam a suis praelatis ex parte domini Papae de hujusmodi errore humiliter acceptantes."

- Locator: Géraud 1843, 2:218.
- Why it matters: institutional authority reasserted (contrast li Muisis's "inviti … cum magno murmure").

> "Magna mortalitas anno illo in Flandria et in Picardia fuit, et in sequenti anno Parisius. … in Domo-Dei Parisius communiter efferebantur mortui septuaginta funera vel octoginta"

- Locator: 1360–61, Géraud 1843, 2:317.
- Why it matters: REC.

**For/against.** This text gives the other side of li Muisis.

- Institution-directed devotion: confession, viaticum and bequests to churches.
- CH: care delivered by nuns. Secular priests fled.
- After the plague, charity cooled and teaching failed, which bears on TRANS.
- REM: flagellants as self-authorised, suppressed through crown, university and Pope.
- Jews: sceptical.

**Source-critical notes.** The author is a Carmelite friar, so mendicant self-interest colours "timid priests" versus "bolder religious". He wrote around 1359–60. Géraud's footnote (p. 212 n. 1) suspects the figure of "500 a day at the Hôtel-Dieu" should be 50. The "fewer teeth" story is a borrowed topos (Rigord 1187), per Géraud p. 215 n. 1.

**Usable in:** Article 2; Article 1.

## 9. English chronicles: Geoffrey le Baker and Robert of Avesbury

### Geoffrey le Baker, *Chronicon* (1350s)

- **Edition.** Baker, Geoffrey le. 1889. *Chronicon Galfridi le Baker de Swynebroke*. Edited by Edward Maunde Thompson. Oxford: Clarendon Press.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/chronicongalfri00thomgoog/chronicongalfri00thomgoog_djvu.txt
- **Read:** pp. 98–100 in full. The OCR is heavily garbled.

**Summary.**

- **Spread.** The plague hit Saracens, Turks and Greeks so hard that "struck with terror they considered receiving the faith and sacraments of Christ". When they heard Christians were dying too, they did not.

## 10. Clement VI's bulls on the Jews (1348) and the flagellants (1349), in Raynaldus

- **Edition.** Raynaldus, Odoricus. 1872 [Theiner ed.]. *Annales ecclesiastici* (continuing Baronius). Vol. 25 (1334–). Bar-le-Duc / Paris: Guérin. 1348 §§31–34, pp. 453–55; 1349 §§18–22, pp. 470–72. The title page reads "Caesaris Baronii … Annales ecclesiastici", Theiner edition; IA metadata date 1864.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/annalesecclesias25barouoft/annalesecclesias25barouoft_djvu.txt
- **Read:** 1348 §§31–34 and 1349 §§18–23 in full.

**What I could establish.**

1. ***Sicut Judeis* (first protection).** Raynaldus quotes the operative clauses ("Nullus Christianus Judaeos eosdem invitos vel nolentes ad baptismum per violentiam venire compellat … Nullus etiam Christianus eorumdem Judaeorum personas sine judicio domini regionis … vulnerare aut occidere, vel suas illis pecunias auferre praesumat, etc."). It is dated **"Datum Avinione IV non. Julii, anno vii", i.e. 4 July 1348**. It is the traditional *Sicut Judeis* constitution reissued; Raynaldus does not print the incipit.
2. **The second bull (= *Quamvis perfidiam*).** Raynaldus quotes a long extract beginning "Nuper ad nostrum fama publica, seu infamia verius, perduxit auditum". He dates it **"VI kal. Octobris", i.e. 26 Sept 1348**. He does not print the incipit *Quamvis perfidiam*; that name comes from modern editions (Simonsohn), which I did not see. The argument is that the plague also strikes Jews and peoples who never lived with Jews, so the charge is implausible. Prelates must announce that Jews are not to be struck, and those who harm them incur papal anathema.
3. **The anti-flagellant bull.** Raynaldus prints the version addressed to the Archbishop of Magdeburg and his suffragans. Its first words after the address are **"Molesta nobis, licet fide digna, magnorum relatio"**, and it is **"Datum Avinione XIII kal. Novembris, anno viii", i.e. 20 October 1349**. Lemaître independently dates the bull read at Tournai to 20 Oct 1349. Raynaldus adds that a longer list of addressees in Germany, Poland, Sweden, England and France is in the papal register. He also cites a letter to Edward III, dated "III non. Decembris anno viii" (3 Dec 1349), asking him to keep flagellants out of England, and says the same letter went to Philip VI. **I could not verify the name *Inter sollicitudines*.** Raynaldus prints no such incipit, and Fredericq was not checked. The project files should give the bull as "Clement VI, bull against the flagellants, Avignon, 20 Oct 1349 (copy to Magdeburg begins *Molesta nobis*)" until the name is checked against Fredericq or Simonsohn.
4. **Plague-time care at Avignon.** From the *Vita Clementis*: physicians were paid to visit the poor, attendants were provided, a cemetery was bought and consecrated ("Campus Floridus"), and indulgences were given to priests who administered sacraments to the sick and to those who buried the dead. A diploma is dated XIV kal. Jun. [19 May] 1348.

**Key passages.**

> "Hac durante tempestate, dictus papa in Avenione rem fecit maximae charitatis : nam pro pauperibus visitandis certos ordinavit medicos, et alios qui eis in suis necessitatibus subvenirent in vita, et cum decedebant, etiam necessaria ad sepulturam ministrabant : ad mortuorum corpora capienda emit unum magnum campum, in quo coemeterium fecit consecrari"

- Locator: 1348 §32, Raynaldus/Theiner 25:454.
- Why it matters: PROV/CH. The institution delivers care at its centre.

> "nonnulla indulgentiarum praemia sacerdotibus quoque qui Ecclesiastica sacramenta percussis peste subministrarent, necnon iis qui pium aliquod officium obsequiumve praestarent, vel etiam defunctos sepulturae mandarent, proposuit"

- Locator: 1348 §32, Raynaldus/Theiner 25:454.
- Why it matters: incentives for clergy to stay at their posts.

> "Nuper ad nostrum fama publica, seu infamia verius, perduxit auditum, quod nonnulli Christiani pestem, qua Deus populum Christianum ipsius peccatis populi provocatus affligit, Judaeorum falso tossicationibus seducente diabolo imputantes, nonnullos ex Judaeis ipsis temeritate propria, non deferentes aetati vel sexui, impie peremerunt"

- Locator: 26 Sept 1348, Raynaldus/Theiner 25:455. OCR "Clnistianum … po[iuIi … ajlati" normalised.
- Why it matters: Jews/THEO. The Pope keeps the penal theology but rejects scapegoating.

> "quia tamen per diversa mundi climata Judaeos ipsos ac alias nationes plurimas, quae cohabitationem Judaeorum eorumdem non noverant, pestis haec ubique fere communis afflixit occulto Dei judicio et affligit; proinde verisimilitudo non recipit, quod Judaei praedicti occasionem tanto flagitio praestiterint sive causam"

- Locator: Raynaldus/Theiner 25:455.
- Why it matters: an empirical argument used by the institution.

> "Non potuit Apostolico hoc studio inusta inique Judaeis sedari infamia : ubique enim, excepto comitatu Avenionensi, tanquam venefici ad necem quaesiti"

- Locator: Raynaldus's own comment, Raynaldus/Theiner 25:455.
- Why it matters: POW. Papal authority was effective only where the Pope ruled directly.

> "quod se per societates et conventicula … diversas circuivit patrias, caeterorum vitam et statum contemnendo : se justificant, et claves Ecclesiae vilipendunt ; ac in contemptum disciplinae Ecclesiasticae crucem Domini ante se … sine superioris licentia deferentes, sub nomine poenitentiae, vitam gerunt insolitam"

- Locator: bull of 20 Oct 1349, Raynaldus/Theiner 25:471.
- Why it matters: REM. The papal charge is contempt for the keys and penance without licence.

> "Turbamur acerbius … quod quidam religiosi, praesertim de Ordinibus Mendicantium, qui alios ab invio revocare debuerant … linguas suas ut alios pertrahant in errorem, acuunt"

- Locator: Raynaldus/Theiner 25:471.
- Why it matters: CH. The Pope admits that mendicant friars led the movement (compare li Muisis's Dominican preacher).

> "cum plerique ex ipsis seu adhaerentes eisdem sub pietatis colore ad impietatis opera laxantes crudeliter manus suas, Judaeorum (quos pietas Christiana recipit et sustinet …) et frequenter Christianorum sanguinem effundere, … bona clericorum et laicorum diripere, et suis juribus applicare, ac superiorem jurisdictionem usurpare … minime vereantur"

- Locator: Raynaldus/Theiner 25:471.
- Why it matters: Jews/POW. The bull ties the flagellants to anti-Jewish killing and to usurping jurisdiction.

> "Per praedicta tamen nequaquam intendimus prohibere, quin Christi fideles impositam sibi canonice poenitentiam vel etiam non impositam (dummodo recta intentione et pura devotione ad illam peragendam procedant) in suis hospitiis, vel alias absque superstitionibus, congregationibus, societatibus et conventiculis supradictis possint facere"

- Locator: Raynaldus/Theiner 25:472.
- Why it matters: REM, key nuance. Private lay penance is explicitly allowed. What is banned is **organised, public, self-governing** penance.

> "nec laici amplius criminum nexus solvendi praesumptam impie auctoritatem sibi arrogarunt"

- Locator: Raynaldus/Theiner 25:472.
- Why it matters: REM. According to Raynaldus, flagellant laymen had claimed the power to absolve sins.

**Source-critical.** Raynaldus was a 17th-century Oratorian, writing an apologetic papal history. He quotes the registers, but selectively and with "etc." truncations. Mansi's notes in this edition draw on Herford via Cornerus. The bull texts should be checked against Simonsohn (*Apostolic See and the Jews*, Documents 1342–1394) when accessible.

**Usable in:** Article 2 (core); Article 1.

## 11. Jacob Twinger von Königshofen on the Strasbourg Jews, 1349 (Marcus 1938, via Fordham)

- **Edition.** Marcus, Jacob R. 1938. *The Jew in the Medieval World: A Source Book, 315–1791*. New York: JPS. 43–48. Reproduced in the Internet Jewish History Sourcebook, "The Black Death and the Jews, 1348–1349 CE" (Halsall 1998).
- **URL.** https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/jewish/1348-jewsblackdeath.asp
- **Read:** the whole page. It contains the confession of Agimet (Châtel, Oct 1348), Königshofen's account, and a Toledo epitaph of 1349.

**Summary.** The Agimet "confession" was extracted under torture ("put to the torture a little … subjected again to torture a little"). In Königshofen's account:

- The Pope protected Jews only at Avignon.
- The rulers of Strasbourg, Freiburg and Basel did not believe the charge. At Basel the citizens forced the council's hand.
- At Benfeld (8 Feb 1349) "the Bishop of Strasbourg … and the Imperial Cities agreed to do away with the Jews".
- The Strasbourg council that tried to save the Jews was deposed.
- About 2,000 were burned on 14 Feb 1349, and children were baptised against their parents' will.
- Debts were cancelled and the cash was shared among the craftsmen. "Some gave their share to the Cathedral or to the Church on the advice of their confessors."
- Jews were readmitted in 1368.

**Key passages.**

> "The pope at Avignon stopped all sessions of court, locked himself in a room, allowed no one to approach him and had a fire burning before him all the time. … And from what this epidemic came, all wise teachers and physicians could only say that it was God's will."

- Locator: Königshofen, trans. Marcus 1938.
- Why it matters: THEO.

> "for this reason the Jews were burnt all the way from the Mediterranean into Germany, but not in Avignon, for the pope protected them there."

- Locator: Königshofen, trans. Marcus 1938.
- Why it matters: POW. Papal protection reached only as far as papal territory.

> "So finally the Bishop and the lords and the Imperial Cities agreed to do away with the Jews."

- Locator: Benfeld, 8 Feb 1349; Königshofen, trans. Marcus 1938.
- Why it matters: CH as an ally of the perpetrators at diocesan level, against papal policy.

> "The money was indeed the thing that killed the Jews. If they had been poor and if the feudal lords had not been in debt to them, they would not have been burnt. After this wealth was divided among the artisans some gave their share to the Cathedral or to the Church on the advice of their confessors."

- Locator: Königshofen, trans. Marcus 1938.
- Why it matters: Jews/CH. The church received the proceeds of the killing; motive economic.

**Source-critical.** Königshofen (c. 1382–1420) wrote decades later, using Closener (1362). Marcus translates him in abridged form with editorial brackets; the inserted dates and the "[Some say …]" glosses are Marcus's. The Middle High German original (Hegel 1870–71, *Chroniken der deutschen Städte* 8–9) was **not obtained**. Fordham's page says the 1938 copyright was apparently not renewed.

**Usable in:** Article 2.

## 12. Friar John Clyn, *Annals* (Kilkenny, 1348–49)

- **Read:** pp. 35–37 in full (1348–49 and the later marginal note).

**Summary.**

- **Pilgrimage.** In September–October 1348 bishops, prelates, magnates and "all of both sexes" went in thousands on pilgrimage to Tech Moling, "some from devotion, but more from fear of the pestilence".
- **Mortality.** Dublin lost 14,000 between August and Christmas. The Pope consecrated a new cemetery at Avignon.

> "quidam venerunt devocionis affectu, alii (sed plures) pestilencie metu"

> "et confitens et confessor simul ducerentur ad sepulchrum. Et pre timore et horrore, pietatis opera et misericordie, videlicet, visitare infirmos et mortuos sepellire, homines excercere vix audebant."

- Locator: Butler 1849, 36.
- Why it matters: CH/PROV. Care collapsed; confessors died with the penitents.

> "dimitto pergamenam pro opere continuando, si forte in futuro homo superstes remaneat, an aliquis de genere Ade hanc pestilenciam possit evadere et opus continuare inceptum."

- Locator: Butler 1849, 37.
- Why it matters: TRANS/eschatology.

- Locator: later hand, Butler 1849, 37.
- Why it matters: **REC**. Contemporaries numbered the recurrences.

**Source-critical.** Clyn was a Franciscan of Kilkenny, writing in the Anglo-Irish colony during the plague itself, so the text is contemporaneous. The prophecy he inserts (a vision at Tripoli) includes "gens quedam nascetur sine capite" (the "headless people") and "ordines mendicantes … adversabuntur". The same "sine capite" phrase is applied to the flagellants by Herford.

## 14. *Ars moriendi*: the Middle English *Craft of Dying* (Comper 1917)

- **Edition.** Comper, Frances M. M., ed. 1917. *The Book of the Craft of Dying and Other Early English Tracts Concerning Death*. London: Longmans, Green.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/bookofcraftofdyi00unse/bookofcraftofdyi00unse_djvu.txt
- **Read:** *The Book of the Craft of Dying*, chapters 1–6 (pp. 3–40), in a modernised text; the introduction was skimmed.
- **Not used.** O'Connor 1942 was downloaded (https://archive.org/download/artofdyingwellde00ocon/artofdyingwellde00ocon_djvu.txt) but not read. It is a 1942 monograph; whether its copyright was renewed is unclear, and the brief excludes copyrighted modern work.

**Summary.** This is the English version of the Latin *Tractatus artis bene moriendi*.

- **Audience.** The craft is needed by "lewd men" (glossed "laymen") as well as religious.
- **Despair.** It is the chief temptation, countered by the image of Christ's open arms.
- **Interrogations.** Six questions are put to the dying.
- **Who assists.** Responsibility for the dying is placed on "every man that hath love and dread of God" toward his "even christians". Prayers are to be said by "some man that is about him".
- **The clergy's place.** The sacraments are still to be sought first, and sickness is punishment for sin. Lay assistance supplements the sacraments rather than replacing them.

**Key passages.**

> "Forasmuch as the passage of death … for uncunning of dying—not only to lewd men but also to religious and devout persons—seemeth wonderfully hard and perilous"

- Locator: Comper 1917, 3. Comper glosses *lewd men* as "laymen".
- Why it matters: REM. The manual is addressed to the laity.

> "Notwithstanding that every secular man, both clerk and layman … must needs die when God will. Therefore ought every man, not only religious, but also every good and devout Christian man that desireth for to die well … live in such wise … that he may safely die, every hour"

- Locator: Comper 1917, 9.
- Why it matters: preparation for death becomes a lay discipline.

> "Therefore no man should despair of forgiveness"

- Locator: ch. 2, Comper 1917, c. 13–14.
- Why it matters: THEO. Despair is the danger, which matters for the "erosion" pathway.

> "And therefore every man that hath love and dread of God, and a zeal of [the heal of] man's soul, let him busily induce and warn every of his even christians that is sick, or in any peril of body or of soul, that principally and first … he diligently provide and ordain for the spiritual remedy and medicine of his soul."

- Locator: Comper 1917, 32.
- Why it matters: REM. Lay neighbours are made agents of the remedy.

> "Also all our evils and adversities, by righteous doom of God, cometh evermore to men for sins … Thou shalt not understand that God doeth the evil of the sin, but yieldeth the punishing for sin."

- Locator: Comper 1917, 33.
- Why it matters: THEO. The penal theodicy, with the Thomist distinction between the evil of sin and the evil of punishment.

> "receiving spiritual medicines, that is to say the sacraments of Holy Church ; ordaining and making his testament"

- Locator: Comper 1917, 33.
- Why it matters: the institution remains central.

> "And if it is a religious person, then when the covent is gathered together … then shall be said first the litany … Afterward, if he live yet, let some man that is about him say the orisons that follow … But nevertheless this ought not to be done of necessity, as though he might not be saved but if it were done … But among seculars that be sick let these prayers be said"

- Locator: ch. 6, Comper 1917, 40.
- Why it matters: REM. Lay bystanders may lead the deathbed prayers.

**For/against.** This supports a gradual transfer of deathbed ministry to the laity, but inside the Church. It is not anti-institutional. It is a later text (1415 and after) and supports Cohn's emphasis on later responses, when plague recurred.

**Source-critical.** This is a modernised edition. The Latin original is a product of Constance-era clerical reform (Gerson is cited as "the Chancellor of Paris"). It is devotional prescription, not a record of practice.

**Usable in:** Articles 1–2.

## 15. Muslim comparison: Ibn al-Khaṭīb, *Muqniʿat al-sāʾil* (c. 1349–50), ed. and German trans. Müller 1863

- **Edition.** Müller, Marcus Joseph. 1863. "Ibnulkhatîbs Bericht über die Pest." *Sitzungsberichte der königl. bayerischen Akademie der Wissenschaften zu München* 1863, 2: 1–34. Session of 6 June 1863.
- **URL.** https://archive.org/download/sitzungsbericht88wissgoog/sitzungsbericht88wissgoog_djvu.txt
- **Read:** the German introduction (p. 1) and the German translation on contagion, pp. 18–22. The Arabic text on pp. 2–12 was not read; the OCR is unusable.

**Summary.** Ibn al-Khaṭīb, vizier of Granada and a physician, argues as follows:

- Contagion is proven "by experience, research, sense perception, autopsy and reliable reports". The disease travels via clothing and vessels and arrives in port towns with infected travellers.
- Isolated people survived. A pious man in Salé walled himself in with provisions and his household survived. Tent-dwelling Arabs were spared.
- Some jurists, by fatwas denying contagion, caused many deaths.
- A proof from tradition that contradicts the senses must be reinterpreted. Pious men in North Africa had publicly retracted their earlier fatwas.

**Key passages (German translation).**

> "Die Existenz der Ansteckung steht fest durch die Erfahrung, die Forschung, die Sinneswahrnehmung, die Autopsie und verbürgte Kunden, und diess sind die Materien des Beweises."

- Locator: Müller 1863, 18. OCR "Forsdiung … Sinneswahmehmung" normalised.
- Why it matters: REM. The remedy is claimed by medical expertise.

> "und Gott gab sie in die Gewalt einiger Rechtsgelehrten, die sich ihnen mit Fetwas entgegen stellten, wie sich die Azâriqa-ketzer ihnen mit den Schwertern entgegen stellten, und es verbluteten unter den Spitzen ihrer Rohrfedern soviel Personen, dass bloss Gott ihre Zahl kennt, obwohl die Absicht der Rechtsgelehrten rein war"

## Synthesis

### What the full texts show collectively

1. **The institution recorded its own failure, in its own registers, early.**
   - Ralph's mandate of 10 Jan 1349: no priests "for zeal or for any stipend", out of "dread of infection".
   - Clement VI's dispensation to York (Oct 1349): priests "nequeunt inveniri".
   - Islip's *Effrenata* (1350, reissued 1362): surviving priests chase stipends and abandon cures.
   - The "failing establishment" is therefore not only a mendicant or Lollard projection, as the project's source guide worries. It is the hierarchy's own diagnosis.
   - Counter-evidence: Venette (deaths confessed, bequests to churches), li Muisis (curates and chaplains who served the sick "multi decesserunt"), Raynaldus (Clement VI's physicians and cemetery), Baker and Avesbury (bishop-funded burial grounds). All show care being delivered at the same time.

2. **Remedies passed to lay hands along three distinct routes.**
   - (a) **Delegated by the hierarchy.** Ralph's lay and female confession. Lincoln's general faculty to all priests. The papal free choice of confessor.
   - (b) **Seized by lay religious movements.** The flagellants: preaching, absolution (according to Raynaldus), funerals and bequests (Tournai).
   - (c) **Taken by lay civic authority.** The Tournai governors acted *because* the clergy profited from death-bells. Gloucester barred Bristol people.
   - The model should distinguish delegated from seized lay control. Delegation was reversible (survivors must re-confess to their own priest). Seizure was suppressed by force: king, town governors and papal bull.

3. **The flagellants intensified devotion and challenged trust in the clergy, but did not abandon the Church.**
   - Their oath asked the curate's licence and swore to defend "the rights of Holy Church" (li Muisis).
   - Yet in practice they mocked clerical keys ("who sent *you*?", Herford), drew crowds who preferred their rite to the divine office, and derided clergy (li Muisis).
   - **Friars and even bishops** led or joined them: Herford (Utrecht), li Muisis (a Dominican preacher), Venette ("sacerdotibus fatuis"), and the bull itself on mendicant ringleaders.
   - The clerical camp was split, which complicates the simple image of laity against clergy.
   - The suppression was resented ("cum magno murmure inviti"). It worked by making public, self-governing penance illegal while explicitly permitting private penance.

4. **Recurrence and deliverance.**
   - Devotion visibly tracked the acute threat: "Cessante autem tempestate cessavit etiam peregrinatio et devotio" (li Muisis). Clyn says the pilgrims came "more from fear".
   - Venette: after deliverance the world was "not changed for the better", and charity grew cold. This supports the DELIV rival explanation for short-term devotional peaks.
   - Contemporaries counted recurrences: Clyn's later hand lists 1349, 1362, 1373, 1382, 1391. Knighton's 1361 "pestis secunda" fell "maxime juvenes et infantes". Islip's 1362 reissue of *Effrenata* is harsher. The 1388–89 gild returns and the *Craft of Dying* show the slow transfer of deathbed ministry to lay hands that Cohn associates with recurrence.

5. **Jews.**
   - Clerical opinion was divided. Herford and Venette reject the poisoning charge and name greed and debts. Li Muisis believes it. Clement VI rejects it on empirical grounds while keeping the penal theology.
   - Papal protection worked **only in the Comtat Venaissin** (Raynaldus; Königshofen).
   - The local church sided with the killers: the Bishop of Strasbourg at Benfeld agreed "to do away with the Jews", and confessors steered the plunder to the cathedral.
   - The bull of 20 Oct 1349 ties the flagellants to the shedding of Jewish blood.
   - This is the clearest case in the corpus of "church allied with perpetrators" at diocesan level against the papal centre.

6. **The Islamic comparison** (Ibn al-Khaṭīb) relocates the conflict to physicians versus jurists over contagion. Tradition is "reinterpreted" in the light of experience, with no lay penitential movement and no sacramental priesthood to contest.

### Surprises and corrections for the project files

- **Holmes 1896 does not print no. 2130.** The text is in Wilkins 1737, 2:745–46.
- **The date of no. 2130 is 10 Jan 1349** (iv Id. Jan.), not 17 Jan as in Gasquet and the guides.
- **Avesbury does not say the English "watched, not joined."** He only says the penance was done "in conspectu populi".
- **The anti-flagellant bull as printed in Raynaldus** (to Magdeburg) begins *Molesta nobis*, dated **20 Oct 1349** (XIII kal. Nov., anno viii). The name *Inter sollicitudines* is unverified.
- **The second Jewish bull is dated 26 Sept 1348** (VI kal. Oct.) in Raynaldus. The first is dated 4 July 1348 (IV non. Jul.).
- **The Tournai "decrees" are by the city governors, not the bishop.** The see was vacant: the bishop had died in June. The guide's "episcopal moral decrees" should be corrected to "civic ordinances".
- **Smith's *English Gilds* contains no explicit plague wording** in the ordinances I found. The "many post-1349 foundations" claim needs another source.
- **The Gutenberg *Decameron* (23700) is Payne's translation (1886), not Rigg's.**
