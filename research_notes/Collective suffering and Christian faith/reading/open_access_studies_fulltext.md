## 1. Dittmar & Meisenzahl (2020), plague and Reformation church ordinances

### Public Goods Institutions, Human Capital, and Growth (2020) — Dittmar, Jeremiah E., and Ralf R. Meisenzahl. 2020. "Public Goods Institutions, Human Capital, and Growth: Evidence from German History." *Review of Economic Studies* 87 (2): 959–996. Open access (CC BY-NC 4.0), LSE Research Online — https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/91195/3/Dittmar_public_goods_institutions_published.pdf
- Read: full (38 pp. PDF of the **published version**; page numbers below are REStud pagination). Supplementary appendices not read.
- Research question: why some German cities in the 1500s adopted Protestant "church ordinances" (*Kirchenordnungen*) that set up public welfare, schooling and secular administration of provision, and what that did to human capital and city growth.
- Data: 239 cities (Table 6); ordinances from Sehling; upper-tail human capital from the *Deutsche Biographie* 1420–1820; city population (Bairoch); plague outbreaks city-year from Biraben (1975); publications from the Universal Short Title Catalogue.
- Identification: diff-in-diff for human capital; for long-run outcomes, IV using the number of plague outbreaks 1500–1522 (up to the first ordinance), controlling for average plague 1350–1499 and quarter-century plague counts from 1350.
- Main estimates: first stage 0.14 (s.e. 0.02), F = 34.02 (Table 6, col. 1); range 0.11–0.14 across six specifications, F falls to 8.70 with principality fixed effects (col. 5). Second stage: a city with an ordinance was 1.61 log points larger in 1800 (col. 1; 1.61–2.30 across columns).
- What is measured vs. interpreted: the *measured* outcome is adoption by 1600 of a church ordinance (a law). The "weakened elites / increased demand" mechanism is narrative (historians cited), supported indirectly by plague raising religious and political printing (Table 9) and not shifting other political institutions (Table 8). The study does not measure belief, attendance or trust in the Catholic Church. Cities that became Protestant *without* an ordinance count as untreated.
- Summary: Plague shocks in 1500–1522 predict which cities adopted Protestant public-goods laws, and those cities later grew faster and attracted more educated people. The authors say plague discredited city elites who fled, and gave survivors a reason to want public provision of care, including care during plague. Crucially, plague did this only after the Reformation had put an institutional alternative on the table. Earlier plagues, including the Black Death, show no comparable institutional effect in German cities. The effect was concentrated in cities subject to lords and muted in free cities.
- Key passages:
  > "Shocks to religious and political competition drove the adoption of public goods institutions. The introduction of religious competition during the Protestant Reformation was a global shock to politics in central Europe. The collapse of the ideological monopoly of the Catholic Church created an opening for political entrepreneurs, who criticized the Catholic Church as a rent-seeking organization." — p. 960 — institutional trust (CH-FAIL framed as rent-seeker); alternatives.
  > "In the early 1500s, local plagues interacted with the global effects of religious competition. Plagues weakened incumbent elites and increased demand for public goods institutions. We use plague shocks as a source of exogenous variation in the adoption of church ordinances." — p. 960 — the sentence the review paraphrases; mechanism is the authors' framing.
  > "The adoption of these institutions reflected local politics, and was driven by popular political movements, not by local elites (Cameron, 1991)." — p. 960 — POW: change from below.
  > "Plague outbreaks led to the breakdown of civic order and discredited city elites, who often fled (Dinges, 1995; Isenmann, 2012). ... The public goods institutions developed by political entrepreneurs incorporated provision for healthcare, including care during plagues (Bugenhagen, 1885; Grell, 2002)." — p. 968 — CARE; failure of incumbents to stay and care.
  > "Experience with plague discredited elites, lowering the relative price of local political action. Before religious competition led to the articulation of institutional alternatives, these shocks did not lead to institutional change." — p. 983 — central for the model: suffering erodes trust in incumbents only where an organised alternative exists.
  > "Protestant reformers criticized past city council behaviour during plagues and advocated institutional change. For example, in 1533 Andreas Osiander scolded Nürnberg's city council for previously abandoning the city during plague outbreaks in his famous 'Plague Sermon' and authored a church ordinance." — p. 983 n. 52 — remedy control; desertion as delegitimiser.
  > "Almost all church ordinances contain provisions on directing priests to visit the sick and offer consolation. In contrast, Catholic theologians and statesmen, 'rejected public participation entirely or wanted to allow it in only very reduced measure' (Roeck, 1999, p. 286)." — p. 983 n. 53 — CARE built into the new church order.
  > "Similar dynamics are observed in contemporary research on AIDS in Africa, which shows that service provision drives conversion to a new religion (Trinitapoli and Weinreb, 2012)." — p. 984 n. 54 — PROV rival / CARE.
  > "each additional plague outbreak between 1500 and 1522 increases the propensity of adopting a law by 14 percentage points. The F-statistic on the excluded instrument is above 34. In Panel B, the point estimate of the second stage implies that a city with a law by 1600 was 1.61 log points larger in 1800 than a city without a law." — p. 986, Table 6 — the 14 pp figure the review cites (col. 1 of six; 0.11–0.14).
  > "We do find evidence that the plague effect on adoption was muted in free cities. This suggests that the effect of plagues on adoption was concentrated in cities subject to feudal lords, where the barriers to political change were higher." — p. 987 — POW: heterogeneity by political subordination.
  > "We find no systematic changes in city-level institutions in the 1300s associated with differential exposure to plagues in this era ... This suggests that the Black Death did not shape variation in city-level outcomes through institutional channels, at least in Germany." — p. 988 n. 63 — the Black Death null; supports the review's "early-sixteenth-century, not Black Death" caveat.
  > "The fact that plagues did not shift participatory institutions is to be expected, given that reformers supported local secular power structures and targeted the Catholic Church as a foreign, rent-seeking organization" — p. 990 — POL/institutional framing.
  > "We find religious and political publication increased significantly in response to plague shocks in the early 1500s." — p. 990; Table 9 (religious publications 2.03***, mean 1.46) — the only measured proxy for "competition".
- Robustness (as reported): plague IV defined to 1550 gives similar estimates (Table 10); early-1500s plagues uniquely predict 1800 population among 25-year plague counts (Table 7; but n. 64 concedes the 1500s and 1475–99 coefficients cannot be statistically distinguished); "plausibly exogenous" bounds (Conley et al.) in appendix.
- Limitations admitted: n. 64 (above); IV much larger than OLS, which the authors discuss as attenuation, treatment intensity, or heterogeneous returns (p. 986); cross-sectional long-run outcomes because pre-adoption population is often missing (p. 961 n. 6).
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - POW: for. The plague effect sits in cities under lords; adoption is driven "from below".
  - THEO / remedy control: for, indirectly. Reformers contrasted council desertion with ordinances that put care of the sick in a public, pastoral order.
  - Church role: for CH-FAIL → exit only when an alternative exists (p. 983). But the "institution" eroded is the old ecclesiastical-civic order; the study does not measure lay trust or faith.
  - CARE: strong (ordinances formalise visiting the sick; welfare).
  - DUR: n. 63 Black Death null vs. 1500s effect; the effect also persists through the 1520s–1540s for late adopters (Table 10). Recurrence matters only in conjunction with the Reformation.
  - TRANS: not tested.
  - Rivals: PROV is part of the mechanism (provision is what the new institution offers); POL is central (political entrepreneurs).
- Source-critical notes: economists' paper, a top journal, careful IV; the historical mechanism rests on a small set of secondary works (Dinges, Isenmann, Cameron, Roeck). "Public goods institutions" is the authors' label for Protestant church ordinances. A pre-publication version circulated as "State Capacity and Public Goods" (CEP DP 1418; FEDS 2016-028); the review cites the 2020 article.
- Usable in: A1 (the cleanest causal plague → institutional change result), A2 (recurrence plus available alternative; who owns the remedy).

## 2. Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama (2019), Black Death mortality and persecution

### Negative Shocks and Mass Persecutions (2019) — Jedwab, Remi, Noel D. Johnson, and Mark Koyama. 2019. "Negative Shocks and Mass Persecutions: Evidence from the Black Death." *Journal of Economic Growth* 24: 345–395. Published version posted on Koyama's site — https://markkoyama.github.io/Papers/Black_Death_Persecutions_JEG.pdf ; WP: MPRA Paper 78630 (6 March 2017, posted 21 April 2017) — https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/78630/1/MPRA_paper_78630.pdf (MPRA 77720 is an earlier posting of the same draft)
- Read: published version, full text (52 pp.; pages below are **JEG pagination** unless marked WP); WP 78630 sections 1, 4, 5 and conclusion (printed WP page numbers) for comparison. Web appendix not read.
- Research question: does a bigger negative shock raise or lower the chance that a minority is persecuted?
- Data: 124 Western European towns that had a Jewish community in 1347 and a recorded Black Death mortality rate (from 363 Jewish communities; 263 towns with mortality; 1,869 towns overall). Outcome: dummy for any persecution 1347–1352.
- Identification: OLS with controls; parallel pre-trends in persecution; IVs (market access to Messina conditional on general market access; months between first infection and October 1347).
- Main estimates: baseline mortality coefficient −0.009*** (s.e. 0.002), constant 0.831***, R² 0.12, N 124 (Table 1, row 1; p. 357). One s.d. of mortality (≈18.5 points) → 0.34 s.d. (≈17.1 points) lower persecution probability (p. 357).
- Summary: About half of Jewish communities were persecuted, but towns with higher plague mortality were *less* likely to persecute. The authors read this as two forces: scapegoating (stronger where antisemitic libels, recent pogroms or Easter/Christmastide timing primed blame) and economic complementarity (stronger where Jews were needed as lenders and traders). The liturgical-calendar result the review cites is in the **published** version as well as the WP. Proximity to the papal seat at Avignon removed the protective effect; bishops' seats showed only a weak, insignificant extra protection.
- Key passages:
  > "Negative shocks increase the likelihood that minorities are persecuted. But, as shocks become more severe, the persecution probability decreases if there are economic complementarities between majority and minority groups. The effects of shocks on persecutions are thus ambiguous." — Abstract, p. 345.
  > "The effect of mortality on the persecution probability, however, is negative, at − 0.009***. This effect is strong: a one standard deviation increase in mortality (≈ 18.5% points) is associated with a 0.34 standard deviation reduction in the likelihood of a persecution (≈ 17.1% points)." — p. 357.
  > "The protective effect was also weaker in cities first infected during Christmastide and Easter—when Christians historically blamed Jews for the death of Jesus—and stronger for Advent and Lent—when Christians were doing penance." — p. 347 (published). The WP wording (p. 2) is "weaker in cities first infected in January or April-May ... (Epiphany and Easter)—and stronger for December or February–March". — THEO: liturgical framing of blame.
  > "Advent Advent, the period before Christmas, is the beginning of the liturgical year. During that period, Christians are encouraged to do penance to prepare for the coming of Christ. Consistent with these motivations, in towns where December was the month of first infection, we find that the protective effect of Black Death mortality was reinforced (row 13, − 0.022**)." — p. 382 (§5.3.3, "The liturgical and agricultural calendars"); Table 8 rows 13–16: Dec −0.022**; Jan +0.016**; Feb–Mar −0.014***; Apr–May +0.011*.
  > "However, by then, 16 persecutions had already taken place in our data, including persecutions around Avignon. The Papal Bull also did not prevent the 42 persecutions in our data after September 1348. One reason for this was that the authority of Papacy had been weakened by the relocation to Avignon in 1309 ... Bishops and Archbishops had independent discretionary authority due the limitations of communication technologies. The low clergy, moreover, often held more antisemitic views (Cohen 1982)." — p. 375 — "the church" disaggregated: papacy, bishops, lower clergy.
  > "We find that close to Avignon there was no protective effect of mortality (row 4, 0.018***). ... Looking at cities that were the seats of bishops or archbishops, we find some evidence that the scapegoating effect was weakened, but not significantly so (row 5, − 0.007). This weak effect is consistent with the limited influence that the Pope had on local decisions." — p. 380.
  > "Note that there is no clear effect for April now (remember that Easter took place in late April in 1348)." — WP p. 28 n. 38 — month-by-month version of the Easter result is weaker.
  > "Nonetheless, Web Appendix Table A.12 shows there is a negative effect on plague reoccurrences on persecution probability (− 0.005*** when using 100 km; − 0.006 (p value: 0.23) when using 5 km). However, given the limitations of this analysis and the fact that plague reoccurrences are possibly endogenous, we do not pursue it further." — p. 367 — DUR.
  > "These results suggest that when there are latent biases against minorities, shocks can lead to these biases manifesting themselves as persecutions, with conspiracy theories one of mechanisms through which scapegoating spreads." — p. 391 (conclusion).
- Robustness: Messina IV −0.016*** (F 31.0); timing IV −0.028*** (F 33.2), both weaker with lat/long controls (F 4.3, 7.3) (WP pp. 17–18; same design in JEG); dropping outliers, alternative mortality measures, Conley SEs.
- Limitations admitted: small N (124); imputed infection month for 29 towns (p. 367); liturgical/agricultural readings overlap (the section is titled "liturgical and agricultural calendars"; Apr–May and October were also borrowing/repayment months, WP pp. 28–29).
- WP vs. published: the WP has a text slip on Lent ("in towns where February or March was the month of first infection, higher mortality conferred no protective effect (row 22, -0.014***)" — WP p. 27) which the published version corrects to "a stronger than usual protective effect" (p. 383).
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - THEO: for. Blame follows the liturgical theology of the season (deicide at Easter/Christmastide vs. penance in Advent/Lent). But it concerns blame of an outgroup, not the sufferers' own faith.
  - Church role: disaggregated. The papacy condemned the libel but could not control local action; near Avignon no protection; bishops' seats weakly protective (Western Europe sample). Contrast Finley & Koyama (Holy Roman Empire, below).
  - CARE / PROV: the complementarity channel is material dependence, not care.
  - DUR: plague recurrences weakly lower persecution; authors do not pursue.
- Source-critical notes: the persecution and mortality data come from Christakos et al., Encyclopaedia Judaica and similar compilations; mortality estimates are heaped (many 50%). Theological interpretation of months relies on secondary literature (Freudmann, Archer).
- Usable in: A1 (shock → scapegoating is non-monotonic), A2 (who is blamed; liturgical theology shapes blame).

## 3. Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama (2022), "The Economic Impact of the Black Death" — religion section

### The Economic Impact of the Black Death (2020 manuscript of 2022 JEL article) — Jedwab, Remi, Noel D. Johnson, and Mark Koyama. 2022. "The Economic Impact of the Black Death." *Journal of Economic Literature* 60 (1): 132–178. Version read: manuscript dated 4 August 2020 hosted by the AEA — https://assets.aeaweb.org/asset-server/files/12804.pdf
- Read: full abstract, introduction, §2.2 (social disorder), §4.5 "How Did The Plague Impact Religious Institutions?" and §5 conclusion in full; other sections skimmed. **Page numbers are those printed on the 2020 manuscript**, not JEL pagination (published version not accessed).
- Summary: A survey. On religion it is historiographical, not a causal estimate (as the review says). It lists "a decline in the authority of religious institutions" among long-run effects, attributes delegitimation to the plague's universality and the clergy's inability to protect, cites Green (2014) on "vibrant lay piety" filling a "spiritual vacuum", and links this with the Avignon papacy and the Schism. But it also records an "organic, non-institutionalized" collective piety, Church cooperation with health boards (citing Cohn 2018), the Bianchi movement supported by priests and bishops, and the absence of Black Death saints (plague saints only in the fifteenth century).
- Key passages:
  > "the demise of serfdom in Western Europe, a decline in the authority of religious institutions, and the emergence of stronger states." — Abstract, p. [title page].
  > "The Church was not only responsible for providing spiritual services, it also looked after the poor and the sick and was involved in education via monasteries, church schools, and universities." — p. 38 — CARE: the church as provider whose capacity was hit.
  > "The Black Death was initially perceived by many as punishment from God. Moreover the clergy were disproportionately hard hit by the Black Death (Christakos et al., 2005, p. 133-138). Several cardinals in Avignon died. As a result there was a shortage of trained clergy for decades." — p. 38.
  > "The immediate response to the plague could not be controlled by the Papacy. ... Movements like the flagellants were also religious in nature and out of the control of the Church ... But the universal nature of the plague and the inability of religious leaders to offer protection for it called into question the legitimacy of the Church (see Johnson and Koyama, 2019, p. 110-112)." — p. 38 — THEO/remedy control; CH-FAIL. This is the passage the review cites.
  > "In the longer run, historians argue that 'the impact of the Black Death seemed only to confirm the failures of the institutional Church. What emerged to fill the spiritual vacuum was a vibrant lay piety that, in time, encouraged further reform' (Green, 2014, 255)." — p. 38 — faith up / institution down (quoted second-hand from Green).
  > "There was an organic, non-institutionalized, religious response too: processions and other examples of collective piety such as fasts, singing, and prayer were organized to fight against the plague (see Cohn, 2018). ... One purpose of these processions was to bring people together as, over time, it became recognized that mutual aid was important in limiting exposure to disease. Nevertheless, historians find it notable that the Black Death was not associated with any saints, despite the fact that chroniclers report numerous priests risking their lives administrating to the sick." — p. 39.
  > "Not all of these movements were opposed to the Church. The Bianchi, in Florence, for instance, was a grassroots movement that promoted peace, and supported by local priests and bishops (Cohn, 2018)." — p. 39.
  > "Cohn (2018, 72) notes that 'the Church cooperated with Health Boards and concurred with the latest findings on contagion. New forms of piety and devotion were invented, such as prayers and litanies at precise hours of the day when, from the confines of quarantined homes, all could participate'." — p. 39 n. 35.
  > "In the 14th century there was no competitor to the Catholic Church. Cohn (2018, 86-88) then documents an increasing association between plague healing and saints during the 15th century." — p. 39 n. 36 — DUR; no alternative (compare Dittmar & Meisenzahl).
  > "(iv) there was a weakening in the political power of religious organizations." — p. 40 (conclusion).
- Evidence for / against: supports the faith-vs-institution split as a historians' claim; the same pages give counter-evidence (institutional adaptation, clergy who served the sick, cooperation with health boards). Lollards, Hussites and the Brethren of the Common Life are said to have "arose following the plague" and to be "seen as prefiguring the Protestant Reformation" (p. 39), which is not a causal claim.
- Source-critical notes: economic historians summarising historians (Green 2014; Cohn 2018); the delegitimation claim is sourced to their own book (Johnson & Koyama 2019, *Persecution and Toleration*). Small error in the manuscript: "Holy Roman Emperor Charles V" in 1349 (p. 13; it was Charles IV).
- Usable in: A1 (state of the field: delegitimation thesis and its counter-evidence), A2.

## 4. Bottan & Perez-Truglia (2015), US Catholic abuse scandals

### Losing My Religion (2015) — Bottan, Nicolas L., and Ricardo Perez-Truglia. 2015. "Losing My Religion: The Effects of Religious Scandals on Religious Participation and Charitable Giving." *Journal of Public Economics* 129: 106–119. Published version hosted by UCLA Anderson Review (Perez-Truglia's institution) — https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Perez-Truglia_2015_losing.religion.pdf
- Read: full (14 pp.; **JPubE pagination**). Online appendix not read.
- Note on the brief: NBER WP 17636 is *not* this paper (it is Lacetera, Macis & Slonim, "Rewarding Altruism?", 2011; downloaded and discarded). The published article says an earlier version circulated as "Religious Participation and Pro-Social Behavior" (p. 106 n.☆); an October 2011 SSRN draft is titled "Religious congregations, charitable giving and welfare" (not used).
- Research question: does a negative shock to religious participation (a scandal) change participation, belief, pro-social attitudes and charitable giving?
- Data: >3,000 scandal events 1980–2010 geocoded to parish zip codes; outcomes: religious employees and Catholic schools (zip), IRS itemised contributions (zip), charity filings, GSS 1994–2010 with county identifiers (raised-Catholic respondents).
- Identification: event study exploiting timing and location of scandals; pre-scandal placebo; placebo outcomes (non-Catholic religious schools).
- Main estimates: −3% long-run religious employees in the zip (≈ −9% of the Catholic sub-population) (p. 107); GSS: Catholic identification −14.2 pp short term, −10.4 pp long term (p. 113); attendance −12.2 pp long term (p. 114); belief in God +0.6 pp and afterlife +1.2 pp, insignificant (p. 114); charitable giving −1.3% (p. 107), $1.77–2.36 billion a year (p. 115).
- Summary: A scandal produces a lasting fall in Catholic affiliation, attendance and charitable giving in the zip code where it breaks, with small spillovers to adjacent zip codes. Beliefs (God, afterlife) and trust do not fall measurably. Catholics first join other denominations, but after about three years most report no religion. The authors favour a social-network mechanism for the fall in giving. The unit is the zip code (and county for GSS), **not** the diocese.
- Key passages:
  > "First, a scandal causes a significant and long-lasting decline in religious participation in the zip code where it occurs. Second, the decline in religious participation does not generate a statistically significant decline in religious beliefs, pro-social beliefs, and some commonly used measures of pro-social behavior." — Abstract, p. 106.
  > "Our event of interest is the scandal and not the abuse itself. Indeed, the vast majority of the scandals occurred decades after the abuses were allegedly committed." — pp. 106–107 — the shock is *disclosure* of institutional failure.
  > "Some Catholics join other religious denominations during the first three years after a scandal. But these individuals later end up with no religious affiliation." — p. 107 — migration to rivals is temporary.
  > "we find that a scandal causes a persistent 3% decline in religious participation in the zip code in which it occurs. This would be equivalent to a 9% decline in the Catholic sub-population." — p. 107.
  > "A scandal reduces the probability of declaring to be Catholic by 14.2 percentage points (p-value < 0.01) in the short term and by 10.4 percentage points (p-value < 0.01) in the long term. Consistent with Hungerman (2013), we find that most of decline in Catholic affiliation is offset by an increase in affiliation to other religious denominations during the year of the scandal and the following three years. However, this offsetting effect is temporary: after those first three years, the majority of the Catholics affected by the scandals report no religious affiliation." — pp. 113–114, Table 4.
  > "The estimates suggest that, in the long term, the scandals had a significant negative effect on church attendance of 12.2 percentage points ... This finding confirms that the scandals did not affect only 'nominal' Catholics, but also Catholics who actively participated in the congregation. ... There is also a negative long-term effect on the probability of praying, although it is not significant at the 10% level." — p. 114.
  > "The results indicate that, in the long term, a scandal increases the probability of believing in God by 0.6 percentage points, and increases the probability of believing in the afterlife by 1.2 percentage points. These two coefficients are positive (rather than the expected negative), small in magnitude and statistically insignificant. ... Due to the precision of the estimates, however, we cannot reject smaller effects on these outcomes." — p. 114 — faith/institution split.
  > "The effects increase in magnitude during the first couple of years after the scandal, and then they remain stable at that level. The affected outcomes do not revert to pre-scandal levels, even more than ten years after the occurrence of the scandal." — p. 107 — DUR: no recovery.
  > "Our preferred interpretation points to the role of social interactions." — p. 107 — giving falls because networks/solicitation are lost, not beliefs.
  > "Nevertheless, there are three important caveats. First, the effects on some of these outcomes are not estimated with a lot of precision. Second, Catholicism differs in key aspects from other religious denominations ... Third, we are studying changes in religious participation during adulthood, but deep beliefs may be more susceptible to changes in religious participation during childhood." — p. 117 — TRANS.
  > "Notably, the indirect cost of the scandals through the decline in giving is an order of magnitude higher than the direct costs of the scandals to the Catholic churches, such as the cost of lawsuit settlements." — p. 118.
- What "giving" means: IRS itemised charitable contributions of all kinds in the zip code (−1.3%), confirmed with contributions to social-service charities and a fall in social-service provision. It is not only church giving.
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - Church role (CH-FAIL / CH-ALLY with perpetrators): strong for exit from the institution; belief unchanged.
  - CARE: provision of social services falls with participation (PROV channel runs through the congregation).
  - DUR: effects persist >10 years.
  - TRANS: authors flag that childhood exposure may matter more (untested).
  - Rival "migration to rivals": only short-run.
- Source-critical notes: careful identification; belief results rest on GSS subsamples and are imprecise (authors say so). Applies to US Catholics.
- Usable in: A1, A4 (cleanest evidence for belonging-without-belief erosion after institutional failure).

## 5. Belloc, Drago & Galbiati (2014 working draft of 2016 QJE)

### Earthquakes, Religion, and Transition to Self-Government in Italian Cities (2014 draft) — Belloc, Marianna, Francesco Drago, and Roberto Galbiati. 2014. "Earthquakes, Religion, and Transition to Self-Government in Italian Cities." Working draft, 2 April 2014 (NBER conference paper). Published as *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 131 (4) (2016): 1875–1926 — https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f72494.pdf
- Read: full (43 pp. incl. tables; **WP page numbers**). The CESifo WP 5566 (2015) and the QJE version could not be downloaded (bot challenges); published numbers may differ (the QJE abstract mentions 121–122 cities, this draft about 60 episcopal cities plus a 47-city placebo sample).
- Research question: do religiosity shocks (earthquakes) slow the move from a bishop's rule to the commune?
- Data: ~61 Episcopal see cities of north-central Italy, 1000–1300, 10-year panel; 141 seismic events (DBMI04) split into damaging (MCS > 5) and "felt"; 47 non-episcopal cities as placebo; modern church counts.
- Identification: city and decade fixed effects, linear probability model; placebo in cities ruled by secular lords; randomised "fake earthquake" placebos.
- Main estimates: earthquake −0.0832*** (s.e. 0.0278) vs. a mean transition rate of 0.039 per decade (Table 3 col. 1; p. 23); damaging −0.0996***, felt-only −0.0579* (Table 4); non-episcopal cities −0.006 to −0.010, insignificant (Table 6); pooled quake×bishop −0.0408*** (Table 6 col. 7).
- What is measured vs. interpreted: measured = the decade in which a commune was formally established (statute or consuls attested). Not measured = religiosity or the bishop's authority. The religiosity channel is inferred from the felt-only effect, the bishop/non-bishop contrast, and a cross-section of **present-day** church counts on medieval quake counts (Table 5). The authors say they cannot test it directly (p. 29).
- Summary: Earthquakes delayed communal self-government by about a decade in cities ruled by bishops, even when quakes did no damage, and not in cities ruled by secular lords. The authors read earthquakes as signs of God's wrath that raised demand for the bishop's religious mediation and so shored up his political rule. The effect is short-lived (no more than ten years). Reconstruction-based explanations are rejected because neither rulers nor communes made transfers to victims.
- Key passages:
  > "For a panel of about 60 Episcopal see cities (governed by a bishop) over 300 years in the medieval northern-central Italy, we document that the occurrence of an earthquake retarded transition from feudal regime to commune." — Abstract, p. 1.
  > "First, shocks heightening people's religiosity, such as seismic events in the Middle Ages, strengthen the status quo regime and, as a consequence, retard institutional change. Second, this effect is the larger the stronger is the shock. Third, the effect lasts only in the short-run." — Abstract, p. 1 — DUR (short-run only).
  > "In the former, that will be the focus of our study, the bishops were at the same time political leaders, monopolists in the provision of religious services, and the supreme religious authorities." — p. 2 — POW/remedy control fused.
  > "During the Middle Ages in Western Europe, earthquakes represented mysterious and unforeseeable events men could only account for as manifestation of God's wrath. ... after an earthquake, peoples' common reaction was panic, consternation, and an immediate urge for reconciliation with God. This resulted in a sudden increase in participation in (and thus an increase in the demand for) religious services, such as collective prayers, processions, and fasts." — p. 3 — THEO.
  > "The mean of the dependent variable in our sample is 0.039. Therefore, the point estimate on the earthquake brings the probability of transition down to zero." — p. 23.
  > "the negative effect of a seismic event on the transition probability is concentrated in the first decade, with the coefficients being, if anything, positive (but not statistically significant) in the second and the third decade following an earthquake." — p. 23 — DUR.
  > "Providing empirical support to this hypothesis is no simple exercise given the absence of systematic data in the medieval period. A possibility is offered by observing that the number of churches in a given city is likely to capture the demand for religious services" — p. 24 — the religiosity proxy is today's stock of churches.
  > "In all the cases, there were no direct transfers from the political authorities to the citizens, and in general the main financial support for reconstruction lay in tax breaks." — p. 28 — PROV rival rejected.
  > "Since the bishop was the mediator between men and God and the monopolist in religious services, on the one hand, and the political leader in Episcopal see cities, on the other, the earthquake may be seen as resulting in a barrier to institutional change. With the available data we are not able to test this interpretation directly" — p. 29.
  > "There is a widespread tendency to look to God, his mediators, or charismatic political leaders for protection in periods of uncertainty and instability" — p. 30.
- Internal inconsistency: the abstract says the effect is "the larger the stronger is the shock" (p. 1) but the conclusion says the effect "is independent of the earthquake's intensity" (p. 29); Table 4 shows larger effects for damaging quakes.
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - POW: for. Disaster entrenches an incumbent who holds both the remedy (mediation with God) and political power.
  - THEO / remedy control: strong for. The bishop is "monopolist in the provision of religious services".
  - Church role: here the church *is* the ruling establishment, and suffering strengthens it in the short run. This is a counter-case to "failing establishment loses trust".
  - DUR: short-run only (≤ 10 years); no test of recurrence.
  - PROV: rejected (no transfers).
- Source-critical notes: the review's description ("measure delayed communal self-government"; mechanism inferred) is accurate for this draft. Dating of commune foundation from secondary sources; small N.
- Usable in: A1, A2 (who controls the remedy when the church is the government).

## 6. Bentzen (2021), prayer searches in the COVID-19 pandemic

### In Crisis, We Pray (2021) — Bentzen, Jeanet Sinding. 2021. "In Crisis, We Pray: Religiosity and the COVID-19 Pandemic." *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 192: 541–583. Published text via Europe PMC (PMC8557987), full-text XML — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8557987/fullTextXML
- Read: full text of the **published version**. The XML has no page numbers, so locators are section titles. Appendices not read.
- Research question: did COVID-19 raise religiosity worldwide, for whom, and why?
- Data: Google Trends search share for the topic "prayer", weekly for 107 countries (daily for 99), 2016–2020; COVID exposure markers (WHO declaration of 11 March 2020, first cases/deaths, anosmia searches, bans on gatherings).
- Identification: growth relative to 52 weeks earlier, holiday dummies, country trends and fixed effects; event timing (WHO declaration common to all countries).
- Main estimates: global prayer search share +29% in March–April 2020, +11% over 2020 vs. 2018–19 (Table 1); pandemic dummy +0.25*** (Table 3); panel persistence to about October 2020, then indistinguishable from zero (Table 2 col. 1); about +15% throughout 2020 in the Americas and Europe; no significant persistence in Asia and Africa from July.
- Summary: Prayer searches hit their highest level ever in March 2020, timed to the WHO declaration more than to local infections. The rise was in private prayer, not a substitute for closed churches: it began before closures, did not differ by lockdown periods, occurred on all weekdays, and searches for online church were about 100 times smaller. The rise was larger where prior religiosity was higher, and socio-economic "vulnerability" effects vanish once prior religiosity is controlled. Persistence was greater in Europe and the Americas; the author suggests that regions with recent epidemics (Asia, Africa) found COVID less surprising.
- Key passages:
  > "During the early months of the pandemic, Google searches for prayer relative to all Google searches rose by 30%, reaching the highest level ever recorded. ... Prayer searches remained 10% higher than previously throughout 2020, particularly so in Europe and the Americas." — Abstract.
  > "The increase is not merely a substitute for services in the physical churches that closed down to limit the spread of the virus. Instead, the rise is due to an intensified demand for religion: People pray to cope with adversity." — Abstract — faith without the institution.
  > "First, people use mainly their intrinsic religiosity (such as personal relation to God and private prayer) rather than their extrinsic religiosity (such as churchgoing) to cope with adversity. Second, the vast majority (98%) of Americans who pray, pray alone and not in church (Barna, 2017)." — Introduction.
  > "First, searches for prayer on the Internet started to rise already in January and February 2020, before the first church closures ... This indicates that prayer searches rose equally much in periods where churches were open compared to periods where churches were closed." — §Mechanisms.
  > "Column (1) replicates the result in column (5) of Table 1, but now revealing that persistence lasts until around October 2020, where-after the rise in prayer searches is statistically indistinguishable from zero." — §Timing, persistence — DUR.
  > "According to the literature, religion is used mostly to cope with unexpected disasters and less so with expected disasters. In modern times, Asia and Africa have been hit by more epidemics or disease outbreaks compared to Europe and the Americas. ... As a consequence, COVID-19 did not instigate the same use of religion for coping as it did in other parts of the globe." — §Timing, persistence — DUR/recurrence (author's conjecture).
  > "Thus, the majority of the intensified interest in prayer on the web was independent of the physical arrival of COVID-19, and instead occurred at the same point in time for all countries: When the WHO declared COVID-19 a pandemic." — §Prayer and the COVID-19 pandemic.
  > "Thus, the availability of religion as a coping tool seems to be more important than the 'need' for such a tool, where need is defined by economic and insecurity measures." — §Characteristics of those who pray more — TRANS (existing religiosity as the stock that crisis draws on).
  > "Earthquakes in areas often hit by earthquakes do not raise religiosity as much as earthquakes in areas less often hit (Bentzen, 2019)." — §Characteristics — DUR.
  > "By the end of 2020, one in ten Americans said their faith had been strengthened by the pandemic (Pew, 2021)." — Introduction.
  > "The future will show whether the rise in religiosity persists beyond 2020" — Conclusion.
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - THEO / devotion: for the "suffering → private devotion" track, independent of the institution.
  - Church role: the institution is bypassed, not delegitimised. Nothing on trust.
  - DUR: rise fades in the overall panel by October 2020; unexpectedness matters (recurrence dulls coping).
  - TRANS: crisis religiosity scales with pre-existing religiosity (stock effect).
- Source-critical notes: Google search shares are a proxy for interest, not practice; the "more than half the world prayed" figure is a back-of-the-envelope extrapolation from one Pew US survey. The abstract's "10% higher throughout 2020" is the global time series; the country panel shows fading by October.
- Usable in: A1, A4 (private-faith track separate from institutional participation).

## 7. Ager & Ciccone (2016 working paper of 2018 JEEA)

### Agricultural Risk and the Spread of Religious Communities (2016 WP) — Ager, Philipp, and Antonio Ciccone. 2016. "Agricultural Risk and the Spread of Religious Communities." Economics Working Paper 1432, Universitat Pompeu Fabra, updated October 2016 (July 2014). Published as *Journal of the European Economic Association* 16 (4) (2018): 1021–1068 — https://econ-papers.upf.edu/papers/1432.pdf
- Read: full text (88 pp. incl. tables and appendix; main text read in full; **WP page numbers**).
- Research question: are religious communities more widespread where populations face more common (uninsurable) risk, because congregations insure members against idiosyncratic risk?
- Data: US Census church members (1890) and seating capacity (1850–1890) for about 2,700 counties; rainfall variability 1895–2000 (PRISM) as a proxy for 19th-century risk; modern ARDA membership.
- Identification: cross-county with geographic and climatic controls; heterogeneity by agricultural share and growing-season rainfall; Altonji–Elder–Taber style stability to immigrant-origin and denomination controls.
- Main estimates: 1 s.d. more rainfall risk → about 10% more church members/seats per capita across all counties; 20% (1890) to 50% (1860) in more agricultural counties; insignificant for 1850; persistence ρ 0.36–0.5 to around 2000 (pp. 3, 30–32).
- What is measured vs. interpreted: measured = church membership/seating per capita. Insurance provision itself is *not* measured; the insurance mechanism is theory plus historical citation. They argue against coping because rainfall risk is persistent (not surprising) and coping literature concerns belief rather than membership.
- Summary: Where farmers faced more rainfall risk, more people belonged to churches, especially in agricultural counties and for growing-season rainfall. The authors read this as demand for the mutual insurance congregations provided, a demand for belonging rather than belief. More than a third of the risk-related difference persists into modern membership, which they attribute to intergenerational transmission.
- Key passages:
  > "Building on the idea that members of religious communities insure each other against some idiosyncratic risks, we argue that religious communities should be more widespread where populations face greater common risk." — Abstract, p. [ii].
  > "A 1-standard-deviation increase in rainfall risk is associated with about a 10-percent increase in total church members and seating capacity relative to population." — p. 3.
  > "Even today, almost 85 percent of those who attend religious services at least once a year believe that their congregation would help them in case of illness or some other difficult situation, according to the US General Social Survey" — p. 3 — CARE.
  > "Individuals in counties with greater rainfall variability should therefore not be taken by surprise by rainfall variability in the same way individuals in counties with, for example, greater earthquake risk might be taken by surprise by an earthquake. Moreover, the coping theory of religiosity appears to be mostly related to religious beliefs and spirituality not to church attendance and membership" — p. 5 — DUR (chronic vs. surprise); faith/institution split.
  > "Using regional data, Bentzen finds a robust positive association between earthquakes and a range of religious beliefs when controlling for individual and country characteristics. On the other hand, Bentzen finds no robust association between earthquakes and church attendance." — p. 7 (summarising Bentzen's 2015 WP version of "Acts of God?") — important second-hand evidence on Bentzen 2019.
  > "The tendency to participate in religious communities therefore appears to be transmitted intergenerationally by families or local communities" — p. 5 — TRANS.
  > "Among more agricultural counties, a 1-standard-deviation increase in rainfall risk was associated with an increase in membership in religious communities between 20 percent (in 1890) and 50 percent (in 1860)." — p. 32.
  > "We do not examine the effect of rainfall risk on the size of specific denominations, as there is no information on denominational differences in insurance provision and substitutability among denominations." — p. 27, footnote (PDF p. 29).
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - CH-OWN + CARE: for. A chronic, recurrent risk produces institutional belonging where the congregation is the sufferers' own insurer.
  - DUR: chronic, predictable risk raises membership (institution), whereas surprise shocks (earthquakes) raise belief (per Bentzen). This is a useful two-track distinction.
  - TRANS: >1/3 persistence over a century.
  - PROV rival: this *is* a provision mechanism (belonging bought by insurance); compatible with the "flour Christians" framing.
- Source-critical notes: 19th-century census membership counts are noisy (authors instrument 1890 membership with 1870 seatings for persistence); rainfall risk is measured after the outcome (assumes persistence, documented r ≈ 0.85–0.9).
- Usable in: A1, A2 (slavery-era US context: congregations as insurance), A3 (provision).

- Usable in: A1, A2 (church as political lord vs. church as spiritual authority).

## Synthesis

What the full texts collectively show
1. **Shocks strengthen religious authorities when those authorities hold political power and the remedy, and erode them only when an alternative exists.** Belloc et al.: earthquakes delay communes only where the bishop rules (short-run). Dittmar & Meisenzahl: plague topples incumbents only once the Reformation supplies an alternative; no Black Death institutional effect in German cities (p. 988 n. 63).
2. **Faith and institutional participation move separately.** Bottan & Perez-Truglia: scandal → affiliation −10.4 pp, attendance −12.2 pp, belief unchanged. Bentzen 2021: private prayer up, institution bypassed. Ager & Ciccone (citing Bentzen's WP): surprise shocks raise belief, not attendance; chronic risk raises membership via insurance.
3. **Duration/recurrence cuts both ways.** Surprise produces devotion (Bentzen); chronic risk produces belonging (Ager & Ciccone); repeated epidemics dull the coping response (Bentzen 2021 on Asia/Africa); Belloc et al.'s effect lasts under a decade; scandal effects do not revert after ten years (Bottan & Perez-Truglia).
4. **"The church" must be disaggregated.** JJK 2019: papacy condemned the libel but could not stop pogroms; near Avignon no protection; bishops' seats weakly protective in Western Europe. Finley & Koyama: prince-bishops in the Empire behaved as rent-seeking lords (pogroms more intense), explicitly not a religiosity effect.
5. **Transmission is the long-run engine.** Voigtländer & Voth (local persistence over six centuries, broken by migration/trade); Ager & Ciccone (>1/3 persistence of risk-induced membership); Voas & Chaves (cohort replacement; non-religious upbringing increasingly sticks).

Surprises
- Bottan & Perez-Truglia find that scandal-driven moves to other denominations are temporary: after three years most leavers report no religion.
- The JJK liturgical-calendar result is in the published 2019 article, not only the WP. The authors frame it alongside an agricultural-credit alternative.
- Bentzen 2021 explicitly tests and rejects the idea that prayer searches rose *because* churches closed.
- Frick & Simmons 2017 is an aggregate time-series letter, not a panel or quasi-experiment, and already reports the Catholic→Protestant spillover.
