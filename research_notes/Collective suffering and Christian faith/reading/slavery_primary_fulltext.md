## 1. Frederick Douglass, *Narrative* (1845)

### Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass (1845) — Douglass, Frederick. 1845. *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave. Written by Himself.* Boston: Anti-Slavery Office. Project Gutenberg eBook #23 — https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/23/pg23.txt
- Read: Preface, chs. II, IX, X (the religion-bearing portions) and the Appendix in full; remaining chapters read by keyword pass (religio*, Christian, pray*, Sabbath, Methodist, class-leader). ~245 KB file. No printed page numbers in this edition: locators by chapter.
- Summary: Douglass's autobiography to his escape (1838). Religion enters at four points: (1) the slave songs of the Lloyd plantation, read as complaint and prayer for deliverance (ch. II); (2) master Thomas Auld's 1832 Methodist camp-meeting conversion, which made him crueller and gave him "religious sanction" (ch. IX); (3) the breaking-up of slave Sabbath schools by white Methodist class-leaders, and Douglass's own clandestine Sabbath school of "over forty scholars" (chs. IX–X); (4) the Appendix, which distinguishes "the Christianity of Christ" from "the slaveholding religion of this land" and indicts northern as well as southern churches "in union with slaveholders". The text is a two-track model in miniature: intense personal and communal devotion inside a self-run space, total distrust of the white church as an institution.
- Key passages:
  > "they breathed the prayer and complaint of souls boiling over with the bitterest anguish. Every tone was a testimony against slavery, and a prayer to God for deliverance from chains." — ch. II — devotion; THEO (deliverance theology in song); remedy located in God, not master.
  > "Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy. The songs of the slave represent the sorrows of his heart; and he is relieved by them, only as an aching heart is relieved by its tears." — ch. II — suffering → song; warns against reading devotion as contentment.
  > "In August, 1832, my master attended a Methodist camp-meeting held in the Bay-side, Talbot county, and there experienced religion. ... after his conversion, he found religious sanction and support for his slaveholding cruelty." — ch. IX — CH-ALLY; revival religion of the master intensifies domination.
  > "His house was the preachers' home. They used to take great pleasure in coming there to put up; for while he starved us, he stuffed them." — ch. IX — CH-ALLY; material alliance of clergy and master.
  > "We slaves loved Mr. Cookman. We believed him to be a good man. We thought him instrumental in getting Mr. Samuel Harrison, a very rich slaveholder, to emancipate his slaves" — ch. IX — counter-evidence: trust is person-specific; a white minister seen as pro-emancipation was loved.
  > "We met but three times, when Mr. West and Mr. Fairbanks, both class-leaders, with many others, came upon us with sticks and other missiles, drove us off, and forbade us to meet again." — ch. IX — CH-ALLY; church officers police Black literacy/worship.
  > "I assert most unhesitatingly, that the religion of the south is a mere covering for the most horrid crimes ... For of all slaveholders with whom I have ever met, religious slaveholders are the worst." — ch. X — institutional distrust at its sharpest.
  > "I look back to those Sundays with an amount of pleasure not to be expressed. They were great days to my soul." — ch. X (Freeland's) — CH-OWN: the self-run Sabbath school as the site of devotion.
  > "I am almost ready to ask, 'Does a righteous God govern the universe? and for what does he hold the thunders in his right hand, if not to smite the oppressor, and deliver the spoiled out of the hand of the spoiler?'" — ch. X — theodicy strain; but framed as a demand on God, not abandonment.
  > "between the Christianity of this land, and the Christianity of Christ, I recognize the widest possible difference ... I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land." — Appendix — THE key two-track statement: faith retained, institution rejected.
  > "The man who robs me of my earnings at the end of each week meets me as a class-leader on Sunday morning, to show me the way of life, and the path of salvation." — Appendix — perpetrator controls the means of grace (remedy control).
  > "The slave auctioneer's bell and the church-going bell chime in with each other ... Revivals of religion and revivals in the slave-trade go hand in hand together. The slave prison and the church stand near each other." — Appendix — CH-ALLY.
  > "I mean by the religion of this land, that which is revealed in the words, deeds, and actions, of those bodies, north and south, calling themselves Christian churches, and yet in union with slaveholders." — Appendix — institutional criterion explicit: complicity ("in union with").
- Evidence for / against model elements:
  - POW: for — the master controls Sunday, literacy and the class meeting; worship in the master's frame is coercive.
  - THEO / remedy control: for — Douglass separates the gospel (remedy) from its white custodians; songs and Sabbath school put the remedy in slaves' hands.
  - Church role: strongly for CH-ALLY driving distrust; CH-OWN (Sabbath school at a free Black man's house) driving devotion. Mild against: the white Methodist Cookman is loved, so distrust targets complicity, not whiteness per se.
  - CARE: the Sabbath school is literacy care; the white church actively destroys it.
  - DUR / TRANS: little direct evidence; Douglass's sense that the songs "still follow me" shows durable memory.
  - DELIV: Douglass's own faith frame is a deliverance hope ("deliver the spoiled out of the hand of the spoiler"), but there is no conversion pattern keyed to emancipation (he writes before it).
  - POL: the Appendix is a political document aimed at northern churches.
- Source-critical notes: written for an abolitionist audience; Garrison and Phillips prefaces; the Appendix itself shows Douglass anticipating the charge of "infidel" from northern clergy. Anticlericalism is partly strategic (the abolitionist "come-outer" campaign against churches in fellowship with slaveholders). Personal, not systematic.
- Usable in: Article 1 (two-track mechanism), Article 2 (slavery case), Article 3 (institutional distrust/complicity).

## 2. Harriet Jacobs, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl* (1861)

### Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl (1861) — Jacobs, Harriet A. ["Linda Brent"]. 1861. *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl. Written by Herself.* Edited by L. Maria Child. Boston: Published for the Author. Project Gutenberg eBook #11030 — https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11030/pg11030.txt
- Read: chs. XII ("Fear of Insurrection") and XIII ("The Church and Slavery") in full; other chapters by keyword pass. ~478 KB file. No printed page numbers: locators by chapter.
- Summary: Edenton, North Carolina. After Nat Turner's revolt (Aug 1831) white musters and patrols terrorise the free and enslaved Black population; the slaves' own "little church in the woods" is demolished and they are confined to white-church galleries. Slaveholders then decide to give "enough of religious instruction to keep them from murdering their masters": the Episcopal Rev. Pike preaches obedience. The enslaved laugh at him and go off "to enjoy a Methodist shout". A slave-trading constable who whips church members is the Methodist class leader. A different, sympathetic Episcopal rector draws many enslaved people to church, and his white parishioners turn against him. Jacobs teaches "uncle Fred", aged 53, to read the Bible in secret. Dr Flint joins the Episcopal church for social position and persecutes her worse afterward.
- Key passages:
  > "The slaves begged the privilege of again meeting at their little church in the woods, with their burying ground around it. It was built by the colored people, and they had no higher happiness than to meet there and sing hymns together, and pour out their hearts in spontaneous prayer. Their request was denied, and the church was demolished." — ch. XII — CH-OWN destroyed by POW after a crisis; direct removal of self-controlled space.
  > "After the alarm caused by Nat Turner's insurrection had subsided, the slaveholders came to the conclusion that it would be well to give the slaves enough of religious instruction to keep them from murdering their masters." — ch. XIII — mission as social control (cf. C. C. Jones below).
  > "His text was, 'Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, in singleness of your heart, as unto Christ.'" — ch. XIII — THEO imposed by the dominant group.
  > "We went home, highly amused at brother Pike's gospel teaching" — ch. XIII — the audience rejects the messenger and the message.
  > "the slaves left, and went to enjoy a Methodist shout. They never seem so happy as when shouting and singing at religious meetings. Many of them are sincere, and nearer to the gate of heaven than sanctimonious Mr. Pike" — ch. XIII — exit from white-controlled service into self-run worship: the two-track pattern in one evening.
  > "The class leader was the town constable—a man who bought and sold slaves, who whipped his brethren and sisters of the church at the public whipping post" — ch. XIII — CH-ALLY embodied in one office-holder.
  > "'My Lord and Master, help me! My load is more than I can bear. God has hid himself from me, and I am left in darkness and misery.' ... 'They've got all my children. Last week they took the last one.'" — ch. XIII — suffering producing a sense of divine absence (a rare erosion-of-faith voice), spoken inside a class meeting.
  > "I saw that constable class leader become crimson in the face with suppressed laughter" — ch. XIII — institutional representative mocks suffering → distrust.
  > "'Ole Satan's church is here below. Up to God's free church I hope to go.'" — ch. XIII (sung by the congregation; repeated at chapter end) — the enslaved theologise the earthly church as Satan's: institution rejected, God retained.
  > "Many colored people, hitherto unaccustomed to attend church, now gladly went to hear the gospel preached. The sermons were simple, and they understood them. Moreover, it was the first time they had ever been addressed as human beings." — ch. XIII — counter-evidence/qualification: a white-run church gains Black attendance when the minister is perceived as on their side.
  > "'Honey, it 'pears when I can read dis good book I shall be nearer to God.'" — ch. XIII (uncle Fred) — devotion via clandestine literacy.
  > "There are thousands, who, like good uncle Fred, are thirsting for the water of life; but the law forbids it, and the churches withhold it." — ch. XIII — institution withholds the remedy.
  > "There is a great difference between Christianity and religion at the south." — ch. XIII — parallel to Douglass's Appendix.
  > "'Yes, Linda,' said he. 'It was proper for me to do so. I am getting in years, and my position in society requires it'" — ch. XIII (Dr Flint on joining the church) — church membership as status for perpetrators.
- Evidence for / against:
  - POW / CH-ALLY: strongly for — post-revolt repression closes Black-run worship and replaces it with obedience preaching.
  - THEO: for — the enslaved reject the "servants obey" theology and keep a Satan's-church / God's-free-church theology.
  - CH-OWN: for — the demolished woods church; the Methodist shout.
  - Qualification: the sympathetic rector attracts Black worshippers. So distrust is conditional on perceived alignment, not on institutional control alone.
  - Erosion: the bereaved mother ("God has hid himself from me") shows that suffering can erode felt faith, at least momentarily.
  - DUR / recurrence: the crackdown after Turner shows repression as a recurrent pattern after each revolt scare.
  - DELIV / PROV: no direct evidence.
- Source-critical notes: pseudonymous; edited by L. Maria Child; aimed at northern white women; the sexual-exploitation theme dominates. The religion chapter uses a satirical register designed for abolitionist readers. Edenton is a town setting, not a plantation.
- Usable in: Articles 1, 2, 3 (repression after revolt; obedience catechesis; two-track).

## 3. Absalom Jones & Richard Allen, *Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People* (1794)

### A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, during the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia (1794) — Jones, Absalom, and Richard Allen. 1794. *A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, during the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia, in the Year 1793: and a Refutation of Some Censures, Thrown upon Them in Some Late Publications.* Philadelphia: printed for the authors by William W. Woodward. Archive.org item b32886561 (Wellcome copy) — https://archive.org/download/b32886561/b32886561_djvu.txt
- Read: full (pp. 3–28, including the mayor's certificate, "An Address to those who keep Slaves", "To the People of Colour", "A short Address to the Friends of Him who hath no Helper", and the closing hymn). ~54 KB OCR. Page numbers from running heads (some garbled; sequence checked).
- Summary: In September 1793 the city's papers asked "people of colour" to nurse the yellow-fever sick, on the assumption that Black people were immune. Jones, Allen and "the elders of the African church" organised nursing, carting and burial. They bled "upwards of eight hundred" under Dr Rush's instructions, and some Black prisoners were freed to nurse at Bush Hill. Mathew Carey's pamphlet then accused Black nurses of extortion and plunder. The authors rebut it with a cash account (out of pocket £177 9s 8d), counter-examples of white theft and cruelty, and the burial figures: 67 Black burials in 1792 against 305 in 1793. They add a mayoral certificate and three addresses. The first is to slaveholders, built on Exodus: God is "the first pleader of the cause of slaves". The second is to people of colour, counselling patience, affection to masters and trust in God. The third thanks the abolitionists.
- Key passages:
  > "we found a freedom to go forth, confiding in him who can preserve in the midst of a burning fiery furnace, sensible that it was our duty to do all the good we could to our suffering fellow mortals." — p. 3 — THEO (Daniel 3 trust), CARE: an epidemic mobilises a Black religious community.
  > "The Lord was pleased to strengthen us, and remove all fear from us, and disposed our hearts to be as useful as possible." — p. 4 — devotion intensifies through service.
  > "two thirds of the persons, who rendered these essential services, were people of colour, who, on the application of the elders of the African church, (who met to consider what they could do for the help of the sick) were liberated" — p. 5 — CH-OWN + CARE: the new Black church (Free African Society / African Church) is the organising body.
  > "we think, that when a physician was not attainable, we have been the instruments, in the hand of God, for saving the lives of some hundreds of our suffering fellow mortals." — p. 5 — remedy control: the community itself is God's instrument.
  > "We can with certainty assure the public that we have seen more humanity, more real sensibility from the poor blacks, than from the poor whites." — p. 10 — moral inversion; community self-respect.
  > "Thus were our services extorted at the peril of our lives, yet you accuse us of extorting a little money from you." — p. 15 — institutional (civic) betrayal: exploited, then blamed.
  > "In 1792, there were 67 of our colour buried, and in 1793 it amounted to 305; thus the burials among us have increased more than fourfold" — pp. 15–16 — quantitative evidence of disproportionate suffering, borne while serving whites.
  > "Few have been the whites that paid attention to us while the black were engaged in the other's service." — p. 15 — care asymmetry.
  > "God and a soldier, all men do adore, / In time of war, and not before; / When the war is over, and all things righted, / God is forgotten, and the soldier slighted." — p. 20 — the authors' own reading of crisis religiosity and post-crisis ingratitude.
  > "We have many unprovoked enemies, who begrudge us the liberty we enjoy, and are glad to hear of any complaint against our colour" — p. 13 — POW.
  > "We wish you to consider, that God himself was the first pleader of the cause of slaves." — p. 24 — THEO: God sides with the enslaved (Exodus, Pharaoh "destroyed by the protector and avenger of slaves", p. 24).
  > "Our hearts have been sorrowful for the late bloodshed of the oppressors, as well as the oppressed, both appear guilty of each others blood" — p. 25 — Saint-Domingue; a warning to slaveholders.
  > "as your hearts are inclined to serve God, you will feel an affectionate regard towards your masters and mistresses ... and if they are otherwise you will have the favour and love of God dwelling in your hearts, which you will value more than any thing else ... and no master can deprive you of it" — p. 26 — an interior theology of suffering: faith as the one possession beyond the master's reach.
  > "here the power of the most cruel master ends, and all sorrow and tears are wiped away." — pp. 26–27 — eschatological remedy.
- Evidence for / against:
  - CH-OWN + CARE: strongly for. The epidemic is met through a newly autonomous Black religious body (the African Church elders) that delivers care, and the authors read their survival and service as God's empowerment. This is the clearest "suffering intensifies faith where the community controls the remedy" text in the corpus.
  - Institutional distrust: directed at the civic establishment (Carey's censures, white neglect), not at God. Note the timing: this is 1794, after the St George's walkout (see Allen 1833 below).
  - POW: free Black people in a hostile white city; dignity is defended in print.
  - THEO: double register. To slaveholders: an Exodus God who avenges slaves. To the enslaved: patience, affection to masters, interior freedom. This is the remedy-in-God's-hands pattern, with escape through gradual abolition.
  - PROV: the community is the provider, not the recipient, of relief. That runs against a "relief causes religiosity" rival.
  - DUR: a single acute epidemic; the proverb anticipates post-crisis forgetting of God.
- Source-critical notes: an apologetic pamphlet against Carey, addressed to white Philadelphia. The tone is conciliatory and the authors seek the mayor's certificate. The counsel to the enslaved to show "affectionate regard" to masters is shaped by the need for respectability and by the gradual-abolition context. Both authors were formerly enslaved Methodist preachers.
- Usable in: Article 1 (an epidemic in which the church is the community's own institution and delivers care), Article 2 (pivot text linking the Black Death–type shock to the slavery case), Article 4 (comparison with plague texts).

## 4. Nat Turner, *The Confessions of Nat Turner* (1831)

### The Confessions of Nat Turner (1831) — Turner, Nat, and Thomas R. Gray. 1831. *The Confessions of Nat Turner, the Leader of the Late Insurrection in Southampton, Va.* Baltimore: Lucas & Deaver. Project Gutenberg eBook #15333 — https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15333/pg15333.txt
- Read: full (Gray's "To the Public", the court certificate, the "Confession", Gray's closing remarks, the trial record and the list of the dead). ~72 KB. No page numbers: locators by section.
- Summary: Gray, a white lawyer, records Turner's statement of 1–3 November 1831 in jail. Turner tells of childhood marks of prophecy, affirmed by his parents and his "very religious" grandmother. He describes fasting and prayer, and the Spirit speaking "at my plough" with "Seek ye the kingdom of Heaven". He saw visions of white and black spirits in battle and blood on the corn. He and a white man (Brantley) baptised themselves "by the Spirit" after "the white people would not let us be baptised by the church". On 12 May 1828 he heard that "the Serpent was loosened" and he must "fight against the Serpent". He read the February 1831 eclipse as a sign, and the revolt followed on 21–22 August. Gray calls him "a complete fanatic".
- Key passages:
  > "My grand mother, who was very religious, and to whom I was much attached—my master, who belonged to the church, and other religious persons who visited the house ... remarked I had too much sense to be raised, and if I was, I would never be of any service to any one as a slave" — Confession — TRANS: religious formation by kin (grandmother, parents) and by whites.
  > "As I was praying one day at my plough, the spirit spoke to me, saying, 'Seek ye the kingdom of Heaven and all things shall be added unto you.' Question—what do you mean by the Spirit. Ans. The Spirit that spoke to the prophets in former days" — Confession — direct, unmediated revelation: remedy control seized from the institution.
  > "the Spirit appeared to me and said I had my wishes directed to the things of this world ... and that I should return to the service of my earthly master—'For he who knoweth his Master's will, and doeth it not, shall be beaten with many stripes'" — Confession — the master's proof-text (cf. Douglass ch. IX) reappears inside Turner's own revelation, then is overturned.
  > "And the negroes found fault, and murmurred against me, saying that if they had my sense they would not serve any master in the world." — Confession — community pressure toward resistance.
  > "I saw white spirits and black spirits engaged in battle, and the sun was darkened—the thunder rolled in the Heavens, and blood flowed in streams" — Confession — apocalyptic THEO of suffering → judgement.
  > "and when the white people would not let us be baptised by the church, we went down into the water together, in the sight of many who reviled us, and were baptised by the Spirit" — Confession — exclusion by the white church → self-administered sacrament (CH-FAIL/ALLY → autonomy).
  > "the Spirit instantly appeared to me and said the Serpent was loosened, and Christ had laid down the yoke he had borne for the sins of men, and that I should take it on and fight against the Serpent, for the time was fast approaching when the first should be last and the last should be first." — Confession (12 May 1828) — THEO: the yoke and the remedy pass to the oppressed.
  > "Ques. Do you not find yourself mistaken now? Ans. Was not Christ crucified." — Confession — suffering and defeat do not falsify faith; crucifixion frame.
  > "He is a complete fanatic, or plays his part most admirably." — Gray's remarks — mediator's framing.
  > "the expression of his fiend-like face when excited by enthusiasm ... yet daring to raise his manacled hands to heaven, with a spirit soaring above the attributes of man; I looked on him and my blood curdled in my veins." — Gray's remarks — white fear of Black prophetic religion.
- Evidence for / against:
  - THEO / remedy control: strongly for. Faith intensifies when revelation is direct and the sacrament is self-administered, and the remedy (judgement) is taken into the believer's hands.
  - CH-FAIL / CH-ALLY: refusal of baptism by "the church" pushes the move to autonomy.
  - TRANS: kin-transmitted prophetic identity.
  - POW: the subsequent repression (see Jacobs ch. XII–XIII and Jones 1842 below) shows the dominant group's response: closing Black-run worship and supplying obedience catechesis.
  - DELIV rival: a deliverance theology, but apocalyptic and active, not a reward of external emancipation.
- Source-critical notes: heavily mediated by Gray, who published it for profit and to reassure white readers that the revolt was local and fanatic-driven. The questions and answers are Gray's framing. The exact wording of Turner's speech cannot be guaranteed. The religious content is nonetheless consistent with other testimony on Turner's preaching.
- Usable in: Articles 1, 2 (prophetic intensification; exclusion from sacraments → autonomy), 3.
