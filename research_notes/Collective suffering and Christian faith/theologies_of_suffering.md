# Theologies of Suffering, Catastrophe, and Plague: From Scripture to Contemporary Contextual Theologies

Scope note: these notes are the "theologies of suffering" section of a literature review on why collective suffering sometimes strengthens Christian faith and sometimes erodes trust in religious institutions. The cases are the Black Death (1347–1351), enslaved African Americans (1619–1877), and other peoples. The main contrast is between two kinds of theology. **(A) Retributive/penitential theologies** read suffering as divine punishment for collective sin. They give power to the institution that mediates repentance and later expose it to blame. **(B) Solidarity/lament theologies** place God with the sufferer (Exodus, the crucified Christ, lament). They can sustain faith while taking legitimacy away from an oppressor's church.

## Q1. Biblical foundations: retribution versus lament, Job, Exodus, the Suffering Servant, Pauline co-suffering, apocalyptic

### Takeaway
The Hebrew Bible holds a deliberate argument within itself. Deuteronomistic retribution ("you suffer because you sinned") stands against the protest literature of Job, Lamentations, the lament psalms and Qoheleth, which refuse that equation or leave it open. Modern scholarship since Westermann and Brueggemann treats lament as a covenantal form of speech that **redistributes power toward the sufferer**. When the church loses lament, it backs the status quo. This is the biblical root of the analytic contrast between the two types.

### Inferences
- The canon contains both Type A and Type B resources. The question for the article is therefore *which canonical voice a community made dominant under catastrophe*, and *who controlled that choice*, rather than "what the Bible says."
- Brueggemann's essay supplies a mechanism that can be tested: communities and institutions that suppress lament in favour of praise or penitence should show weaker capacity to voice grievance against power. Communities that keep lament (the spirituals; Katongole's East Africa) can sustain faith *and* make institutional critique.

## Q2. Early church and plague: Cyprian, Dionysius, Stark, Harper, Augustine, Gregory the Great

### Takeaway
Third-century bishops framed plague in two ways at once. Cyprian's *De mortalitate* treats plague as a test that separates true from false Christians and as release into immortality, which is a strongly faith-intensifying frame. Dionysius praises Christian nursing as "equal to martyrdom." Stark's sociological reading turns this into a mechanism: mutual aid raised survival rates and network-driven conversion. Harper supplies the epidemiological background. The ancient material shows plague *strengthening* a minority church that did **not** control the means of penance for the whole society and that could contrast its own behaviour with pagan flight.

- The Cyprianic/Dionysian frame combines **eschatological consolation** with **practical solidarity** and an **apologetic contrast** against pagan neighbours. It strengthens faith *and* trust in the (persecuted, minority) church, because that church is not the one accused of failing.
- By the late sixth century, episcopal plague litanies make the clergy the brokers of collective penance (Type A). This sets up the medieval pattern in which the institution's authority depends on whether its intercession visibly works.
- Stark's thesis is sociological and contested. Scholars dispute ancient demography and the size of mortality differences. Cite it as a hypothesis about *mechanisms* (care, networks), not established fact.

## Q3. Medieval: penitential theology, the Black Death, purgatory, Julian of Norwich, Aquinas, late-medieval Passion piety

### Takeaway
Black Death sources overwhelmingly read the plague as **divine vengeance for sin** (Horrox). The responses were penitential processions, flagellants, scapegoating (anti-Jewish violence) and intensified investment in purgatory and intercession (Le Goff). The papacy tolerated lay penitential enthusiasm until the flagellants bypassed priestly mediation. Clement VI's bull of 20 October 1349 condemned them as a menace to the priesthood. This is a clear instance of Type A empowering and then threatening the institution. At the same time, late-medieval Passion piety (Ross, Bynum) and Julian of Norwich offered a Type B counter-current: a wounded, grieving, merciful God and "all shall be well."

### Inferences
- **Mechanism for the Black Death case.** A penitential explanation (Horrox) puts the institution at the centre as broker of repentance (processions, masses, indulgences, purgatorial intercession; Le Goff). When repeated waves (1348, 1361, 1369…) defeat intercession, and clergy die or flee, the same frame turns into an **indictment** of clerical failure. The flagellant episode (Clement VI, 1349) shows the institution acting to keep its monopoly on mediating penance once lay penitents claimed direct access, including "letters from heaven" that bypassed Rome.
- The Type B current inside late-medieval Catholicism (Ross's "grieving God," Bynum's blood piety, Julian) shows that intensified *devotion* could coexist with diminished *institutional* trust. Devotion to the suffering Christ did not require clerical mediation in the same way. This fits the pre-Reformation pattern of lay piety that was intense but critical of the clergy. (This is my inference; it is not a direct claim of these sources.)

## Q4. Reformation and early modern: Luther, Zwingli, Calvin, providentialism, Leibniz and Voltaire

### Takeaway
Reformers kept a strong providentialism but changed the *agent of mediation*. Plague became a call to vocation and neighbour-love (Luther) and to personal lament and trust (Zwingli), rather than to purchased intercession. Walsham shows English providentialism reaching every layer of society, not only Puritans. Judgment-readings of disaster thus survived the Reformation and were spread by print and preaching. Leibniz's *Théodicée* (1710) and the 1755 Lisbon earthquake mark the turn to philosophical "theodicy" proper, which twentieth-century critics (Q5) reject.

### Inferences
- The Reformation did not abolish Type A. It **moved** it. Disaster still read as judgment (Walsham) now pointed to moral and civic reform administered by magistrates and preachers, not to purgatorial intercession. - Lisbon (1755) moves the question from "what sin caused this?" to "can God be justified?" (theodicy). Twentieth-century "anti-theodicy" writers (Q5) reject this move as morally obscene in the face of mass suffering.

## Q5. Modern theology: the suffering God, liberation, political theology, and the critique of theodicy

### Takeaway
Twentieth-century theology turned decisively toward **divine co-suffering**. Key moments: Kitamori's "pain of God" (1946); Bonhoeffer's "only the suffering God can help" (1944); Moltmann's *Crucified God* (1972); Sölle's critique of "theological sadism"; Sobrino and Ellacuría's "crucified people"; Gutiérrez on Job; and Metz's *memoria passionis*. In parallel, an **anti-theodicy** current (Surin, Tilley, Hauerwas, Swinton, Hart) argues that theoretical justifications of evil are morally suspect, and that the proper Christian response is lament, practice and resistance. These are the main Type B resources.

## Q6. Contextual theologies of suffering (Black, womanist, Asian, Taiwanese, minjung, Dalit, Palestinian, Native American, African, Holocaust)

### Takeaway
Contextual theologies mostly radicalise Type B. God is located among the oppressed (Exodus, the cross, the lynching tree, *han*, *minjung*, the "crucified people," the Dalit Christ). That location then becomes the ground for **delegitimising the church of the oppressor**. The womanist (Williams) and humanist (Jones, Pinn) critiques add a third position: even "redemptive suffering" is suspect. That critique bears on whether faith survives at all. For the Taiwanese author, C. S. Song's trilogy and the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan's 1971/1975/1977 statements are a local instance in which a church *gained* legitimacy by siding with a suffering people against the state.

## Q8. Synthesis: theology type → framing of collective suffering → predicted effects (mapping table)

### Takeaway
How a theology frames collective suffering predicts two things that can move separately: **faith** (trust in God / religious commitment) and **institutional trust** (in the church that mediates or legitimates the suffering). Type A frames concentrate authority in mediating institutions and make them accountable for outcomes. Type B frames spread authority to the sufferers and to God-with-them. This tends to sustain faith while taking legitimacy away from institutions allied with the oppressor.

### Inferences
- **Core analytic claim, supported by the cases above.** Type A frames tie the *institution's* credibility to the *outcome* of the catastrophe: if repentance "works," the institution gains, and if not, it is indicted. Type B frames tie God's credibility to God's *presence with the sufferer*, which outcome cannot falsify. So Type B can sustain faith through defeat while shifting legitimacy away from institutions seen as allied with the cause of suffering.
- **Black Death vs. enslaved African Americans.** In 1347–51 the mediating church was *the* church. Its penitential monopoly was both strengthened and strained, as Clement VI's suppression of the flagellants shows. Under slavery, the oppressor's church and the sufferers' church were *different institutions*. Type B theology (Exodus, the spirituals, the crucified Christ) therefore allowed faith ↑ together with trust ↓ in the white slaveholding church and trust ↑ in the "invisible institution" and in independent Black churches.
- **Testable hypothesis for the article:** the effect of collective suffering on institutional trust depends on (a) whether the dominant theology is retributive-mediated or solidarity-based, and (b) whether the institution is seen as *with* the sufferers or *with* the agents of their suffering.

### Gaps
- The arrows (↑/↓) are theoretical predictions drawn from the literature, not measured outcomes. Empirical support would need the historical and sociological sources covered by other sections of the review (e.g., Black Death clerical mortality and the anticlericalism literature, and the growth of independent Black churches after 1865).
