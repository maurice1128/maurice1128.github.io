# Deep-research workflow result

**Question:** How do contemporary churches lose or gain trust when their members and societies suffer, and what do historical cases (Black Death Europe, enslaved African Americans, Taiwan's Presbyterian Church under martial law, Rwanda 1994) teach today's churches? Focus on verified evidence that belief persists while institutional trust falls ("believing without belonging", the nones and the dones, abuse crises in Ireland/US/Germany/France, COVID-19 church responses, churches' stances on contested social and political issues, racial justice after 2020), and evidence that churches grow when they act as the sufferers' own community (Global South Pentecostalism, Chinese house churches, Black churches). Include Taiwan data (Taiwan Social Change Survey religion modules, churches in the 2018 referendums, PCT social-justice tradition). Stay analytically neutral on the contested issues themselves. Prioritize peer-reviewed studies and major survey organizations (Pew, Gallup, PRRI, Academia Sinica), with Chicago author-date citations.

## Summary

The verified evidence shows a long decline in trust in church institutions. The steepest drops follow scandals. In the US, confidence in organized religion fell from first among institutions in 1973 to 44% in 2012, and Gallup ties the falls to the 2002 Boston abuse cover-up and the 1980s televangelist scandals (Saad 2012). In Germany, peer-reviewed event studies find that Catholic abuse and financial scandals raised formal church exits, including exits from the Protestant Church, which was not involved (Frick and Simmons 2017; Frick, Moser, and Simmons 2021). In England and Wales, about a third of Catholics who used to attend Mass or give money pulled back because of the abuse crisis while still calling themselves Catholic (Pound and Ryan 2024). Belief often outlasts belonging: only a third of US nones reject God, and in many of 22 countries sizable shares of nones hold spiritual beliefs (PRRI 2016; Pew Research Center 2025). In Britain, however, belief has fallen as fast as affiliation and attendance, mainly through generational replacement, so "believing without belonging" may be a transitional stage rather than a stable state (Voas and Crockett 2005). Evidence that churches grow by acting as sufferers' own community is thinner. Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity is at least a quarter of world Christianity and among its fastest-growing parts (Pew Research Center 2006). Black Americans value churches mainly for spiritual comfort, fellowship and material help rather than political preaching, but nearly half say Black churches have lost influence (Pew Research Center 2021). No verified claims came back for Taiwan or for the historical cases (Black Death, enslaved African Americans, the PCT under martial law, Rwanda 1994).

## Verified findings


### 1. (high, vote 3-0 (all three merged claims))

Over five decades, US confidence in the church as an institution has fallen sharply. Church or organized religion ranked first of all institutions in 1973 and stayed first in most years through 1985. It fell below 60% in the late 1980s, recovered to 60% in 2001, dropped to 45% in 2002 and 46% in 2007, and reached a low of 44% in June 2012 (Gallup, n=1,004, ±4 points). Gallup links the dips to scandals. It names the Bakker and Swaggart televangelist scandals in the late 1980s and the Boston Catholic abuse and cover-up crisis in 2002, including Cardinal Law's resignation. Gallup presents these as likely explanations, not as tested causes (Saad 2012).

- Evidence: Merges claims 0, 1 and 2, each verified 3-0 against Gallup's primary 2012 write-up. Gallup uses hedged wording ('possibly', 'most likely', 'likely'). Verifiers note that later Gallup readings (about 31-32% in 2022-23) show the decline continued after 2012.
- Sources: https://news.gallup.com/poll/155690/confidence-organized-religion-low-point.aspx

### 2. (high, vote 3-0 (all four merged claims))

Leaving religion often does not mean losing belief. In PRRI's 2016 survey, 25% of Americans had no religious identity. Most of them had left a childhood religion (19% of all Americans) rather than growing up without one (9%). Only 33% of the unaffiliated said they do not believe in God, and 18% were 'Unattached Believers' for whom religion is personally important (PRRI 2016). Pew's 2023-24 surveys point the same way across countries. In many of the 22 countries analyzed, sizable shares of nones believe in something spiritual beyond the natural world or in spirits in nature, and in Argentina 62% of nones believe in God, against 99% of the affiliated (Pew Research Center 2025).

- Evidence: Merges claims 3, 4, 5 and 8, all verified 3-0 by two independent major survey organizations. A verifier also cites Pew 2024: about 70% of US nones believe in God or another higher power, and only about 19% are strict nonbelievers. In every country Pew studied, nones still believe less than the affiliated.
- Sources: https://prri.org/research/prri-rns-poll-nones-atheist-leaving-religion/; https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/09/04/what-do-religious-nones-believe/

### 3. (medium, vote 3-0)

Belief may outlast belonging only for a while. Using British Household Panel Survey and British Social Attitudes data, Voas and Crockett (2005) find that religious belief in Britain has fallen at the same rate as affiliation and attendance. They argue the decline is mainly generational: parents pass on about half of their religiosity, while non-religion is passed on almost always. This contradicts Davie's 'believing without belonging' thesis. It implies that keeping belief alive without institutional belonging tends to fail across generations.

- Evidence: Claims 9 and 10, verified 3-0. The source is one peer-reviewed article (Sociology 39(1): 11-28). A verifier notes support from Molteni and Biolcati Rinaldi (Social Forces 2022/23), who attribute European attendance decline to cohort replacement. Counter-positions exist: Callum Brown's 1960s period shock, the difficulty of separating age, period and cohort effects, and Bible Society's disputed 2025 'Quiet Revival' report.
- Sources: https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/religion-in-britain-neither-believing-nor-belonging/

### 4. (medium, vote 2-1 (reasons) and 3-0 (Catholic net loss))

Institutional misconduct, not only loss of belief, drives disaffiliation. In PRRI's 2016 survey of Americans who left their childhood religion, 60% said they stopped believing its teachings. But 29% cited negative teachings about or treatment of gay and lesbian people, and 19% cited the clergy sexual-abuse scandal. Among former Catholics, 32% cited the abuse scandal. Catholics had the largest net loss of any major group: 31% were raised Catholic but about 21% now identify as Catholic, a 10-point drop (PRRI 2016).

- Evidence: Merges claim 6 (2-1 vote) and claim 7 (3-0). Respondents could name more than one reason. The base appears to be former adherents who are now unaffiliated. The 32% vs 19% comparison is with all who left their childhood religion, not all unaffiliated Americans. Fieldwork was in August 2016.
- Sources: https://prri.org/research/prri-rns-poll-nones-atheist-leaving-religion/

### 5. (high, vote 3-0)

Abuse and financial scandals cause measurable formal exits, and the damage spreads beyond the church at fault. An Arellano-Bond analysis of German church exits from 1953 to 2015 finds that much of the year-to-year variation comes from external shocks. These include unpopular papal encyclicals, reunification, the 2010 abuse revelations and the 2014 Limburg finance scandal, on top of an underlying downward trend (Frick and Simmons 2017). A diocese-level event study finds that Catholic abuse and financial scandals raised Catholic exits and, through spillover, Protestant (EKD) exits as well (Frick, Moser, and Simmons 2021).

- Evidence: Merges claims 11, 12, 16 and 17, all verified 3-0. Two peer-reviewed studies by overlapping authors (Applied Economics Letters 24(20); JSSR 60(3): 482-497). In Germany, exits are formal civil-register withdrawals tied to church tax. The 2017 paper uses a single dummy for abuse and finance scandals. Hungerman (2013) and Bottan and Perez-Truglia (2015) report similar responses in the US.
- Sources: https://relbib.de/Record/177158551X; https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13504851.2017.1284985

### 6. (medium, vote 3-0)

Many Catholics in England and Wales who stay Catholic have distanced themselves from the institution because of the abuse crisis. In a YouGov survey of 3,120 self-identified Catholics (fieldwork June-July 2022), about a third of former Mass-goers said they had reduced or stopped attending because of the crisis. About a third of former donors had stopped giving, and 79% said the Church must change 'a great deal' to prevent further abuse. The researchers call this 'intentional distancing from the Church' (Pound and Ryan 2024).

- Evidence: Merges claims 18, 19 and 20, verified 3-0. The source is a university press release (the primary URL returned 403 and was verified through a Durham mirror, the BRIN catalogue, the Catholic Herald and Premier). The causes are self-reported. COVID also cut attendance by about 40% in 2019-21. The survey used an online panel, not a probability sample.
- Sources: https://www.durham.ac.uk/departments/academic/arts-humanities/news/abuse-crisis-in-catholic-church-has-led-to-drop-in-mass-attendance

### 7. (high, vote 3-0)

US religious attendance has not recovered from COVID-19. Attendance in the past seven days averaged 34% in 2016-19 and 30% from 2020 to 2023 (31% in May 2023), about 10 points below 2012. Catholics fell more (37% to 30%) than Protestants (44% to 40%). Gallup cannot say whether the pandemic caused the drop or continued an existing trend. It does say closures and COVID avoidance got many Americans out of the habit of weekly attendance, and in 2023 it did not expect a return to pre-pandemic levels (Jones 2023).

- Evidence: Merges claims 13, 14 and 15, verified 3-0 against Gallup's primary June 2023 report. Since 2020 the measure counts virtual as well as in-person attendance. The descriptive figures are solid; the cause is explicitly unresolved.
- Sources: https://news.gallup.com/poll/507692/religious-service-attendance-decline.aspx

### 8. (medium, vote 3-0)

Black Americans value churches mainly as a community of care, not as political actors, and they give churches less credit for advancing equality. Asked what is essential in a house of worship, 72% named spiritual comfort, 71% community or fellowship, 55% helping the needy with bills, housing or food, and 25% sermons on political topics. Only 29% said Black churches have done 'a great deal' for equality (77% say at least 'some'), against 60% for civil rights organizations. Asked which institution has done the most, 10% named churches and 74% civil rights groups. A plurality (47%) say Black churches are less influential than 50 years ago (30% say more), and younger Black adults are more likely to say churches have too much influence (Pew Research Center 2021).

- Evidence: Merges claims 21, 22 and 23, verified 3-0. All come from one major-survey report fielded from late 2019 to mid-2020, mostly before the 2020 racial-justice protests. The 'most' question forced a single choice, which widens the gap. No generational percentages were published for the 'too much influence' item.
- Sources: https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/02/16/churches-and-religion-in-black-american-life/

### 9. (medium, vote 3-0)

Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity is among the fastest-growing parts of global Christianity. The World Christian Database estimated that at least a quarter of the world's roughly 2 billion Christians belonged to these movements (Pew Research Center 2006). A later Pew report using Gordon-Conwell data put the figure at about 584 million, or 27%, in 2010.

- Evidence: Claim 24, verified 3-0. The figure is a hedged estimate built on denomination-reported counts with broad, overlapping categories. The verified evidence documents size and growth only. It does not test why the movement grows, such as whether it serves as the sufferers' own community.
- Sources: https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2006/10/05/spirit-and-power/

## caveats

```
"Large parts of the question have no verified evidence. No claims survived on Taiwan (the TSCS religion modules, churches in the 2018 referendums, the PCT's social-justice tradition under martial law). None survived on the historical cases (Black Death Europe, enslaved African Americans, Rwanda 1994), Chinese house churches, Ireland's or France's (CIASE) abuse reports, the 'dones', church responses to racial justice after 2020, or how churches' stances on contested issues affect trust beyond PRRI's 2016 LGBT item. Any report on those topics needs more sourcing and should not be inferred from this set. The thesis that 'churches grow when they act as the sufferers' own community' is only indirectly supported: the Pentecostal and Black-church data describe size and the functions members value, not causes. Several findings are dated: PRRI 2016, Pew Black-church fieldwork 2019-20, Gallup 2012 and 2023, Pew 2006, Durham fieldwork 2022. Gallup's later confidence figures (about 31-32% in 2022-23) show the decline continued. One claim (PRRI reasons for leaving) passed 2-1. The Durham findings rest on a press release and self-reported causes, with COVID as a confounder. German exit data reflect the church-tax system, and the 2021 data window comes before the EKD's own 2024 ForuM abuse study, so later Protestant exits are not pure spillover. Gallup's links between scandals and confidence are its interpretation, not causal estimates. The 'believing without belonging' evidence conflicts across contexts: cross-sectional data on US and global nones show persistent belief, while British cohort data show belief falling with belonging. The two are reconciled by reading persistence as possibly transitional, not by treating either as definitive."
```
