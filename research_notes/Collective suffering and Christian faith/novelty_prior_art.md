# Novelty check: prior art for a comparative study of collective suffering and Christian faith (Black Death vs. enslaved African Americans)

Verification legend used throughout:
- **[page opened]**: I opened the publisher, journal or article page and confirmed the data.
- **[catalog/search-verified]**: confirmed from a publisher, catalog, RePEc or journal listing returned in search results, but I did not open the full page.
- **[unverified]**: given from background knowledge or seen only second-hand. Check it before citing. None of these are invented, but details such as page ranges or editions may be wrong.

Closeness scale: **direct overlap** (same comparison or same explanatory model), **partial overlap** (same question or one or two of the same variables, but different cases or method), **background only** (useful context, no real overlap).

Scope note: 25 tool calls were used. Google Scholar, JSTOR, ProQuest, PhilPapers, ATLA, SSRN and OSF cannot be queried directly from this environment. I reached them only through open web search, which indexes their public listing pages. Fetching eprints.soas.ac.uk failed because the host is not on this session's network allowlist. The null results below are strong evidence of novelty, but they are not an exhaustive database search.

## Q1. Does any work directly compare the Black Death (or medieval plague) with slave religion / New World slavery on religious response?

### Takeaway
I found no article, chapter, book, dissertation or thesis, in English, French or German, that compares the Black Death with the religion of enslaved African Americans, or medieval plague with New World slavery, on religious response. The only pieces that put the two cases near each other are web pages and student summaries that discuss them separately. The closest bridge item is an undergraduate thesis on African American redemptive-suffering theodicy during the 1793 Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic, which I could not open.

### Cited Findings
- An extended-mode search for "Black Death" plus "slave religion" (faith, suffering, comparison) returned only separate treatments of each case: a World History Encyclopedia article on Black Death religious responses, an encyclopedia entry on slave religion, a Substack opinion piece and study notes. None of them compares the two. — [World History Encyclopedia, "Religious Responses to the Black Death"](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1541/religious-responses-to-the-black-death/); [South Carolina Encyclopedia, "Slave Religion"](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/slave-religion/)
- The French and German searches ("peste noire" esclavage foi; "Schwarzer Tod" Sklaverei Glaube) found nothing comparing the two cases theologically. The only plague–slavery link that came up is economic: Mediterranean slavery after the 14th-century plague, discussed by Jean-Claude Hocquet. — [Académie des sciences d'outre-mer PDF](https://www.academieoutremer.fr/images/files/20220310_b21f827a0a294b33da09c1f0185d3727.pdf)
- The dissertation and thesis search ("comparing medieval plague and American slavery religious response theodicy") found works on one side only. On slavery: an SBTS dissertation on theodicy and slave experience; Emma Norman's Pitzer thesis on Black theodicy in the antebellum U.S.; Duane Loynes's Marquette dissertation (2017) on a "critical race theodicy." On plague: several Black Death and Church theses. None covers both. — [Marquette ePublications, Loynes dissertation](https://epublications.marquette.edu/dissertations_mu/738); [core.ac.uk thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/148695188.pdf)
- Bridge item: a senior thesis by Kyle Boone on African American theodicy of redemptive suffering during the 1793 Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic, listed at Olivet Nazarene University Digital Commons. The listed URL returned HTTP 404 when fetched, so author, title and year are **[unverified]**. — [Olivet Digital Commons listing (404 on fetch)](https://digitalcommons.olivet.edu/hist_ugrd/2)
- The Humanist's "A Tale of Two Plagues: How Faith Changes" (Julia Shapiro, 29 June 2020) compares the Black Death with COVID-19, not with slavery. **[page opened]** — [TheHumanist.com](https://thehumanist.com/news/international/a-tale-of-two-plagues-how-faith-changes/)

### Inferences
- The core pairing, Black Death Europe and enslaved African Americans as contrasting outcomes of collective suffering, appears to be unclaimed in scholarship I could reach.
- The Boone thesis is the only item that joins an epidemic to Black Christian theodicy. It is still a single-case study with no comparison to medieval Europe. Treat it as background or partial overlap.

### Gaps
- ProQuest Dissertations, ATLA RDB, JSTOR full text and Google Scholar could not be queried natively. A final manual check is advised: Google Scholar for "Black Death" AND "slave religion"; ATLA for plague AND slavery AND theodicy; ProQuest for (plague OR "Black Death") AND (slavery OR enslaved) AND (theodicy OR faith).
- I did not search Spanish, Portuguese or Italian literature on peste negra / esclavitud religión beyond one Spanish thesis hit.

## Q2. Comparative works on religious responses across several collective catastrophes or oppressions

### Takeaway
Several edited volumes and monographs compare religious responses across disasters, but each stays inside one tradition and region (Reformation Europe, the United States) or one hazard type (pandemics, natural disasters). None brings an epidemic and a system of enslavement into one explanatory frame, and none asks why the same tradition (Christianity) strengthens in one case and loses institutional trust in another.

### Cited Findings (each with Chicago author-date data and a closeness rating)

1. **Janku, Andrea, Gerrit Jasper Schenk, and Franz Mauelshagen, eds. 2012. *Historical Disasters in Context: Science, Religion, and Politics*. New York: Routledge.** **[catalog/search-verified; SOAS repository fetch blocked]**. It is an international collection of comparative case studies on how societies coped with disaster, giving particular weight to religion, science and politics, and asking why religion stayed central to disaster response. — [SOAS Research Online](https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/13414); [SOAS worktribe](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/402574)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It shares the comparative "religion in disaster" framing and Schenk's disaster-history approach. Its cases are natural disasters across cultures, it has no slavery case, and it offers no faith-vs-institution outcome theory.

2. **Cunningham, Andrew, and Ole Peter Grell. 2000. *The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse: Religion, War, Famine and Death in Reformation Europe*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.** **[catalog/search-verified]** xiii + 360 pp. It argues that people in 1490–1648 read war, famine and pestilence apocalyptically. Reviewers noted it struggles to explain why Catholics facing the same crises were less apocalyptic, and that its examples are mostly Protestant. — [Cambridge Core review, Medical History](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/EA6A4A17D90C735E8C68E113A0230484/S0025727300056581a.pdf/andrew-cunningham-and-ole-peter-grell-the-four-horsemen-of-the-apocalypse-religion-war-famine-and-death-in-reformation-europe-cambridge-university-press-2000-pp-xiii-360-illus-pound4250-usdollar6495-h.pdf); [JEH review](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-ecclesiastical-history/article/four-horsemen-of-the-apocalypse-religion-war-famine-and-death-in-reformation-europe-by-andrew-cunningham-and-ole-peter-grell-pp-xiii360-incl-71-plates-1-fig-and-3-tables-cambridge-cambridge-university-press-2000-4250-cloth-1595-paper-0-521-46135-9-0-521-46701-2/97FB819005E49D9ED301B1980A48E1C0)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It compares several catastrophe types within Christianity. Its outcome is apocalyptic interpretation, not faith versus institutional trust. Reviewers flagged an unexplained confessional variation, which the planned article's variables could address.

"New Directions in the Study of Religious Responses to the Black Death." *History Compass* 7 (5).** **[page opened at medievalists.net; issue number from search snippet; pages/DOI not confirmed]** It argues that older scholarship overstated differences among Christian, Muslim and Jewish plague responses, stresses diversity within each community, and calls for more nuanced comparison. — [Medievalists.net](https://www.medievalists.net/?p=14733)
   - **Closeness: background only, but important.** It warns against monolithic "Christian response" coding of the Black Death. A reviewer may cite it against coding the Black Death simply as "eroded trust."

6. **Steinberg, Ted. 2000. *Acts of God: The Unnatural History of Natural Disaster in America*. 8. **Alexander, Jeffrey C., Ron Eyerman, Bernhard Giesen, Neil J. Smelser, and Piotr Sztompka. 2004. *Cultural Trauma and Collective Identity*. Berkeley: University of California Press.** **[catalog/search-verified]** Cases include the Holocaust, U.S. slavery (Eyerman, "Cultural Trauma: Slavery and the Formation of African American Identity," pp. 60–112 per one bibliography) and 9/11. — [Lafayette TOC](https://libcat.lafayette.edu/Record/in00000338261/TOC?lng=bn); [Hodges Figgis](https://www.hodgesfiggis.ie/book/cultural-trauma-and-collective-identity/jeffrey-c-alexander/9780520235953)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It compares collective traumas including slavery and offers a general theory of how suffering becomes collective identity. Religion is not its outcome variable and it has no medieval case. It is a likely reviewer reference for "general theory of collective suffering."
   - Related **[unverified]**: Eyerman, Ron. 2001. *Cultural Trauma: Slavery and the Formation of African American Identity*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

   - **Closeness: partial overlap, and important.** It is a published mechanism in which epidemic suffering strengthens Christianity because the church acts as the sufferers' own caring community, which is the planned "church's role" variable. Stark uses one case and no comparison, but reviewers will cite him.

10. **Little, Lester K., ed. 2007. *Plague and the End of Antiquity: The Pandemic of 541–750*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.** **[unverified; mentioned in search summary only]** Also: **Nutton, Vivian, ed. 2008. *Pestilential Complexities: Understanding Medieval Plague*. 11. **Snowden, Frank M. 2019. *Epidemics and Society: From the Black Death to the Present*. New Haven: Yale University Press.** **[unverified; search returned nothing]**
    - **Closeness: background only.** Comparative epidemics; religion is a minor theme.

### Inferences
- Comparative catastrophe-and-religion literature exists, but it is organized by hazard type (disasters, pandemics) or by period and region. A design that crosses hazard type (biological vs. human-made structural oppression) and holds the tradition constant (Christianity) appears to be unoccupied.
- Cunningham & Grell's reviewers left an open puzzle: why did confessional groups facing the same crises respond differently? That puzzle has the same shape as the planned article's question and can be cited as motivation.

### Gaps
- I could not open tables of contents for Janku et al. (2012), Little (2007) or Nutton (2008) to rule out a chapter on slavery or African diaspora religion. That seems unlikely, but check before submission.
- I did not locate a book or volume literally titled "Religion and Disaster" or "Religious Responses to Disasters" beyond Borkataky-Varma et al. Christopher Ives, Lester Little's "Religion and catastrophe" pieces, Philip Jenkins's *The Great and Holy War* (2014, WWI and religious revival) and Charles Rosenberg's epidemic "dramaturgy" essay were not checked. All are background only at most.
- Special issues of MDPI *Religions* on COVID-19 and religion (2020–2023) were not enumerated individually.

## Q3. Works proposing a general theory of when catastrophe strengthens vs. weakens religion or religious institutions

### Takeaway
The closest prior art for the explanatory model sits in economics of religion and classical sociology, not in history or theology. Belloc, Drago & Galbiati (2016) show that a disaster's effect on religious authority depends on whether political and religious power are fused, which is an institutional-position moderator. Chaney (2013) shows that shocks raise religious leaders' political power. Ager & Ciccone (2018) show that church communities grow where they insure members against risk, which parallels the "church as the sufferers' own community" variable. Bentzen (2019) shows that disasters raise religiosity. None uses theodicy type as a variable, and none uses slavery or other human-made oppression as a case.

### Cited Findings
1. **Belloc, Marianna, Francesco Drago, and Roberto Galbiati. 2016. "Earthquakes, Religion, and Transition to Self-Government in Italian Cities." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 131 (4): 1875–1926. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjw020.** **[catalog/search-verified via RePEc]**
   - Earthquakes in 1000–1300 slowed the move to communal self-government only in episcopal-see cities, where political and religious authority were fused. They also raised church building there. — [EconPapers/RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:131:y:2016:i:4:p:1875-1926.); [CESifo WP 5566](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp5566.pdf)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap, and the strongest overlap on mechanism.** Disaster strengthens religious authority conditional on the institution's position in the power structure. The planned article differs in three ways: its outcome is lay faith vs. institutional trust, not political regime; it adds theodicy type; and it uses a human-made oppression case.
2. **Chaney, Eric. 2013. "Revolt on the Nile: Economic Shocks, Religion, and Political Power." *Econometrica* 81 (5): 2033–2053.** **[catalog/search-verified]** During abnormal Nile floods, Egypt's top religious authority was less likely to be replaced, which Chaney reads as shocks increasing religious leaders' power. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2013/09/01/revolt-nile-economic-shocks-religion-and-political-power)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** Shocks strengthen religious institutions vis-à-vis rulers, in an Islamic case. It has no theodicy or faith-vs-institution distinction.
3. **Ager, Philipp, and Antonio Ciccone. 2018. "Agricultural Risk and the Spread of Religious Communities." *Journal of the European Economic Association* 16 (4): 1021–1068. https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvx029.** **[catalog/search-verified]** In 19th-century U.S. counties, church membership was higher where rainfall risk was higher, which they read as religious communities providing mutual insurance. — [SUFE listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/268219); [UPF paper page](https://econ-papers.upf.edu/en/onepaper.php?id=1432)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap on the "church's role" variable.** Churches thrive when they function as the sufferers' own mutual-aid community. It covers the U.S. only and not slavery specifically. A related item, "Church Membership and Social Insurance: Evidence from the American South," appears in the same RePEc series and was not opened. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/kud/kuiedp/1429.html)
4. **Bentzen, Jeanet Sinding. 2019. "Acts of God? Religiosity and Natural Disasters Across Subnational World Districts." *Economic Journal* 129 (622): 2295–2321.** **[catalog/search-verified]** People become more religious after earthquakes. A later study summarizes Bentzen as finding that surprising disasters raise religiosity and unsurprising ones do not. — [RePEc WP](https://ideas.repec.org/p/kud/kuiedp/1302.html); [author PDF](https://web.econ.ku.dk/bentzen/religiousness%20and%20natural%20disasters_wp.pdf)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It is a general theory of the religious-coping direction, applied to natural hazards. It has no institutional-trust outcome.
5. **Frijters, Paul, et al. 2024. "Importance of Religion after Adversity." *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*. https://doi.org/10.1111/jssr.12879.** **[catalog/search-verified; co-authors and volume not confirmed]** It finds no evidence that major U.S. natural disasters increase the importance of religion. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jssr.12879)
   - **Closeness: background only.** A useful counter-finding showing the direction is not universal, which supports the need for conditional variables.
6. A news report on a 2018 UBC study found that disasters with many injuries strengthen religiosity among people who were already religious, while economic damage alone has weak effects. **[news report; underlying paper not identified]** — [ScienceDaily 2018](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/06/180605172518.htm)
7. **Weber, Max. Theodicy by social stratum.** Weber's sociology of religion (1922; "The Social Psychology of World Religions" in Gerth and Mills, eds., 1946, *From Max Weber*) treats theodicy comparatively: privileged strata need a "theodicy of good fortune" and disprivileged strata need a theodicy of suffering and salvation religion. Secondary sources confirm Weber's comparative treatment of theodicy; Norbert Wiley's critique notes that the compensating theodicy differs by class. **[primary text not opened; unverified page data]** — [U. Oregon lecture notes](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~sporter/lecture8.html); [Wiley on Academia](https://coll-serv.academia.edu/NorbertWiley); [OAPEN, Max Weber's Sociology of Religion](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/52168)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap at the level of theory, and the biggest conceptual risk.** Weber already links power position to theodicy type. The planned article adds a third variable (the institution's role), an explicit outcome distinction (faith vs. institutional trust), and a controlled comparison.
8. **[unverified, from background knowledge; not searched]** Further general theories a reviewer may raise:
   - Norris, Pippa, and Ronald Inglehart. 2004. *Sacred and Secular: Religion and Politics Worldwide*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. Existential insecurity sustains religiosity.
   - Glock, Charles Y. 1964. "The Role of Deprivation in the Origin and Evolution of Religious Groups." In *Religion and Social Conflict*, edited by Robert Lee and Martin E. Marty. New York: Oxford University Press. Deprivation theory.
   - Berger, Peter L. 1967. *The Sacred Canopy*. Garden City, NY: Doubleday. Theodicy and "masochism."
   - Pargament, Kenneth I. 1997. *The Psychology of Religion and Coping*. New York: Guilford.
   - Bentzen, Jeanet Sinding. 2021. "In Crisis, We Pray: Religiosity and the COVID-19 Pandemic." *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 192: 541–583.
   - Jedwab, Remi, Noel D. Johnson, and Mark Koyama. 2019. "Negative Shocks and Mass Persecutions: Evidence from the Black Death." *Journal of Economic Growth* 24 (4): 345–395. Black Death and Jewish persecution, which bears on the plague case's religious aftermath.

### Inferences
- The empirical economics-of-religion literature already establishes that the effect of disaster on religion is conditional. The planned article should present itself as offering a historical and theological mechanism for that conditionality, and should cite Belloc et al. and Ager & Ciccone, rather than claim the idea of conditionality as new.
- No work I found combines (power position) × (theodicy type) × (institutional role) to predict a split outcome (personal faith up, institutional trust down or up). That combination is the novel contribution.

### Gaps
- I did not verify Norris & Inglehart, Glock, Berger, Pargament, Bentzen 2021 or Jedwab et al. directly.
- Sociology of disaster ("therapeutic community" theory, e.g., Fritz 1961) and the collective-victimhood psychology literature were not searched.

## Q4. Theodicy-comparative works across traditions and catastrophes (Holocaust–slavery, Neiman, Jones, Pinn)

### Takeaway
The comparative-theodicy literature pairs slavery with the Holocaust (Thomas 1993; Alexander et al. 2004) or natural evil with moral evil (Neiman 2002, Lisbon vs. Auschwitz), and never with the Black Death. Black theodicy (Jones 1973; Pinn 1995) treats slave and Black suffering in its own right and makes race central, which the planned article deliberately sets aside.

### Cited Findings
1. **Thomas, Laurence Mordekhai. 1993. *Vessels of Evil: American Slavery and the Holocaust*. Philadelphia: Temple University Press.** xvii + 211 pp. (LCCN 93012386). **[catalog/search-verified]** A moral-philosophy comparison distinguishing extermination from domination and natal alienation. Reviewed by James Sterba in *Ethics* and critically in *Shofar*. — [Shofar review (Project MUSE)](https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/shofar/v014/14.3.goldman.pdf); [ECU catalog](https://lib.ecu.edu/catalog-preview/catalog/489623)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It is the leading comparison of slavery with another collective catastrophe. It is philosophical, not about religious response, and has no plague case.
2. **Neiman, Susan. 2002. *Evil in Modern Thought: An Alternative History of Philosophy*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press.** (Revised edition 2015.) **[catalog/search-verified]** It asks whether natural evils (Lisbon) and human evils (Auschwitz) are versions of the same problem. — [ÖAW catalog](https://oeaw.ac.at/resources/Record/9781400873661); [Wilson Quarterly](https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/_/evil-in-modern-thought-alternative-history-philosophy/)
   - **Closeness: background only, but a risk.** Its natural/moral evil distinction maps onto Black Death (natural) vs. slavery (human-made). A reviewer may say the cases differ in kind, so the article must address this directly.
3. **Jones, William R. 1973. *Is God a White Racist? A Preamble to Black Theology*. Garden City, NY: Anchor/Doubleday.** (Reissued Boston: Beacon Press, 1998.) **[catalog/search-verified for Beacon ed.]** It argues that maldistributed ethnic suffering raises the question of divine racism and critiques the theodicies of Washington, Cone and Cleage. — [Powell's](https://www.powells.com/book/is-god-a-white-racist-9780807010334); [Harvard Theological Review article by Jones](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/theodicy-and-methodology-in-black-theology-a-critique-of-washington-cone-and-cleage/D908F4952B72C8656B19C4794CC546D9)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It is a theodicy of collective oppression that makes race the hinge, the opposite of the planned design. It is the most likely source of the critique that excluding race is untenable.
4. **Pinn, Anthony B. 1995. *Why, Lord? Suffering and Evil in Black Theology*. New York: Continuum.** **[unverified; search returned nothing]** It rejects redemptive-suffering theodicy (confirmed second-hand). — [Wikipedia, Anthony B. Pinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_B._Pinn)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It argues that redemptive suffering is unhelpful, which bears on the "God-with-the-sufferer" variable.
5. **Jackson, Sherman A. 2009. *Islam and the Problem of Black Suffering*. New York: Oxford University Press.** **[OUP listing seen; year from memory]** It puts Jones's challenge in dialogue with classical Islamic theological schools. — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/10420)
   - **Closeness: background only.** Cross-tradition theodicy of Black suffering.
6. **Loynes, Duane T., Sr. 2017. "A God Worth Worshiping: Toward a Critical Race Theodicy." PhD diss., Marquette University.** **[listing seen; exact title wording to confirm]** — [Marquette ePublications](https://epublications.marquette.edu/dissertations_mu/738)
   - **Closeness: background only.**
7. A study titled "Suffering history: Comparative Christian theodicy in Korea" compares Catholic and Protestant theodicies of collective historical suffering (han) and invokes Nietzsche's "slave revolt." **[author, venue and year not confirmed]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304001601_Suffering_history_Comparative_Christian_theodicy_in_Korea)
   - **Closeness: partial overlap.** It compares Christian theodicies of collective suffering within one nation and offers a possible extra case (Korea under colonial rule) for the expanded article.
8. "The Problem of Suffering and the Sociological Task of Theodicy" (Academia.edu) distinguishes theodicy from "sociodicy." **[author not confirmed]** — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/338855/The_Problem_of_Suffering_and_the_Sociological_Task_of_Theodicy)
   - **Closeness: background only.**
9. **Sindler, Frank. 2011. "Comparative Study of Christian, Jewish, and Islamic Theodicy." Thesis, Reformed Theological Seminary.** **[PDF listing seen; degree type unconfirmed]** It finds no uniquely Christian response to suffering. — [RTS PDF](https://cdn.rts.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/201105-Sindler-Frank.pdf)
   - **Closeness: background only.**
10. **[unverified, not searched]** Surin, Kenneth. 1986. *Theology and the Problem of Evil*. Oxford: Blackwell. Moltmann, Jürgen. 1974 [German 1972]. *The Crucified God*. London: SCM. Cone, James H. 1975. *God of the Oppressed*. New York: Seabury. Cone, James H. 2011. *The Cross and the Lynching Tree*. Maryknoll, NY: Orbis. ### Inferences
- The slavery-and-Holocaust pairing is crowded. The plague-and-slavery pairing is empty.
- Black theodicy treats race as constitutive. Bracketing race is defensible only if the article argues explicitly that the mechanism (power position plus theodicy plus institution) generalizes to non-racialized cases, such as Irish famine victims or serfs under plague. Expanding the case set would make that argument.

### Gaps
- Pinn 1995, Surin, Moltmann and Cone were not verified in this session.
- I did not search for Jewish Holocaust theodicy works (Rubenstein, Fackenheim, Braiterman) as comparators. They are relevant if the article expands to genocide.

## Q6. Does any work use the three variables (power position, theodicy type, institutional role) to explain variation?

### Takeaway
No. Each variable has strong prior art on its own: power position (Weber; Steinberg; Belloc et al.), theodicy type (Weber; Jones; Pinn; Rittgers; Moltmann), and institutional role as a community of care or mutual insurance (Stark; Ager & Ciccone; Raboteau's "invisible institution" for slave religion). I found no work that combines all three into a single explanatory model of when collective suffering intensifies Christian faith versus eroding trust in religious institutions, or that tests one across the Black Death and slavery.

### Cited Findings
- Power-position moderator of disaster effects on religious authority: [Belloc, Drago & Galbiati 2016 (RePEc)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:131:y:2016:i:4:p:1875-1926.)
- Theodicy differentiated by social stratum: [Weber via U. Oregon lecture notes](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~sporter/lecture8.html); [Wiley critique](https://coll-serv.academia.edu/NorbertWiley)
- Disaster discourse masking power and inequality: [Steinberg 2000 (Open Library)](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3477326W)
- Church as mutual insurance under risk: [Ager & Ciccone 2018](https://econ-papers.upf.edu/en/onepaper.php?id=1432)
- Church as community of care in epidemics: [Stark, via CMF summary](https://www.cmf.org.uk/?p=6505)
- Religious networks must "prove themselves" in disasters (Fiji 1997: Christians helped by their church, Hindus and Muslims burdened by temple and mosque demands), cited in Bentzen's working paper: [Bentzen WP](https://web.econ.ku.dk/bentzen/religiousness%20and%20natural%20disasters_wp.pdf)

## Q7. Verdict, novelty claims, and risks

### Takeaway
**Verdict: the core comparison is not taken.** Across all the phrasings, languages and listing sources I could reach, no work compares Black Death Europe with enslaved African Americans, or medieval plague with New World slavery, on religious response. No work proposes the three-variable model (power position × theodicy type × institutional role) to explain why collective suffering intensifies Christian faith in some cases and erodes institutional trust in others. Each component idea, though, has well-known prior art, which the article must cite and position itself against.

### Cited Findings (basis for the novelty claims)
1. **First systematic comparison of the Black Death and slave Christianity as contrasting outcomes of collective suffering.**
   - Goes beyond: Thomas 1993 and Alexander et al. 2004, which compare slavery with the Holocaust or 9/11, not with plague, and do not treat religious response as the outcome.
   - Sources: [Shofar review](https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/shofar/v014/14.3.goldman.pdf); [Lafayette TOC](https://libcat.lafayette.edu/Record/in00000338261/TOC?lng=bn)
2. **Splitting the outcome into personal faith vs. institutional trust, and explaining when they diverge.**
   - Goes beyond: Bentzen 2019 and Frijters et al. 2024, which measure aggregate religiosity, and Belloc et al. 2016 and Chaney 2013, which measure religious leaders' political power. None separates lay faith from confidence in the institution.
   - Sources: [Bentzen](https://ideas.repec.org/p/kud/kuiedp/1302.html); [Frijters et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jssr.12879); [Belloc et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:131:y:2016:i:4:p:1875-1926.)
3. **Adding theodicy type (punishment vs. God-with-the-sufferer) as a causal variable alongside structural position.**
   - Goes beyond: the economics literature, which has no theological variable, and Weber, who links stratum to theodicy but does not model the institution's role or test it on a paired historical comparison.
   - Sources: [Weber notes](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~sporter/lecture8.html)
4. **Institutional role as "failing establishment" vs. "sufferers' own community," applied across a biological catastrophe and a human-made structural oppression.**
   - Goes beyond: Stark 1996 (one epidemic case, church as caregiver) and Ager & Ciccone 2018 (U.S. rainfall risk, church as insurance). Neither contrasts an establishment church that fails with a sufferer-built church.
   - Sources: [Stark summary](https://www.cmf.org.uk/?p=6505); [Ager & Ciccone](https://econ-papers.upf.edu/en/onepaper.php?id=1432)
5. **Explaining puzzles left open in existing work.**
   - Goes beyond: Cunningham & Grell 2000, whose reviewers say the book does not explain why Catholics and Protestants facing the same crises responded differently, and Stearns 2009, who calls for nuanced comparison. The three-variable model offers an explanation, and the expanded case set tests it.
   - Sources: [JEH review](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-ecclesiastical-history/article/four-horsemen-of-the-apocalypse-religion-war-famine-and-death-in-reformation-europe-by-andrew-cunningham-and-ole-peter-grell-pp-xiii360-incl-71-plates-1-fig-and-3-tables-cambridge-cambridge-university-press-2000-4250-cloth-1595-paper-0-521-46135-9-0-521-46701-2/97FB819005E49D9ED301B1980A48E1C0); [Stearns](https://www.medievalists.net/?p=14733)

### Inferences: risks (likely reviewer objections)
- **"Weber (and Nietzsche, Marx, Glock) already did this."** Class-differentiated theodicy and deprivation-driven religiosity are classical theory. Mitigation: present the article as operationalizing and extending Weber with a third, institutional variable and a split outcome, and cite him prominently.
- **"Belloc, Drago & Galbiati / Ager & Ciccone already showed the conditionality."** Economists of religion will see the power-position and community-insurance mechanisms as known. Mitigation: cite them, and stress the theodicy variable and the faith/institution split.
- **"Incommensurable cases."** Natural vs. moral evil (Neiman 2002), roughly four years vs. roughly 250 years, and a whole-society catastrophe vs. the domination of one group by another. In the slavery case the church's role is also partly collinear with power position, because the establishment church was the masters' church. Mitigation: justify the paired comparison explicitly (most-different design), and add cases that break the collinearity, such as a plague-era confraternity or an oppressed group whose own church failed.
- **"Bracketing race is untenable for slave religion."** Black theology (Jones 1973; Cone; Pinn 1995) makes race constitutive of the theodicy question. Mitigation: argue that race is set aside as an explanatory variable but acknowledged as context, and show the mechanism working in non-racialized cases such as Irish famine, serfdom or colonial cases.
- **"The Black Death dependent variable is mis-coded."** Stearns 2009 warns against monolithic readings. Popular essays (First Things 2020) and Rittgers 2012 note that lay piety intensified after the plague through flagellants, confraternities and devotional literature, so faith also intensified, not just distrust. Mitigation: code the Black Death as "faith intensified + institutional trust eroded." That is a stronger case for the faith/institution split.
- **"Superficial overlap with COVID-era Black Death–faith essays"** (Shapiro 2020; Chan 2021). Mitigation: avoid "lessons for today" framing in the abstract.
- **Residual search risk:** ProQuest, ATLA and full-text JSTOR were not natively searchable. A low but non-zero chance remains that a master's thesis or conference paper makes the comparison. Run the manual checks listed in Q1's Gaps before submission.
