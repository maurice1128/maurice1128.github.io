## Q1. Devotional intensification after 1348: flagellants, pilgrimage, plague saints, confraternities, chantries and masses, wills, ars moriendi, transi tombs, danse macabre, mysticism

### Takeaway
The scholarship strongly supports more intense and more visible devotion after 1348. Its main forms were penitential processions, plague-saint cults, confraternal charity, intercessory masses and macabre art. The best empirical work (Cohn on wills, Wray on Bologna, Henderson on Florentine confraternities) says the change was delayed and uneven, and was routed through clergy and church institutions rather than away from them. The decisive change in testamentary piety came after the second plague of 1363, not 1348. It took the form of strategic, family-centred "remembrance," which complicates a simple "more faith" reading.

### Cited Findings

**Wills, pious bequests and the "cult of remembrance"**
- **Cohn, Samuel K., Jr. 1992. *The Cult of Remembrance and the Black Death: Six Renaissance Cities in Central Italy*. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press.** xiii + 429 pp. (bibliography pp. 381–413). Verified via library catalogues. An ACLS Humanities E-Book edition followed in 2002. The book compares testaments from Arezzo, Florence, Perugia, Assisi, Pisa and Siena. It argues that after the 1363 plague, testators of all classes in Tuscany and Umbria stopped dividing bequests into many small pious sums. Instead they concentrated them in larger gifts designed to perpetuate memory, such as chapels, masses and named artworks. Cohn reads this as a shift in ideas of the afterlife and in charity, not as Burckhardtian individualism. — [Glasgow eprints / catalogue summary](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/122848); [Holy Cross library record](https://library.holycross.edu/Record/b1305291/Details)
  - Relevance: This is the main evidence for "devotion intensified." Cohn frames the change as a turn to *memorialisation*, with bequests still paid to church institutions, not as a turn away from the church. The trigger was the *recurrence* of plague (1363), not 1348 alone.
- **Wray, Shona Kelly. 2009. *Communities and Crisis: Bologna during the Black Death*. The Medieval Mediterranean 83. Leiden: Brill.** About 300 pp.; ISBN 978-90-04-17634-8. Verified via De Gruyter Brill and the Wellesley catalogue. The book draws on more than a thousand notarial testaments from 1348 (*Libri Memoriali*, Demaniale and Provvisori registers). It challenges chroniclers' accounts of total social breakdown and stresses continuity and resilient neighbourhood, family and notarial networks during the plague. — [De Gruyter Brill](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/isbn/9789047429784/html); [Medievalists.net summary/TOC](https://www.medievalists.net/2010/02/communities-and-crisis-bologna-during-the-black-death/); [Wellesley catalogue](https://libcat.wellesley.edu/Record/ebs945860e/Details)
  - Relevance: This is a counterweight to the "collapse of social and religious bonds" image found in chroniclers such as Boccaccio. Wills record routine pious provision continuing during the crisis.

**Confraternities, charity and the poor**
- **Henderson, John. 1994. *Piety and Charity in Late Medieval Florence*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.** Page count varies by record (about 533–552 pp.). **Mysticism (Julian of Norwich)**
- Julian was about six when plague reached Norwich in 1348. Scholars observe that the *Revelations/Showings* contain no direct mention of the plague. Some read the disfigured, suffering body of Christ in her visions as refracting plague-damaged bodies. Her optimistic theodicy ("all shall be well") is often contrasted with the prevailing "plague as punishment" reading. Claims that she lost husband and children to plague (Benedicta Ward) are conjecture. — [Church Life Journal (Notre Dame), "The God of Mercy in a Time of Plague"](https://churchlifejournal.nd.edu/articles/the-god-of-mercy-in-a-time-of-the-plague/); [Wikipedia summary of scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_of_Norwich). These are semi-popular sources. I found no peer-reviewed article specifically on Julian and plague this session **[gap]**.

### Inferences
- The premise's first half, that devotion intensified, is well supported. But the most rigorous quantitative work (Cohn 1992) places the decisive shift after **1363**, so recurrent plague mattered more than the first strike. The change also *redirected* piety into durable, clergy-administered commemoration (chapels, perpetual masses, altarpieces). That is evidence of continued *dependence* on the institution, not alienation from it.
- The flagellants are the strongest evidence of lay devotion turning against clerical mediation (Kieckhefer). They were suppressed within about a year and are better read as a short spike than a lasting rupture.
- Macabre and transi imagery peaks c. 1400–1500 (Cohen, Binski, Oosterwijk). This argues against a simple "1348 trauma → morbid piety" causation, and points to cumulative plague recurrence interacting with older penitential culture.

- No verified English-language monograph on pilgrimage after 1348, or on St Roch's cult before 1500, was found this session.

## Q2. Declining trust in clergy and institution: clerical mortality and replacement, anticlericalism, Lollardy/Hussites, papal response (Clement VI, 1350 Jubilee, anti-flagellant bull), Ralph of Shrewsbury

### Takeaway
High clerical mortality is well documented. Recent quantitative work challenges the textbook claim that replacements were poorly trained and drawn from lower social strata, and diocesan studies stress administrative resilience. The causal links from plague to Lollardy and to the Hussite revolution are weak in current scholarship. The papacy was active, not absent: indulgences, waived dues, protection of Jews, the Jubilee. The "decline of trust" thesis rests mainly on older syntheses and popular history, not on recent archival studies.

### Inferences
- The second half of the premise, that trust in the institution declined, is the weaker half in current specialist scholarship. The best recent quantitative evidence (Robinson 2019) does not support "inferior replacement clergy." Diocesan studies (Dohar; Aberth on Ely) show administrations functioning through the crisis. The papacy responded energetically, with indulgences, tax relief and the Jubilee (Rollo-Koster; Wood).
- Where institutional trust plausibly *did* fray is in specific mechanisms rather than general "loss of faith in the church":
  1. Clerical flight and avoidance of the sick, which Ralph of Shrewsbury's letter both documents and addresses.
  2. Competition between chantry and stipendiary posts and parish cure, with pluralism and absenteeism (Platt).
  3. Flagellant lay penance that bypassed the sacraments, suppressed by the pope (Kieckhefer).
- Lollardy (1370s–) and Hussitism (1400s–) are now explained mainly by intellectual, political and national factors. Plague is at most a contributing background condition, and in Bohemia the relevant epidemic is 1380, not 1348 (Mengel). The literature review should treat plague → heresy claims as contested, with the burden of proof on the causal claim.

## Q3. Contemporary theological interpretation (divine punishment, astrology, well-poisoning) and the persecution of Jews, 1348–1351

### Takeaway
Contemporaries combined three explanations that did not exclude each other: God's punishment for sin, read through penitential and sometimes eschatological frames; celestial or astrological and miasmatic causes, as in the Paris medical faculty; and human agency, meaning well-poisoning by Jews and others. Recent scholarship (Cohn 2007; Barzilay 2022; Nirenberg 1996) treats the anti-Jewish massacres as shaped by elites, municipal and political actors and pre-existing accusation patterns. They are not read as spontaneous popular religious frenzy. Cohn's long-run comparison (2018) argues that epidemics more often produced compassion than hate.

## Q4. The Meiss thesis (1951) and its critiques

### Takeaway
Meiss argued that the calamities of the 1340s, plague above all, produced a sterner, more hieratic and otherworldly religious art in Florence and Siena, read as penitential response to divine punishment. From the 1970s critics (Boskovits, Cole, van Os, Norman, Steinhoff) showed the stylistic shift began before 1348 and was pluralistic. They argue it is better explained by patronage, politics and the art market. That weakens art as evidence that devotion "intensified" because of plague.

### Cited Findings
- **Meiss, Millard. 1951. *Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death: The Arts, Religion and Society in the Mid-Fourteenth Century*. ## Q5. Long-term claims: the Black Death and the Reformation, secularisation, "crisis of the late Middle Ages"

### Takeaway
Grand claims linking plague to Reformation or secularisation come mainly from synthesisers and popular historians: Huizinga in a looser cultural sense, Ziegler, Gottfried, Cantor, and to a degree Herlihy and Platt. Specialist work tends to rebut or heavily qualify them, including Duffy on robust late-medieval piety, Wray and Cohn on resilience and redirection, Bailey on institutional change in England, and Bowsky's framing of "turning point?" as a question. The consensus treats plague as a powerful accelerant of change already under way, operating mainly through *recurrence*, not a cause of religious rupture.

## Q6. "New" Black Death scholarship since c. 2010 (aDNA, global plague) and recent plague-and-religion work (2010–2026)

### Takeaway
Post-2010 scholarship transformed the biology and geography of the Black Death. Ancient-DNA (aDNA) work confirmed *Yersinia pestis*, Green's "four Black Deaths" reframed it as a global, multi-lineage pandemic with Central Asian origins, and Mengel remapped Bohemia. This left the *religious-history* picture largely intact. It mainly removes Cohn's 2002 "not-plague" argument and invites global/comparative framing. Aberth 2021 is the main up-to-date English synthesis with a religious-response chapter. Dedicated post-2010 English monographs on Black Death religion are surprisingly scarce.

### Inferences

### Cross-question synthesis: overall assessment of the working premise
(Synthesis of the inferences above; each claim is sourced in the relevant section.)
- **Supported:** Devotion intensified and took new forms: flagellants, plague saints, confraternal charity, intercessory masses and chantries, Jubilee pilgrimage, macabre art. See Kieckhefer 1974, Marshall 1994, Henderson 1994, Cohn 1992, Platt 1996, Binski 1996.
- **Qualified:** Much of this intensification ran *through* the institution, which grew busier and richer in intercessory services. The decisive testamentary shift came with *recurrence* (1363), not 1348 (Cohn 1992). Art-historical "intensification" (Meiss) is largely discredited as a direct plague effect (van Os, Steinhoff).
- **Weakly supported or contested:** "Declining trust in clergy." Robinson 2019 rebuts the inferior-replacement thesis. Duffy 1992 rebuts long-term English decay. Plague → Lollardy/Hussitism links are weak (Mengel 2011; Nodl via VuF). The strongest evidence is local and episodic: clerical flight (Ralph of Shrewsbury) and flagellant lay penance against clerical mediation (Kieckhefer).
- **A suggested reformulation for the article:** Collective suffering increased *demand* for religious meaning and intercession. Whether that demand strengthened or eroded trust in the institution depended on its *capacity to deliver*: priests present at deathbeds, sacraments available, indulgences granted, protection from violence. When the church supplied pastoral services (episcopal dispensations, papal indulgences, chantry masses), devotion and institution rose together. When it was visibly absent or suppressive (clerical flight, the condemnation of the flagellants), lay religion moved toward self-directed forms.
